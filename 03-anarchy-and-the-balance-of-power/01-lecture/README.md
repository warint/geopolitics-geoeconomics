## 1. Anarchy: the starting point

In international relations, **anarchy** means the absence of a government above states — no world
authority that can make rules binding, enforce contracts or guarantee survival. It does **not** mean
chaos or constant war: most of the time, most states are at peace and trade under rules.

What follows from anarchy, in the realist tradition:

- **Self-help.** Each state must ultimately ensure its own survival.
- **Uncertainty about intentions.** A state cannot know what another will do with its power
  tomorrow.
- **Relative gains.** Because today's partner may be tomorrow's adversary, states care not only what
  they gain from cooperation but how much *more* the other gains (Grieco, 1988).

**Why a firm should care.** Relative-gains thinking is why trade becomes a security question: a
deal that makes both richer but the rival richer *faster* may be refused, restricted or reversed. It
is the logic behind export controls on advanced semiconductors.

## 2. Two realisms

**Defensive realism — Waltz (1979), *Theory of International Politics*.** The structure of the
system — anarchy plus the **distribution of capabilities** — shapes behaviour. States seek an
*appropriate* amount of power: enough for security. Too much provokes balancing coalitions, so
expansion is usually self-defeating. Waltz judged **bipolar** systems more stable than multipolar
ones: fewer miscalculations.

**The security dilemma** (Jervis, 1978). What one state does to make itself secure — arming,
alliances — makes others less secure, and they respond. Both end up worse off without anyone
intending aggression.

**Offensive realism — Mearsheimer (2001).** Five bedrock assumptions:

1. The international system is **anarchic**.
2. Great powers possess some **offensive military capability**.
3. States can never be certain of others' **intentions**.
4. **Survival** is the primary goal of great powers.
5. Great powers are **rational actors**.

From these, Mearsheimer derives that great powers **maximise relative power**; the ideal is to be the
**regional hegemon** and to prevent any other region from having one. His prediction for China:
it will try to dominate Asia as the United States dominates the Western Hemisphere, and the
United States will try to stop it. *"China cannot rise peacefully."*

| | Defensive (Waltz) | Offensive (Mearsheimer) |
|---|---|---|
| How much power do states want? | enough for security | as much as possible, relative to others |
| What restrains them? | balancing by others | only the limits of their capabilities |
| Expected US–China outcome | managed competition | intense security competition |

## 3. Power transition and the Thucydides's Trap

**Power transition theory** (Organski, 1958; Organski & Kugler, 1980) reverses Waltz's emphasis:
hierarchy, not balance, keeps the peace. A **dominant power** sets the international order. War is
most likely when a **dissatisfied challenger** approaches **parity** with the dominant power — a
threshold often operationalised as the challenger reaching around 80% of the leader's capabilities.

**Allison (2017).** *"It was the rise of Athens and the fear that this instilled in Sparta that made
war inevitable"* (Thucydides). Allison and the Harvard Thucydides's Trap project identify **sixteen**
cases in five hundred years in which a rising power challenged a ruling one; **twelve** ended in war.

**The critics.**

- **Case selection.** The cases are chosen on the outcome of interest; peaceful transitions (the
  UK to the US) may be under-counted; the definition of "rising" and "ruling" is elastic.
- **Nuclear weapons** change the expected cost of great-power war in a way no pre-1945 case can
  capture.
- **Interdependence.** The US and China are bound by trade and finance far more tightly than
  Athens and Sparta, or Britain and Germany — though Britain and Germany were also major trading
  partners in 1914 (Session 04 takes this up).
- **Measuring "rise".** Beckley (2018) argues gross indicators — GDP, population, CINC — overstate
  China's power because they ignore the costs of governing, feeding and protecting a population.

## 4. Measuring power: the CINC

The Correlates of War **Composite Index of National Capability** (Singer, Bremer & Stuckey, 1972):

1. Take **six indicators** for every state in the system each year: total population, urban
   population, iron and steel production, primary energy consumption, military personnel,
   military expenditure.
2. For each indicator, compute the state's **share of the system total** that year.
3. **Average** the six shares. CINC is a number between 0 and 1; the system sums to about 1 (not exactly: a state missing a
   component is averaged over the ones it has).

**What it rewards.** Size and heavy industry. Two of six components are population and two are
smokestack industry. CINC therefore shows China passing the United States **much earlier** than
military spending or GDP at market exchange rates do. One reality, three measures, three dates:
the *break* step of the practice round is to see it.

**What it misses.** Technology, quality of forces, alliances, financial power, control of networks
— the dollar, SWIFT, the cloud — which Session 08 and Session 11 show to be the instruments of
coercion that reach firms most directly.

---

## 5. What it means for the multinational

| | |
|---|---|
| **The balance of power sets the rules of trade** | Who can impose sanctions, export controls and market-access conditions depends on who holds capabilities others need |
| **A transition changes which rules you follow** | A firm in both the US and China increasingly faces two compliance regimes — and penalties for following one in the other's jurisdiction |
| **Relative gains turn commerce into security** | Products that make a rival stronger — chips, AI, batteries, aerospace — move from the trade ministry to the security council |
| **The date of the transition depends on the measure** | Board slides that say "China has overtaken the US" should say on **what** |
| **Security dilemmas escalate without intent** | A defensive move by one side (an export control, a military exercise) is read as offensive and triggers a response that reaches your supply chain |

---

## 6. What the figure does not license

- CINC is a measure of **material capabilities**, not of influence, intentions or likelihood of
  war. A crossover on the chart is not a prediction of conflict.
- It **overweights size**: population and heavy industry. It cannot see semiconductors, alliances or
  finance.
- It ends in **2022**: the most recent shifts are not in it.
- It describes **states**, not markets: a firm's exposure depends on where its revenue and suppliers
  sit, not on the system's distribution of power.
