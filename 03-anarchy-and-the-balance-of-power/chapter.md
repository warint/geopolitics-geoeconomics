## A system with no government

The previous chapter described the international economic order as a set of rules, written by
particular states and held in place by something. This chapter asks what that something is when it
is not law. Inside a country, a contract is enforced by courts, and the courts are backed by a state
that holds a monopoly of legitimate force. Between countries there is no such state. There is no
world government that can make a rule binding, enforce a contract between two governments, or
guarantee that a state which disarms will not be invaded. That absence is the starting point of the
realist tradition in international relations, and it is the reason this course cannot treat
geopolitics as a branch of regulation.

The session's question follows directly. In a system with no government above states, who sets the
terms of trade — and how would a multinational know that the balance is shifting? The realist answer
to the first half is blunt: the terms are set, in the end, by those who hold the capabilities
others need, and they are revised when the distribution of capabilities changes. The second half is
a measurement problem, and it is the subject of the practice round, which rebuilds the long-run
distribution of material capabilities among the major powers and asks when, and on what measure,
China overtook the United States.

The chapter proceeds in five steps. It defines anarchy and what follows from it; sets out the two
main realist theories of how states respond, defensive and offensive; turns to power transition
theory and its popular form, Graham Allison's "Thucydides's Trap"; examines how power is measured,
and what the standard measure rewards and misses; and ends with what all this means for a firm that
operates in both the rising and the ruling power.

## Anarchy, and what it does not mean

::: {.definition}
[**Anarchy**]{.term} — in international relations, the absence of any authority above states
capable of making binding rules, enforcing agreements between them, or guaranteeing their
survival. It describes the *structure* of the system, not the level of violence within it.
:::

The word is a trap for newcomers, because in ordinary language it means chaos. In realist theory it
means nothing of the kind. Most of the time, most states are at peace with most others; they trade,
sign treaties, join organisations and settle disputes by negotiation. Anarchy is compatible with all
of that. What it denies is that any of it is *guaranteed* by a higher authority. A treaty is kept
because the parties find it in their interest to keep it, not because a court can compel them; a
trading relationship continues because both sides value it, not because a sovereign above them will
punish defection.

Three consequences follow, in the realist reading, and each matters for firms.

The first is **self-help**. Because no one else guarantees a state's survival, each state must
ultimately provide for its own security. It may ally with others, but alliances are arrangements of
convenience, and a state that relies entirely on an ally has placed its survival in someone else's
hands.

The second is **uncertainty about intentions**. A state can observe another's capabilities — its
army, its industry, its population — but it cannot observe its intentions with confidence, and
intentions can change faster than capabilities. A peaceful neighbour with a large army is a
neighbour whose government could change. Because the cost of misjudging a hostile state is so
high, states tend to plan for capabilities rather than for stated intentions.

The third is concern for **relative gains**. Because today's partner may be tomorrow's adversary,
states care not only about how much they gain from cooperation, but about how much more the other
side gains. A trade agreement that makes both countries richer but makes the rival richer faster
shifts the balance of capabilities, and in an anarchic system that shift can be a threat.

::: {.definition}
[**Relative gains**]{.term} — the concern of a state with its gains from cooperation *relative to*
those of its partner, rather than only with its own absolute gains, arising from the possibility
that the partner's gains will be converted into capabilities used against it [@grieco1988].
:::

Joseph Grieco's 1988 article made this the central point of difference between realists and
liberal institutionalists such as Keohane (Chapter 2): institutions can solve the problem of
cheating, Grieco argued, but they cannot solve the problem that cooperation may help a rival more
than oneself. @powell1991 sharpened the argument by showing that the weight a state places on
relative gains is not fixed but depends on circumstances — in particular, on whether the use of
force is a live possibility between the two states. Where force is unthinkable, as between Canada
and the United States, relative gains matter little and cooperation is easy; where it is
conceivable, relative gains matter a great deal.

That is why the concept belongs in a course on international business. Relative-gains thinking is
the logic by which trade becomes a security question. A sale of advanced semiconductors to a
rival, or of the equipment to make them, may be profitable for the seller and beneficial for the
buyer, and still be prohibited, because it narrows a capability gap the seller's government wishes
to preserve. Export controls on advanced chips are not a failure of economic reasoning; they are
the application of a different calculus, in which the question is not "are we better off?" but "are
we better off *relative to them*?". A firm that sells into the gap between two powers' capabilities
should expect its business to be judged by the second question.

## Two realisms

Realists agree that anarchy shapes behaviour. They disagree about how much power states want, and
the disagreement has direct implications for what to expect from the relationship between the
United States and China.

### Defensive realism: enough is enough

Kenneth Waltz's *Theory of International Politics* (1979) is the founding text of structural, or
neo-, realism. Its claim is that the behaviour of states is shaped less by their domestic
characteristics — their regimes, their leaders, their ideologies — than by the structure of the
system they inhabit: anarchy, plus the **distribution of capabilities** among the units
[@waltz1979]. States are alike in their basic function, which is to survive, and they differ in how
well equipped they are to do it.

::: {.definition}
[**Balance of power**]{.term} — the distribution of capabilities among the major states of a
system, and the tendency, in realist theory, of states to respond to a concentration of power by
building their own capabilities or forming coalitions against the stronger state ("balancing").
:::

In Waltz's version, states seek an *appropriate* amount of power: enough to be secure. Seeking more
is usually self-defeating, because a state that grows too strong provokes others to balance against
it, and ends up less secure than before. The system therefore tends towards equilibrium. Waltz also
argued that a **bipolar** system — two great powers, as in the Cold War — is more stable than a
multipolar one, because with only two principal actors there are fewer relationships to manage,
fewer opportunities for miscalculation, and no uncertainty about who the main threat is.

Waltz's theory sits on an older idea that is essential for understanding how conflict can arise
without anyone wanting it.

::: {.definition}
[**Security dilemma**]{.term} — the situation in which measures a state takes to increase its own
security, such as arming or forming alliances, reduce the security of others, who respond in kind,
leaving all less secure than before even though none intended aggression [@herz1950; @jervis1978].
:::

John Herz named the dilemma in 1950. Robert Jervis's 1978 article gave it its modern form by asking
when it is most severe. His answer turned on two variables: whether offensive and defensive weapons
and postures can be distinguished, and whether offence or defence has the advantage. Where defensive
preparations look defensive and defence is strong, states can secure themselves without
threatening others, and the dilemma is mild. Where they cannot be told apart and offence is
favoured, every precaution looks like preparation for attack, and the dilemma is acute.

The security dilemma is the most useful single idea in this chapter for a risk analyst, because it
describes escalation without intent. A government imposes an export control to protect a
technological lead; the other side reads it as an attempt at containment and responds with
restrictions on a critical mineral; the first reads that as coercion and tightens the control. Each
step is defensive in the mind of the state that takes it. A firm caught in the sequence does not
need either government to want a confrontation in order to lose a market or a supplier.

### Offensive realism: as much as possible

John Mearsheimer's *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), the session's first big work,
accepts Waltz's starting point but reaches a darker conclusion. It rests on five bedrock
assumptions [@mearsheimer2001]:

1. The international system is **anarchic**.
2. Great powers inherently possess some **offensive military capability**.
3. States can never be certain about other states' **intentions**.
4. **Survival** is the primary goal of great powers.
5. Great powers are **rational actors**.

None of these, Mearsheimer notes, is individually alarming. Together they imply that great powers
fear one another, cannot rely on anyone else for their security, and conclude that the best
guarantee of survival is to be as powerful as possible relative to potential rivals. States are
therefore not satisfied with an appropriate amount of power. They **maximise relative power**, and
the ultimate goal is hegemony.

Global hegemony, however, is practically impossible, because of what Mearsheimer calls "the stopping
power of water": large bodies of water make it extremely difficult to project land power across
oceans. The realistic goal is therefore **regional hegemony** — domination of one's own region —
together with preventing any other great power from dominating its region. The United States, on
this account, is the only regional hegemon in modern history, having achieved dominance in the
Western Hemisphere in the nineteenth century, and its foreign policy in the twentieth century can be
read as a series of efforts to prevent the emergence of a hegemon in Europe or in North-East Asia.

The prediction for China follows mechanically. If China continues to grow economically, it will
seek to dominate Asia as the United States dominates the Western Hemisphere, and the United States,
which has spent a century preventing exactly that, will try to stop it. Mearsheimer's answer to the
question of whether China can rise peacefully is no: the result will be intense security
competition, with a considerable possibility of war.

| | Defensive realism (Waltz) | Offensive realism (Mearsheimer) |
|---|---|---|
| How much power do states want? | enough for security | as much as possible, relative to others |
| What restrains them? | balancing by others; expansion is self-defeating | only the limits of their capabilities |
| Expected US–China outcome | managed competition | intense security competition |

A caution belongs here, and the lecture states it twice. Realism predicts **competition**, not war.
In Waltz's version it often predicts restraint, since overexpansion provokes balancing. Even
Mearsheimer's version predicts security competition, of which war is one possible outcome among
several. A risk analyst who reads realism as a forecast of war has misread it; one who reads it as a
forecast that the economic relationship will be subordinated to the strategic one has read it
correctly.

## Power transition and the Thucydides's Trap

Balance-of-power theory treats an equal distribution of capabilities as the condition of stability.
A different tradition reverses the claim.

::: {.definition}
[**Power transition theory**]{.term} — the theory, due to A. F. K. Organski, that peace is most
secure when a dominant power holds a clear preponderance and that war is most likely when a
dissatisfied challenger approaches parity with it [@organski1958; @organski1980].
:::

Organski's *World Politics* (1958) described the international system as a hierarchy rather than a
balance. At the top sits a dominant power that built the existing international order and benefits
from it; below are great powers, some satisfied with the order and some not. Industrialisation
drives the rise of new powers, and their growth is uneven. Danger arises when a challenger that is
*dissatisfied* with the order — that did not write its rules and does not benefit from them as much
as it believes it should — draws close to the dominant power in capabilities. At that point the
challenger has both the motive and, increasingly, the means to revise the order, and the dominant
power has reason to act before its lead disappears. In empirical work, parity is often
operationalised as the challenger reaching around 80 per cent of the dominant power's
capabilities, a threshold the practice round places on the chart as a reference line.

@organski1980 took the theory to data in *The War Ledger*, and a substantial literature followed,
including the essays collected in @kugler1996. The tradition connects to Robert Gilpin's account of
hegemonic war, in which the order established by a dominant power becomes misaligned with the
underlying distribution of power as others grow, until a war redistributes both [@gilpin1981]. It
connects too to Paul Kennedy's history of the great powers, which argued that dominant powers tend
to overextend their commitments relative to their economic base [@kennedy1987].

Power transition theory has an obvious affinity with Chapter 2. There, the question was whether the
rules of the economic order would survive the shrinking share of their authors. Here, the theory
supplies a mechanism: a rising power that did not write the rules is likely to be dissatisfied with
them, and as its capabilities approach those of the rule-writer, its ability to revise them grows.

### Allison's sixteen cases

Graham Allison's *Destined for War* (2017), the session's second big work, brought the argument to
a mass audience under a classical name. Thucydides, the historian of the war between Athens and
Sparta in the fifth century BCE, wrote, in the translation Allison uses, that "it was the rise of
Athens and the fear that this instilled in Sparta that made war inevitable" [@allison2017].

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · Thucydides, fifth century BCE]{.archive-label}

The Peloponnesian War began in 431 BCE and ended, after a long truce and its collapse, with the
defeat of Athens in 404. Its historian, Thucydides, was an Athenian general who had been exiled
after failing to prevent the loss of a city to Sparta's forces, and who spent his exile collecting
the accounts of both sides.

The sentence Allison made famous comes early in the history, where Thucydides distinguishes the
immediate grievances that both sides publicly cited — disputes over Corcyra and Potidaea, over
alliances and trade — from what he calls the truest cause, the one least spoken of: the growth of
Athenian power and the alarm it caused in Sparta. That distinction between the stated occasion and
the structural cause is the analytical move every realist since has borrowed.

It is worth reading what Thucydides does *not* say. He does not say that rising powers always fight
ruling ones; he says this war had this cause. He was writing about a particular conflict between
two particular cities, one a maritime and commercial empire and the other a land power with a
very different political system. The generalisation to a law of history is Allison's, and the
critics' first objection is to the generalisation, not to Thucydides.
:::

Allison and the Thucydides's Trap project at Harvard identify **sixteen** cases in the past five
hundred years in which a rising power challenged a ruling one. **Twelve** ended in war. The four
that did not — among them the United States overtaking Britain in the early twentieth century, and
the Cold War rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union — are presented as evidence that
war is not inevitable, but that avoiding it requires deliberate and painful adjustment by both
sides.

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · Britain and the United States, 1895–1903]{.archive-label}

At the end of the nineteenth century the United States overtook Britain as the world's largest
economy, and its navy was growing. The two powers had serious quarrels. In 1895 a boundary dispute
between Venezuela and British Guiana brought a sharply worded American demand that Britain accept
arbitration, and talk of war on both sides of the Atlantic. Britain accepted arbitration, and a
tribunal issued its award in 1899. In 1901 the Hay–Pauncefote Treaty released the United States from
an earlier agreement to share control of any canal across Central America, clearing the way for an
American-controlled canal at Panama. In 1903 a tribunal settled the boundary between Alaska and
Canada largely in the American favour, with the British member siding with the Americans against
the Canadian members' objections.

In each dispute, the ruling power gave way. Britain, facing a rising Germany closer to home and
overstretched across its empire, chose to accommodate the rising power in the Western Hemisphere
rather than contest it. The case is the strongest evidence that a transition can be peaceful. It is
also a reminder of the price: the accommodation was made, in part, at the expense of a third party —
Canada — whose interests the ruling power judged less important than the relationship with the
rising one.

For a firm, the analogy is uncomfortable and useful. A peaceful transition between great powers is
not costless for everyone else. The terms on which it is reached may be paid by actors who were
not at the table.
:::

### The critics

The Thucydides's Trap has been criticised on four grounds, and the lecture puts them to the room
before showing them.

**Case selection.** The cases were assembled with knowledge of the outcome. What counts as a "rising"
power, a "ruling" power, and a "challenge" is elastic enough that peaceful transitions — which leave
less of a trace in history than wars — may be under-counted, inflating the proportion that ended in
war. A ratio of twelve to sixteen is only as informative as the rule that produced the sixteen.

**Nuclear weapons.** No pre-1945 case involved two powers each capable of destroying the other.
Nuclear deterrence changes the expected cost of great-power war so drastically that it is not clear
any pre-nuclear case is informative about the present. The Cold War, the one transition-like rivalry
between nuclear powers in the dataset, did not end in war between the principals.

**Interdependence.** The United States and China are bound by trade, investment and finance far more
tightly than Athens and Sparta were. Liberal theory holds that interdependence raises the cost of
conflict and so makes it less likely. The realist reply is that Britain and Germany were also major
trading partners in 1914, and fought anyway. Session 04 takes up the debate with data.

**Measuring "rise".** The trap depends on knowing when a challenger is approaching parity, and that
depends on how power is measured. @beckley2018 argues that the conventional gross indicators — GDP,
population, composite indices of capability — systematically overstate the power of large, populous
countries, because they ignore the costs a state bears in governing, feeding, policing and
protecting its population. A country with a large GDP and a large population may have much less
surplus available for projecting power than the gross figures imply. Beckley proposes measures that
account for such costs, and on them the gap between the United States and China looks larger and the
transition further off.

The last criticism is the one this session's practice round can examine directly, because it turns
on the choice of measure.

## Measuring power

Every theory in this chapter turns on a quantity — the distribution of capabilities, parity,
relative power — that none of them can observe directly. Power is a latent concept. What can be
observed are its supposed ingredients: people, money, industry, arms. The standard way of combining
them in quantitative international relations was devised in the 1960s at the University of Michigan
by J. David Singer and his collaborators on the Correlates of War project.

::: {.definition}
[**Composite Index of National Capability (CINC)**]{.term} — a yearly index of a state's material
capabilities, equal to the unweighted average of its shares of the state-system total on six
indicators: total population, urban population, iron and steel production, primary energy
consumption, military personnel and military expenditure [@singer1972].
:::

The construction has three steps. For every state in the international system in a given year, take
six indicators, grouped into three dimensions: demographic (total population, urban population),
industrial (iron and steel production, primary energy consumption) and military (military
personnel, military expenditure). For each indicator, compute the state's share of the system total
that year. Average the six shares. The result is a number between 0 and 1; summed over all states,
it comes to about 1 — not exactly, because a state with a missing component is averaged over the
components it has. In the current release, the National Material Capabilities dataset version 7.0,
the coverage runs from 1816 to 2022, one row per state-year.

The design has real virtues. It is transparent: anyone can recompute it. It is long: two centuries
of comparable data, which is what allows Organski's and Mearsheimer's arguments to be confronted
with evidence at all. And by using shares of the system total, it sidesteps the problem of comparing
military spending in nineteenth-century pounds with twenty-first-century dollars — each year is
compared only with itself. Mearsheimer's own measures of latent power in the *Tragedy* rely on
similar ingredients: for the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, he proxies a state's wealth
with indicators of industrial output such as steel production and energy consumption.

### What it rewards

The design also embodies a theory of power, and it is a theory of the 1960s. Two of the six
components are population and two are heavy industry. CINC therefore rewards **size and
smokestacks**. A large, populous, industrialising country — one that produces a great deal of steel
and burns a great deal of energy — will score highly even if its technology is behind, its
productivity low and its military poorly equipped.

The consequence is visible in the replication. CINC shows China passing the United States much
earlier than military expenditure or GDP at market exchange rates do. One underlying reality, three
measures, three dates. The practice round asks you to find each date for yourself; the point to
take into it is that the date of the "transition" is a property of the measure, not only of the
world.

### What it misses

CINC is equally instructive for what it cannot see. It has no component for **technology** — the
quality of weapons, the sophistication of industry, the capacity to innovate. It has no component for
**alliances**, although a state with many capable allies commands far more power than its own
figures suggest. It has nothing on **financial power**: the role of a state's currency in
international payments, the depth of its capital markets, its control of the institutions through
which money moves. And it has nothing on **control of networks** — the payment systems, cloud
platforms and communications infrastructure that @farrell2019 identify as instruments of coercion
(Chapter 2). These are precisely the forms of power that reach firms most directly, and Sessions 08,
10 and 11 return to them.

The choice of measure is not a technicality, and Beckley's critique shows why. If power is gross
resources, China caught up long ago. If it is net resources after the costs of a large population,
or military capability weighted by quality, or control of the networks through which the world
economy runs, the picture changes. Each measure is defensible. None is neutral.

## Reading two centuries in one chart

The long-run chart of capability shares is a compressed history of the modern state system, and it
is worth knowing what to expect before building it, so that the anomalies are recognisable as
anomalies.

The first thing to expect is that the chart is not a chart of a fixed set of countries. The
Correlates of War data describe the members of the state system in each year, and membership
changes. China and Japan enter the data in 1860. Germany, coded `GMY`, disappears in 1945 and
returns in 1991; in between, the Federal Republic and the Democratic Republic appear as separate
states, `GFR` and `GDR`. Japan is absent from 1946 to 1951, under occupation. The Soviet Union, by
contrast, is coded as `RUS` throughout, so that Russia, the USSR and Russia again form one continuous
series. Each of these is a decision by the data's authors about what counts as the same state over
time, and each produces a visible break or a visible continuity on the chart that has nothing to do
with power and everything to do with coding. The codes themselves are a second trap: they look like
ISO codes and are not, so that a join with the World Bank data of Chapter 2 on the three-letter code
will silently fail for Germany, the United Kingdom and France.

The second thing to expect is that the components are incomplete. Missing values of the six
components are coded as `-9`, not left blank. The index itself has no missing values, because it is
averaged over whatever components exist, but any calculation that uses a component directly must
exclude the `-9`s first or it will be wrong in a way that is easy to miss on a chart of shares.

The third is the substantive story. Read with these cautions, the chart shows a sequence that the
theories of this chapter were built to explain: a nineteenth-century system in which European
powers and then the United States hold the largest shares; the two World Wars as moments when the
distribution is violently rearranged; a Cold War in which two powers dominate — Waltz's bipolarity,
visible as data; and, from the late twentieth century, the rise of China's share. The practice round
asks you to date the last of these yourself, and to see how the date moves when the measure does.

### Bipolar, multipolar, or something else?

The chart also bears on a question that has occupied realists since the Soviet Union dissolved in
1991: what is the *polarity* of the system now? The 1990s were widely described as a unipolar
moment, with the United States unmatched. Waltz expected that unipolarity would not last, because
other states would balance against a power with no counterweight. Whether the present system is
returning to bipolarity — the United States and China — or moving to a multipolar one with several
significant powers is contested, and the answer depends, once again, on the measure.

The question matters to firms because the two configurations imply different environments.
A bipolar system tends to produce two blocs with relatively clear membership, so that the firm's
problem is choosing, or being made to choose, a side, and organising its operations to comply with
two sets of rules. A multipolar system produces more hedging states — governments that trade with
both sides, refuse to join either bloc, and bargain for advantage between them — and therefore more
room for firms to locate operations in third countries that remain open to both. The geography of
"connector" economies, which Session 04 examines with trade data, is a direct consequence of which
of the two worlds firms are in.

## How a firm would know the balance is shifting

The second half of the session's question — how would a multinational know that the balance is
shifting? — has two answers, a structural one and a behavioural one.

The structural answer is the one the practice round builds: track the distribution of capabilities
on several measures, and watch the ratios. A challenger's share approaching the dominant power's on
one measure, while still far behind on others, is a transition in progress and a transition
contested; it is the situation in which the security dilemma is most acute, because each side can
read the evidence as it wishes. Structural indicators of this kind, in the typology of Session 01,
explain *why* tension is building. They do not say *when* it will matter.

The behavioural answer is to watch what states do with the rules. A shifting balance shows up first
not in war but in the instruments of economic statecraft: export controls, investment screening,
sanctions and counter-sanctions, restrictions on data, rules about where a firm may source a
component. These are the measures by which states convert relative-gains concerns into policy, and
they are observable — in official gazettes, in company disclosures, in the event datasets of later
sessions — long before any military confrontation. For a firm, the most reliable signal that the
balance is shifting is a change in which rules it is being asked to follow.

## What it means for the multinational

The realist frame leads to five implications for a firm, each of which recurs later in the course.

**The balance of power sets the rules of trade.** Who can impose sanctions, export controls and
market-access conditions depends on who holds capabilities others need: the largest market, the
dominant currency, the critical technology. A firm's exposure to a state's economic statecraft is a
function of how much of its business depends on capabilities that state controls.

**A transition changes which rules you follow.** A firm operating in both the United States and
China increasingly faces two compliance regimes — and, since China adopted its own instruments of
counter-sanction in 2020 and 2021, the possibility of penalties in one jurisdiction for complying
with the other's rules. The question "is this transaction legal?" has acquired a second clause:
"legal where, and under whose law?".

**Relative gains turn commerce into security.** Products that would make a rival stronger — advanced
semiconductors, the tools that make them, artificial intelligence, batteries, aerospace — move from
the trade ministry's jurisdiction to the security establishment's. For firms in these sectors,
market access is no longer negotiated mainly on economic grounds, and commercial arguments about
mutual benefit carry less weight than they did.

**The date of the transition depends on the measure.** A board slide that says "China has overtaken
the United States" should say *on what*. On some measures it happened decades ago; on others it has
not happened. The decision the slide is meant to inform — whether to expand, hedge, or relocate —
depends on which kind of power is relevant to it.

**Security dilemmas escalate without intent.** A defensive measure by one government — an export
control, a military exercise, an investment restriction — is read by the other as offensive and
triggers a response that may fall on firms that had nothing to do with the original measure. A firm
cannot assume that because neither government wants a confrontation, none will occur.

Mapped onto the five channels of this course, the balance of power runs through every one, but
most directly through **market access** (which markets will remain open to firms from which bloc)
and **assets and people** (where conflict, if it comes, will fall). It reaches **inputs** through
export controls on critical goods, **finance and payments** through the dominant currency, and
**data and operations** through the control of networks.

## What the figure does not license

The target figure — the shares of world material capabilities of the major powers since 1816, with
the US–China crossover — is the picture behind both big works. It does not license four
conclusions.

CINC measures **material capabilities**, not influence, intentions or the likelihood of war. A
crossover on the chart is not a prediction of conflict. Power transition theory says war is more
likely near parity *if* the challenger is dissatisfied; dissatisfaction is not on the chart.

It **overweights size**. Population and heavy industry account for four of six components. The
index cannot see semiconductors, alliances, the dollar or control of networks — the forms of power
that matter most for the economic statecraft firms face.

It **ends in 2022**. The most recent shifts in the distribution of capabilities are not in it, and
for a firm making decisions now the most recent years are the most relevant.

It describes **states**, not markets. A firm's exposure depends on where its revenue, suppliers,
assets and data sit, not on the system's distribution of power. Two firms in the same country can
face entirely different consequences from the same transition.

## A short review of the literature

::: {.lit}
Realism's modern structural form begins with @waltz1979, who explained state behaviour by anarchy
and the distribution of capabilities and judged bipolarity the more stable configuration. The
security dilemma that underlies much of it was named by @herz1950 and given its analytical form by
@jervis1978, who related its severity to the offence–defence balance. @grieco1988 made relative
gains the realist objection to liberal institutionalism, and @powell1991 showed that the weight
states put on relative gains depends on whether force is a live option between them.
@mearsheimer2001 pushed the structural argument to its offensive conclusion — power maximisation
and regional hegemony — and applied it to China.

A parallel tradition locates danger in hierarchy rather than balance. @organski1958 introduced power
transition theory; @organski1980 and the contributions in @kugler1996 tested it on data. @gilpin1981
gave a systemic account of hegemonic war, and @kennedy1987 a historical account of how great powers
rise and decline relative to their economic base. @allison2017 reframed the transition debate for the
US–China relationship through sixteen historical cases.

Measurement runs through all of it. @singer1972 introduced the Composite Index of National
Capability and the Correlates of War data on which much of the quantitative literature rests.
@beckley2018 argued that gross indicators overstate the power of populous states and proposed net
measures, which change the dating of the US–China transition. @farrell2019 identified a form of
power — control over network hubs — that no capability index was designed to capture.

The through-line is that every theory of the balance of power is also a claim about how to measure
it, and that the measurement is where theories can be checked against the world — and where they
most often disagree.

## Into the practice round

The practice round rebuilds the distribution of material capabilities among the major powers from
1816 to 2022 using the Correlates of War data, then sets the US–China relationship against the
parity and 80 per cent thresholds, and then measures the same transition with military expenditure
and GDP. The steps are in the brief that follows. What this chapter adds is the reason each step
matters.

When you sum CINC across all states and find that the total is not exactly one, you are seeing the
consequence of averaging over the components a state has — a reminder that every index carries its
construction with it. When you build the ratio of challenger to leader and set a reference line at
80 per cent, you are operationalising Organski's theory, and the line is only as meaningful as the
theory. When you find the crossover year, and then find two different years on two other measures,
you are reproducing Beckley's critique on your own screen.

Then write the catch, for your team's firm. A crossover on a capability chart does not tell a
multinational that war is coming, that its Chinese revenue is at risk, or that its American
suppliers are safe. It tells it that, on one measure that rewards size and heavy industry, the
distribution of material capabilities has shifted. The question for the firm is which kind of power
its own exposures depend on — markets, technology, currency, networks — and whether the measure on
the chart can see it.
