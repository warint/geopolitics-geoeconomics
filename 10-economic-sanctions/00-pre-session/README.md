## 1. The reading — 80 min

**Felbermayr, G., Kirilakha, A., Syropoulos, C., Yalcin, E., & Yotov, Y. V. (2020). The Global
Sanctions Data Base. *European Economic Review*, 129, 103561.**
[doi:10.1016/j.euroecorev.2020.103561](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2020.103561) — read in full.
**Its descriptive figures — cases over time by type, and outcomes by objective — are today's target.**

**Hufbauer, G. C., Schott, J. J., Elliott, K. A., & Oegg, B. (2007). *Economic Sanctions
Reconsidered* (3rd ed.). Peterson Institute for International Economics.** — chapter 1 and the
chapter on how success is assessed (library reserve).

**Pape, R. A. (1997). Why economic sanctions do not work. *International Security*, 22(2), 90–136.**
[doi:10.1162/isec.22.2.90](https://doi.org/10.1162/isec.22.2.90)

Annotate four things:

| | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **1** | How Hufbauer et al. **code success** — the two components they combine |
| **2** | Pape's **critique**: why he believes most of their "successes" are not |
| **3** | How the GSDB is **built**: what a case is, the types, the objectives, the outcomes |
| **4** | One way in which sanctions became a **risk for firms** that were not their target |

## 4. Self-check — 15 min

1. Distinguish **comprehensive** from **targeted** ("smart") sanctions, with one example of each.
2. Why might the **threat** of sanctions succeed more often than sanctions actually imposed?
3. What are **secondary sanctions**, and why do they matter to a firm headquartered in a third country?
4. A firm leaves a sanctioned market although the law does not require it. Name two reasons.
