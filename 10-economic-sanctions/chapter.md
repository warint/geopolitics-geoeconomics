## The instrument of first resort

When a government wants to do something about a foreign state's behaviour and is not prepared to go
to war over it, the menu is short. It can protest, which costs nothing and changes nothing. It can
withdraw an ambassador, which is visible and mostly symbolic. Or it can impose economic sanctions:
restrict what its firms and banks may sell to, buy from, lend to or invest in the offending state,
its government, its companies or its leading citizens. Over the last three decades the third option
has moved from the end of the menu to the top. Sanctions are now what governments reach for first,
in response to invasions and coups, nuclear programmes and election interference, human-rights
abuses and cyberattacks. The Global Sanctions Data Base documents both the rise in the number of
active cases since the end of the Cold War and the change in what those cases are made of
[@felbermayr2020].

This chapter asks whether the instrument is an efficient mechanism for *regulating* geopolitical
risk — a way of raising the cost of aggression without the cost of war — or a *source* of it. The
two are not mutually exclusive, and the argument here is that, for a multinational, the second
matters more than the first. A firm does not need to know whether the sanctions on a given state
will achieve their political goal. It needs to know whether they will cut it off from a customer, a
supplier, a bank or an asset; how far the effective perimeter of the measures extends beyond their
legal text; and what the target can do to it in return.

::: {.definition}
[**Economic sanctions**]{.term} — restrictions on economic relations imposed by one or more
*senders* on a *target* — a state, a government, a firm or an individual — in order to change its
behaviour, weaken it, constrain what it can do, or signal disapproval. The instrument is the
restriction; the objective is political.
:::

The definition's last clause lists four objectives, and the answer to "do sanctions work?" depends
on which of them the instrument is held to. A measure that fails to change a regime's policy may
still succeed in making its weapons programme slower and more expensive, or in telling allies and
voters at home that something has been done. Much of the disagreement in the literature comes from
asking the same question of cases that were never trying to do the same thing.

## A very old weapon

Economic coercion is older than the states that now practise it. Thucydides records the Megarian
decree, by which Athens barred the merchants of Megara from the harbours of its empire, among the
grievances that preceded the Peloponnesian War; Napoleon's Continental System tried to defeat
Britain by closing the continent to British trade, and was undone by smuggling and by the costs it
imposed on France's own allies. Both contain, in miniature, the problems this chapter returns to:
sanctions hurt the sender's own merchants, invite evasion through third parties, and have uncertain
political effects.

The modern idea — sanctions as an organised, collective alternative to war — dates from the League
of Nations. Article 16 of its Covenant obliged members to sever trade and financial relations with
any member that resorted to war in breach of its commitments. The idea was tested once, and the test
is the founding case of the literature.

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · The League of Nations and Italy, 1935]{.archive-label}

Italy invaded Ethiopia in October 1935. Within days the League's Council found that Italy had
resorted to war in breach of the Covenant, and the members agreed a package of measures that came
into force in November: an arms embargo, a ban on loans and credits to the Italian government, a ban
on imports of Italian goods, and an embargo on the export to Italy of a list of raw materials.

What was left off the list mattered more than what was on it. Oil was not embargoed; nor were coal
and steel, and the Suez Canal, through which Italy supplied its army in East Africa, stayed open.
The great powers feared driving Italy into Germany's arms, and the United States was outside the
League. Italian forces took Addis Ababa in May 1936, and the League lifted the sanctions in July.

The lesson drawn at the time was that collective sanctions had failed. The lesson worth drawing now
is subtler. The senders wanted to be seen to act while protecting their own interests, and the gap
between the announced objective and the instrument deployed was visible to everyone, including the
target. Every later debate about whether sanctions "work" is, in part, a debate about whether the
measures studied were ever designed to.
:::

The United Nations Charter, drafted a decade later, put the idea on a firmer legal footing: Article
41 allows the Security Council to decide on "measures not involving the use of armed force",
including the "complete or partial interruption of economic relations", and makes them binding on
all members. For most of the Cold War the veto kept the provision almost idle. The Council imposed
mandatory sanctions on Southern Rhodesia after its unilateral declaration of independence in 1965
and an arms embargo on South Africa in 1977, and little else. Sanctions in this period were
overwhelmingly unilateral and American. The end of the Cold War unblocked the Council, and the 1990s
became the "sanctions decade".

## Four ways to describe a sanction

Before anything can be counted it must be classified, and classification embeds a theory of what
sanctions are for. Four dimensions are useful, and the GSDB codes three of them directly.

The **sender** may be one state acting alone, a group of states acting together — the European Union
is the most important plurilateral sender, its measures adopted by unanimity in the Council — or the
United Nations Security Council, whose decisions bind every member. The distinction tells a firm how
many jurisdictions it must satisfy and how much room a competitor from a non-sending state has to
take its place.

The **type** describes the instrument: restrictions on trade (exports, imports, or both, partial or
complete), on finance (loans, investment, payments, asset freezes), on travel, on arms, on military
assistance, and a residual category. The GSDB records each as a separate indicator, so a single case
can be of several types at once — which is why any stacked count of cases by type exceeds the number
of cases.

The **scope** distinguishes measures aimed at a whole economy from measures aimed at named persons,
entities or sectors.

::: {.definition}
[**Comprehensive versus targeted sanctions**]{.term} — *comprehensive* sanctions restrict all, or
nearly all, economic relations with a target country; *targeted* or "smart" sanctions restrict
relations only with named individuals and entities (asset freezes, travel bans), with specific
sectors (energy, finance, defence), or in specific goods (arms, dual-use items, luxury goods). The
distinction is about who is meant to bear the cost.
:::

The **objective** is what the sender says it wants. The GSDB distinguishes changing a policy,
destabilising a regime, resolving a territorial conflict, preventing a war, ending one, fighting
terrorism, protecting human rights and restoring or defending democracy. Many cases have more than
one, and the stated objective is not always the real one. A sender that announces "policy change"
may be content with "punishment", and one that announces "democracy" may care more about signalling
to its own electorate. Coders work from the public record, so the objective variable is a record of
what senders said, not of what they wanted.

## Do they work? Coding success

The modern empirical literature begins with one book in three editions. Gary Clyde Hufbauer and
Jeffrey Schott, later joined by Kimberly Ann Elliott and Barbara Oegg, assembled every sanctions
episode of the twentieth century they could document and asked of each a question that sounds
simple: did it succeed [@hufbauer2007]?

Their answer to the problem of defining success has become the field's reference point, so it is
worth stating precisely. Each episode is scored on two components. The first is the **policy
result**: how far the outcome the sender sought was actually achieved, on a scale from failure to
success. The second is the **contribution of sanctions**: how far the sanctions, as opposed to other
factors — military force, a change of government for unrelated reasons, a negotiation that would
have happened anyway — contributed to that outcome. Each component is scored from 1 to 4, and the
two are multiplied into a success score. A case counts as a success when the product reaches 9 or
more: that is, when the outcome was at least partly achieved *and* sanctions made at least a modest
contribution to it.

::: {.definition}
[**Sanctions success (Hufbauer et al.)**]{.term} — an episode in which the sender's policy goal was
at least partly achieved and sanctions made at least a modest contribution to that achievement,
operationalised as a product of the two scores, each from 1 to 4, of 9 or more. Success is a joint
judgement about an outcome and a cause.
:::

On this definition, roughly a third of the episodes in the study count as at least partial
successes. The rate varies systematically with the circumstances. Sanctions succeed more often when
the goal is modest — the release of a prisoner rather than the overthrow of a regime — when the
target is economically small and weak relative to the sender, when target and sender were previously
on friendly terms and trade heavily, and when the measures are imposed quickly and decisively rather
than ratcheted up slowly, giving the target time to adapt. They succeed less often when the goal is
major, when the target is an adversary, and when the target can turn to other suppliers and buyers.

That last condition was formalised by Bryan Early as *sanctions busting*: third countries whose
firms step into the trade the sanctions forbid, for profit or for strategic reasons, and so supply
the target with the alternatives that make resistance affordable [@early2015]. It is the reason
multilateral sanctions are not straightforwardly more effective than unilateral ones. The more
senders there are, the fewer alternatives remain for the target, but the harder it is to agree on
anything strong, and the more likely the package resembles the League's of 1935.

The "about a third" figure appears in policy debates as a fact about the world. It is nothing of the
kind. It is a fact about one coding of one set of cases, produced by one team's judgements on two
four-point scales, and changing any of those three elements changes the number.

## Pape's audit, and why the debate never closed

The most consequential demonstration of this came from Robert Pape, who in 1997 went back through
the cases Hufbauer and his colleagues had counted as successes in their second edition and asked
whether each one survived scrutiny [@pape1997]. His conclusion was that most did not. In many, he
argued, force or its threat had done the work; in others the target had made no real concession, or
none attributable to sanctions. By his reckoning only five of the forty claimed successes held up,
and he concluded that economic sanctions rarely achieve major political goals and should not be
expected to.

Elliott replied that the glass was half full rather than empty [@elliott1998]. Sanctions, she
argued, had never been claimed to be a substitute for force in pursuit of major goals, and judged
against the modest goals for which they are typically used they did better than Pape allowed;
moreover, their record had improved as they were used more often in concert, after the Cold War.
Pape answered that sanctions still did not work [@pape1998]. The exchange is usually remembered as a
debate about effectiveness. It is better understood as a debate about coding: about what counts as a
concession, how much a contribution must be, and what the counterfactual is.

The counterfactual problem is the deep one, and David Baldwin had set it out a decade earlier
[@baldwin1985]. To say that sanctions failed is to say that some other instrument would have done
better. If the alternative was war, a sanction that achieved nothing at little cost may still have
been the better choice; if the alternative was diplomacy, a sanction that achieved a little at great
cost may have been the worse one. Effectiveness, on Baldwin's account, is always comparative and
always relative to cost, and a success rate computed without reference to the alternatives answers a
question no policymaker faces.

A second line of argument asks *how* sanctions are supposed to work, and finds the naive answer
wanting. Johan Galtung, studying the sanctions on Rhodesia, described what he called the naive
theory: economic pain causes political disintegration, which forces the government to comply
[@galtung1967]. The Rhodesian evidence suggested the opposite. Pressure produced a rally around the
government, a turn towards self-sufficiency, and domestic winners — smugglers, import substitutes,
holders of scarce foreign exchange — with an interest in the sanctions continuing. Jonathan Kirshner
later generalised the point: sanctions work, when they work, by changing the relative power of
groups within the target, and their effect therefore depends on who inside the target bears the cost
and who profits from it [@kirshner1997].

::: {.step}
**The lesson for an analyst.** "Do sanctions work?" has no answer independent of how a case, an
objective and a success are coded, and of what the comparison is. Which objective counts when a case
has several, whether cases under way enter the denominator, how large a contribution must be: each
coding decision moves the share judged successful.
:::

## The cases we never see

There is a deeper problem with any success rate computed from imposed sanctions, and it was put most
clearly by Daniel Drezner. His first book began from a puzzle: states impose sanctions most often on
their adversaries, yet adversaries are the targets least likely to concede, because they expect
future conflict and fear that any concession will be exploited; allies concede more readily, but are
sanctioned less often [@drezner1999]. The second step of the argument concerns the threat
[@drezner2003]. If a target that would concede does so as soon as sanctions are threatened, the
episode ends before any measure is imposed. The cases that reach imposition are disproportionately
those in which the target had decided to resist.

::: {.definition}
[**Selection effect of threats**]{.term} — the bias that arises when sanctions most likely to
succeed do so at the threat stage and are therefore never imposed. Samples of imposed sanctions
over-represent hard cases, so imposed sanctions look less effective than the instrument is. The most
significant effects of economic coercion may be the ones that never appear in a list of sanctions.
:::

The implication for measurement is direct. A dataset that records only impositions cannot estimate
the effectiveness of the instrument, because it has selected its cases on a variable correlated with
the outcome. This is the reason the Threat and Imposition of Economic Sanctions dataset exists. T.
Clifton Morgan, Navin Bapat and their colleagues recorded threats as well as impositions, first for
1971–2000 [@morgan2009] and then, in an extended version, for 1945–2005 [@morgan2014]. In TIES a
case begins with a threat or an imposition, whichever comes first, and the dataset records whether
sanctions were in fact imposed. A substantial share of its cases never were.

The selection argument also cuts the other way: imposition is a costly signal of the sender's
seriousness, and some of its effects may come from the signal rather than the pain. Neither effect
can be read off a count of imposed cases. A dataset that records threats can at least show where
the cases go.

### What the record of threats shows

TIES contains 1,412 cases that began between 1945 and 2005. In 845 of them sanctions were
eventually imposed; in the other 567 — two cases in five — the episode ended without any measure
taking effect. @fig-ch10-ties-cases shows when the cases began. The post-Cold War surge is there,
as in every count of sanctions: 547 cases began in the 1990s, against 226 in the 1980s, and the
single busiest year was 1993, with 87. The share that never got past a threat moves more than the
total: a third in the 1950s and 1960s, more than half in the 1980s, a third again in the 1990s and
almost half between 2000 and 2005. Counted by impositions alone, the 1980s look quiet. They were a
decade of threats.

Two findings stand out. The first is Drezner's prediction in its plainest form: complete
acquiescence is more common at the threat stage (31 percent of cases that ended there) than after
imposition (22 percent). Targets that will give in tend to do so before the measures bite. The second is less often
noticed: threats are frequently bluffs. In 40 percent of the cases that ended at the threat stage
the sender backed down without imposing anything, and a sender that backs down after imposition —
lifting measures the target has not complied with — is almost as common, at 30 percent. A
"success rate" that counts only targets that comply hides the fact that senders often quit first.

@fig-ch10-ties-outcomes splits the same outcomes by the issue at stake, and the selection effect
turns out to be concentrated where Drezner said it would be. Half of the TIES cases concern trade
practices or economic reform alone — many of them disputes between the United States and its
allies over market access. In those economic disputes, 31
percent of the cases settled at the threat stage ended with the target's complete acquiescence,
against 11 percent of those that went on to imposition, where negotiated settlements and
stalemates dominate. In political and security disputes, the difference between the stages almost
disappears (30 against 27 percent), and what changes instead is the sender's resolve: half of the
political threats ended with the sender backing down. Allies concede to threats over trade; adversaries
facing threats over security mostly wait to see whether the sender means it.

## From comprehensive to smart

The sanctions decade of the 1990s produced the instrument's most damaging case, and that case
changed how sanctions are designed.

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · Iraq, 1990–2003]{.archive-label}

Four days after Iraq invaded Kuwait in August 1990, the Security Council adopted Resolution 661,
imposing comprehensive sanctions: a ban on all trade with Iraq except medical supplies and, in
humanitarian circumstances, foodstuffs. The war of 1991 expelled Iraq from Kuwait; the sanctions
stayed, tied now to the disarmament of Iraq's weapons programmes.

They stayed for more than a decade, and their humanitarian consequences became the central fact of
the debate about them. Malnutrition, the collapse of water and health services and a rise in
mortality were documented by UN agencies and researchers, although the size of the excess mortality
and its attribution to sanctions rather than to war damage and the regime's choices remain
contested. In 1995 the Council created the Oil-for-Food Programme (Resolution 986), allowing Iraq to
sell oil under UN supervision to pay for food and medicine. The programme relieved the shortages and
was in turn corrupted through surcharges and kickbacks, and the subsequent inquiry named companies
from many countries.

The regime survived the sanctions intact. Those who bore their cost were the population and, through
the programme's scandals, the firms that had traded with the regime under UN licence.
:::

The Iraqi experience made it difficult for senders to defend comprehensive sanctions on a whole
population, and the response was the turn to *targeted* measures. In intergovernmental processes
around the turn of the century — at Interlaken on financial sanctions, at Bonn and Berlin on arms
embargoes and travel bans, at Stockholm on implementation — governments and experts worked out how
to aim sanctions at those responsible: freeze the assets and ban the travel of named leaders and
financiers, embargo arms and the goods that sustain a war, restrict specific sectors.

Targeted sanctions created problems of their own. Listing is an executive act with severe
consequences, and in the *Kadi* judgment of 2008 the European Court of Justice annulled the EU's
implementation of a UN listing for disregarding the listed person's rights of defence; review
procedures and a UN Ombudsperson followed. For firms the lesson was that lists change, are
litigated, and may be annulled — and that a listing in one jurisdiction may not exist in another.

Whether targeting works better is an empirical question with a mixed answer. The most comprehensive
study of UN targeted sanctions distinguishes three purposes — to coerce a change in behaviour, to
constrain the target's ability to act, and to signal a norm — and finds that the measures do better
at constraining and signalling than at coercing [@biersteker2016]. On humanitarian cost the evidence
is clearer for the comprehensive kind: Dursun Peksen finds that sanctions are associated with a
deterioration of the target government's respect for physical-integrity rights, and that the effect
is stronger for comprehensive measures [@peksen2009]. The targets of sanctions, in other words, tend
to pass the cost down.

## The financial turn

The second change in the design of sanctions since 2000 has been the rise of financial measures, and
it is the change that matters most to a multinational.

Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman explained it in the framework introduced in Chapter 8
[@farrell2019]. Global economic networks — payment systems, financial messaging, correspondent
banking, the internet's infrastructure — are not flat. They have hubs, and the hubs are
disproportionately under the jurisdiction of a few states, above all the United States. A state with
jurisdiction over a hub can exploit it in two ways: as a *panopticon*, using the information flowing
through the hub to see what others are doing, and as a *chokepoint*, denying others access to the
hub and therefore to the network. International dollar payments clear, in the end, through banks
with access to the US financial system; banks around the world communicate payment instructions
through SWIFT, a Belgian cooperative subject to European law. Cutting a bank off from either is a
sanction of a different order from an embargo on its country's exports.

This is why targeted does not mean contained. A financial sanction is aimed at named banks, but
every firm that pays or is paid through those banks is affected, and the banks of third countries —
which need access to the dollar system more than they need any single customer — will cut off a
sanctioned client long before the law requires them to. Sanctions on banks, insurers and shippers
propagate down every supply chain that depends on them.

The measures of 2022 against Russia showed the reach of the instrument and its new forms: the
freezing of a large part of the Russian central bank's foreign reserves, the disconnection of major
Russian banks from SWIFT, and, from December 2022, a price cap on seaborne Russian crude, initially
set at 60 US dollars a barrel. The cap did not try to keep Russian oil off the market, which would
have raised the world price; it allowed Western insurers, shippers and financiers to service cargoes
only if the oil had been sold at or below the cap. The chokepoint was the maritime services the
trade relied on, and the market's answer — an older, opaquely owned tanker fleet operating outside
those services — showed that a sanction built on a chokepoint lasts only as long as the chokepoint
does.

## The firm in the middle

Sanctions are aimed at states, governments and the people who run them. They are implemented by
firms. Banks, exporters, shippers and insurers decide, transaction by transaction, whether a
payment, shipment or contract falls within the rules, and bear severe penalties if they decide
wrongly. The firm is therefore not a bystander to sanctions policy. It is its executor, and its
executor of last resort.

### Lists, ownership and the perimeter

Each sending jurisdiction maintains its own legal architecture. In the United States, sanctions
programmes rest mostly on the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977 and are
administered by the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control, whose Specially Designated
Nationals list is the most consulted list in global commerce. In the European Union, restrictive
measures are adopted by Council decision and given effect by regulations binding every person and
firm in the Union. In Canada, they rest on the *Special Economic Measures Act*, the *United Nations
Act* and the *Justice for Victims of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act*. The lists overlap but do not
coincide, and a firm operating across jurisdictions must satisfy all of them.

The list is only the beginning of the perimeter. Under the US "50 percent rule", an entity owned,
directly or indirectly, 50 percent or more in aggregate by listed persons is itself treated as
blocked, whether or not it appears on any list; EU guidance extends the restrictions to entities
*owned or controlled* by listed persons, with control judged on a range of indicators. A firm must
therefore screen not only its counterparties but their owners, and their owners' owners, through
chains of holding companies that may be designed to obscure them.

::: {.definition}
[**Compliance perimeter**]{.term} — the set of counterparties, transactions, goods and jurisdictions
a firm must treat as restricted in order to comply with every sanctions regime that applies to it.
It is wider than any list: it includes entities owned or controlled by listed persons, goods
restricted by end use, and the jurisdictions whose rules reach the firm through its nationality, its
currency, its banks or its inputs.
:::

### Diversion and the duty to look downstream

Sanctions on goods have always leaked through third countries. After 2022 the leak ran through
distributors in countries that did not apply the measures, which re-exported to Russia goods that
had once been shipped there directly. The senders' response moved the perimeter towards the firm.
The United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom and Japan published common lists of
high-priority items — mostly electronic components found in Russian weapons — whose sale to any
destination at risk of diversion called for particular scrutiny. The EU's twelfth package, of
December 2023, obliged exporters of the most sensitive goods to write into their contracts with
third-country buyers a clause prohibiting re-export to Russia, and in the same month the United
States extended its secondary reach to foreign banks serving Russia's military-industrial base.

The trade statistics show the leak plainly. @fig-ch10-diversion indexes the exports of the 27
member states of the European Union to five of Russia's neighbours against their 2021 level, as
reported by the exporting countries to the IMF [@imf2026]. EU exports to Russia fell from 105 billion US
dollars in 2021 to 41 billion in 2023. Over the same two years EU exports to Kyrgyzstan rose almost
tenfold, from 0.31 to 2.95 billion dollars; exports to Armenia and Kazakhstan more than doubled, and
those to Georgia and Uzbekistan rose by more than half (@tbl-ch10-diversion). German exports to
Kyrgyzstan, a country of seven million people with no large manufacturing base, went from 59
million dollars to 772 million. Nothing in these economies explains such growth; their position
between the European Union and Russia — inside a customs union with Russia, for three of them —
does. Maxim Chupilkin, Beata Javorcik and Alexander
Plekhanov trace the same "Eurasian roundabout" at the level of products: the surge is concentrated
in the goods the European Union stopped sending to Russia directly, and the neighbours' exports to
Russia rose in the same goods [@chupilkin2026].

The numbers also put the leak in proportion. EU exports to the five neighbours rose by 14 billion
dollars between 2021 and 2023, while exports to Russia fell by 64 billion: even if every additional
dollar had been re-exported, the roundabout replaced about a fifth of the lost trade. The sanctions
worked on volume and leaked at the margin — and the margin is where enforcement, and the exporter's
exposure, now sit.

The compliance question is therefore no longer only "is my customer listed?" but "where do my goods
end up?". A manufacturer's exposure runs through its *downstream* channels — distributors,
resellers, freight forwarders — as well as through its own counterparties.

### Secondary sanctions and blocking statutes

Primary sanctions bind the sender's own nationals and transactions on its territory. Secondary
sanctions go further: they threaten third-country firms with penalties — exclusion from the sender's
market, its financial system, or its government contracts — if they deal with the target, even where
no person or transaction of the sender is involved.

::: {.definition}
[**Secondary sanctions**]{.term} — measures that penalise third-country persons for dealing with a
sanctioned target, by restricting their own access to the sender's market or financial system. They
make a sender's foreign policy binding on firms that are not its nationals, by forcing them to
choose between the sender and the target.
:::

The tension this creates between allies is old. When the United States legislated against foreign
firms investing in Cuba, Iran and Libya in 1996, the European Union adopted a *Blocking Statute*
forbidding EU firms to comply, and updated it in 2018 when the United States reimposed secondary
sanctions on Iran. European firms nevertheless left Iran in large numbers. The choice a secondary
sanction forces is not a legal one but a commercial one: the US market and the dollar are worth more
to almost any multinational than the target's market is, and no blocking statute changes that
arithmetic. The case of BNP Paribas, which pleaded guilty in the United States in 2014 to processing
transactions for sanctioned parties and paid a penalty without precedent at the time, settled the
question for many compliance departments: dollar transactions with sanctioned parties anywhere in
the world fall within US reach.

The blocking statute has since acquired a mirror image. As Chapter 3 noted, China adopted
instruments of its own in 2020 and 2021 against compliance with foreign sanctions it regards as
discriminatory, so that a firm large in both the United States and China can find that obeying one
state's law breaches the other's.

### Overcompliance and de-risking

Penalties are severe, rules are complex, lists change without notice, and the cost of a mistake is
asymmetric: a bank that refuses a lawful payment loses a fee, a bank that processes an unlawful one
may lose its licence. The rational response is to go further than the law.

::: {.definition}
[**Overcompliance and de-risking**]{.term} — the withdrawal of firms, and above all banks, from
whole countries, sectors or categories of client that the law does not close, because the expected
cost of an error exceeds the revenue at stake. The effective scope of sanctions is set by compliance
departments as much as by governments.
:::

The evidence that this matters is now substantial. Matthieu Crozet and Julian Hinz, studying the
sanctions on Russia after 2014 and the Russian counter-sanctions, find that the trade losses borne
by the sending countries fell largely on products that were not embargoed at all — "friendly fire"
that they attribute mainly to the financial measures and the uncertainty they created [@crozet2020].
A firm selling unrestricted goods to an unrestricted customer may still be unable to get paid,
insured or shipped. If its lawyers say the measures do not require it to exit and its bank says it
will no longer process payments to that country, its sanctions policy has been set by its bank.

## Russia 2022: the exit decision

The full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 produced the largest and fastest expansion of
sanctions against a major economy since the Second World War, and it confronted hundreds of Western
multinationals with a decision most had never modelled: whether to leave a large market that the
law, for many of them, did not require them to leave. Public and employee pressure, the difficulty
of making payments and moving goods, and the expectation of further measures pushed many to suspend,
scale back or exit.

Exit turned out to be harder than announcing it. Russia's countermeasures made the sale of a
foreign-owned business subject to approval by a government commission, required sellers to accept a
steep discount on the valuation, and added a payment to the state budget; buyers were frequently
local managers or firms close to the state. In 2023 a presidential decree allowed the temporary
state management of foreign-owned assets, and the Russian subsidiaries of Danone and Carlsberg were
placed under it within weeks. The firms that waited lost the option of selling on their own terms.

The four options — stay, suspend, sell, walk away — each have a cost and a residual risk. McDonald's
sold its Russian restaurants to a local licensee, which reopened them under a new name. Renault sold
its controlling stake in AvtoVAZ to a state entity for a symbolic rouble, keeping an option to buy
it back within six years — a sale designed to preserve an option on a return. Others suspended
operations and paid for idle assets; others stayed, citing obligations to employees or the essential
nature of their products, and accepted the reputational cost and the risk of seizure.

The firms that left did not leave a vacuum. @fig-ch10-russia-suppliers follows Russia's imports
from its main suppliers, as reported by the exporters. EU exports to Russia, 152 billion dollars in 2013,
fell after Crimea, recovered to 105 billion by 2021, and after the invasion fell to 34 billion by
2025. China's exports to Russia went the other way: from 68
billion dollars in 2021 to 111 billion in 2023 and 116 billion in 2024. Türkiye's almost doubled,
from 5.8 to 10.9 billion, before falling back as Turkish banks, wary of US secondary sanctions,
balked at Russian payments. This is Early's sanctions busting at the scale of a great power:
the target found a supplier large enough, and indifferent enough to the senders' displeasure, to
make the measures a cost rather than a constraint [@early2015].

For a firm weighing exit, the implication is uncomfortable: its market share was not held in
trust. Competitors from non-sending states took it within two years, so a return after sanctions
would be a new entry against established rivals — which makes an option to buy back, like
Renault's, worth less than it looks.

::: {.warn}
**The right answer depends on the firm, not on the country.** Put the same choice to a
consumer-goods firm and to an energy firm, and the answers diverge: the first has brands, local
staff and a domestic supply chain that a buyer can run; the second has assets that cannot be moved,
partners that may be sanctioned, and a product the target state regards as strategic. Country-level
exposure tells a firm that a decision must be taken. It does not tell it which one.
:::

## Through which channels sanctions reach the firm

Sanctions run through all five of the channels set out in Chapter 1. Through **market access**,
export and import bans close markets directly, or indirectly when customers are listed or owned by
listed persons, and dual-use export controls close specific product lines to specific end users.
Through **inputs**, a sanctioned supplier can no longer be paid, and the target's counter-sanctions
close supply from the other side, often deeper in the supply chain than the firm can see. **Finance
and payments** is where financial sanctions bite first — the correspondent bank that will not
process a payment, the letter of credit that cannot be confirmed, the insurer that will not cover a
cargo — and the channel through which overcompliance operates. Through **assets and people**, assets
can be frozen by the sender, or seized, placed under temporary management or sold at a forced
discount by the target, and employees are part of the exit's cost. Through **data and operations**,
software licences, cloud services and technical support may be restricted services in themselves.

Four quantities follow. The firm's **exposure** — revenues, assets and suppliers in sanctioned or
at-risk jurisdictions — is the starting point. The **pipeline** of threats is the selection effect
applied: the cases that matter most are those not yet imposed. **Propagation** runs through the
banks, insurers and logistics providers whose own exposure could cut the firm off. And the target's
**counter-measures** against foreign firms on its soil determine, as Russia showed, what an exit
costs.

## From episodes to a dataset

The Global Sanctions Data Base was built to answer, at scale, questions that case studies cannot:
how the use of sanctions has changed over time, what types and objectives dominate, and — joined to
trade data — what sanctions do to trade flows [@felbermayr2020]. Its unit is the *case*: a sanctions
episode defined by its senders, its target, its start and end, its types and its objectives, with an
outcome coded for each objective. Successive releases have extended the period and revised earlier
cases; the third added the measures following the pandemic and the escalating sanctions on Russia
[@syropoulos2024], and the fifth codes 1,794 cases whose first years run from 1949 to 2025; it is
distributed on request and for non-commercial use only, so the most complete public record of the
instrument cannot be built into a commercial product.

A time series of "active cases" counts, for each year, the episodes whose interval from start to end
contains it, and three decisions are buried in that construction. Cases that have not ended must be
given an end — and the distributed file gives ongoing cases an end year that is the last year of the
data, which is not what its codebook says, so that an analyst who follows the codebook and one who
follows the file obtain different series in the final years. A case of several types appears once in
each type, so a stacked chart of types overstates the number of cases. And a case is a case whether
it consists of a single listing or of a comprehensive embargo, so the number of active cases
measures the frequency of the instrument's use, not its intensity.

The outcome coding carries the success debate into the data. Each objective of each case receives an
outcome — total success, partial success, a negotiated settlement, failure, or ongoing — and the
share judged successful depends on which objective a multi-objective case is counted under and on
whether the ongoing cases, which by definition have not succeeded yet, enter the denominator. These
are not technical details. They are Hufbauer's and Pape's disagreement reproduced in two lines of
arithmetic.

TIES, set beside the GSDB, supplies what the GSDB cannot: the threats that were never imposed. The
two differ in unit, period, coding of senders and targets and definition of outcome, so they cannot
be merged case by case. What they can do is be compared, year by year, to give an order of magnitude
to the selection effect: how much of economic coercion happens before any measure is imposed.

The end-year decision is not peculiar to the GSDB, and TIES shows how much it weighs. Of the 845
TIES cases in which sanctions were imposed, 337 have no end year. Some were still in force when
coding stopped; others faded from the news unrecorded. With the date of the last news story the
coders found, an analyst has three defensible rules: the case was still in force at the end of the
data, it ended at the last news report, or — the implicit choice of anyone who drops missing values
— it lasted only the year it began.

## A short review of the literature

::: {.lit}
The empirical study of sanctions begins with @hufbauer2007, whose three editions established the
case list, the two-component coding of success and the "about a third" finding; @pape1997 audited
the claimed successes and found few that survived, @elliott1998 replied, and @pape1998 rejoined.
@baldwin1985 had already argued that effectiveness must be judged relative to the alternatives and
their costs, which reframes the whole exchange. The mechanism literature runs from @galtung1967,
whose study of Rhodesia dismantled the "naive theory" that pain produces compliance, to
@kirshner1997, who located the effect of sanctions in the redistribution of power among groups
within the target.

The strategic turn came with @drezner1999, who explained why sanctions are imposed most on the
adversaries least likely to concede, and @drezner2003, who identified the selection effect of
threats. The data response was TIES, which records threats as well as impositions [@morgan2009;
@morgan2014]; its outcome codes show complete acquiescence more often at the threat stage than
after imposition, above all in economic disputes. @early2015 showed how third-country sanctions busters sustain targets, which helps
explain why multilateral sanctions do not always outperform unilateral ones.

On design, @biersteker2016 evaluate UN targeted sanctions against the purposes of coercing,
constraining and signalling, and @peksen2009 documents the human-rights cost of sanctions to the
populations of target states. @farrell2019 explain the rise of financial sanctions through the
chokepoints of global networks. The GSDB [@felbermayr2020] and its later releases [@syropoulos2024]
made it possible to study sanctions at the scale of trade data, and @crozet2020 used that kind of
data to show that much of the trade cost of the Russia sanctions after 2014 fell on products that
were not sanctioned at all — the quantitative trace of overcompliance. @chupilkin2026 trace the
opposite leak after 2022: goods rerouted to Russia through the Caucasus and Central Asia.
:::

## What the case count does not license

The descriptive record of the GSDB is clear in its broad lines: the number of active sanctions cases
has risen steeply since the end of the Cold War, and the mix of instruments has shifted from trade
towards finance, travel and named persons. That picture is the right starting point for any view of
sanctions risk. It is also easy to over-read, and four conclusions often drawn from it are not
supported.

It does not license a conclusion about a firm's *exposure*: a count of cases naming a country says
how often senders have used the instrument against it, not how much of a firm's revenue, supply or
payments the measures touch, and a single financial sanction on a country's largest banks can matter
more than a dozen travel bans. It does not license a conclusion about the *perimeter*, which is
drawn by ownership rules, secondary sanctions, diversion clauses and the firm's own banks, and which
reaches customers in countries that appear nowhere in the data as targets. It does not license a
conclusion about the *pipeline*, because the cases that would matter most next year are threats
today. And it says nothing about *duration*: whether a given set of measures will be lifted soon
enough to make waiting the cheaper option, a cost the Russian exits showed to be set less by the
senders than by the target's countermeasures.

The deeper conclusion is about the instrument itself. Sanctions were designed to regulate conflict
between states without war, and on the evidence they do that job partially, at a cost that falls
largely on people who did not choose the policy. For firms the balance is less ambiguous. Each
expansion of the instrument — from trade to finance, from lists to ownership, from targets to the
neighbours through which goods are diverted — has moved more of the work of enforcement onto the
private sector. Whatever sanctions do to their targets, they have made the multinational the place
where foreign policy is executed, and its sanctions risk lives in its footprint, its counterparties'
owners, its banks, its distributors and the countermeasures of its host states. None of those is in
the case list.
