## Count the rules in a telephone

Pick up a mobile phone and ask how many rules it obeys. Its radio speaks a language specified by
3GPP, a partnership of standards organisations in which the engineers of competing
telecommunications firms draft the technical specifications for each generation of mobile networks.
Its wireless connection follows a family of specifications developed under the IEEE, a professional
association of engineers. Its charging port follows a specification written by an industry forum.
The factory that assembled it is probably certified to ISO 9001 for quality management and ISO 14001
for environmental management; its data centre partners to ISO/IEC 27001 for information security.
The company that sold it reports its emissions under the Greenhouse Gas Protocol, a standard
developed by a research institute and a business council, and its suppliers of tin, tantalum,
tungsten and gold carry out due diligence on conflict minerals under frameworks developed by an
intergovernmental organisation and audited by private firms.

Now ask how many of those rules were written by a government. Very few, and in most cases none. Some
have since been made compulsory or referenced in procurement and trade law, but they were drafted
and revised by engineers, auditors, industry associations and non-governmental organisations. The
multinational that sells the phone spends far more of its compliance effort on these rules than on
any treaty.

Session 05 looked at intergovernmental institutions — the organisations states build and in which
states vote. This chapter is about the other half of global governance, the rules that come from
somewhere other than states, and about why that half is increasingly geopolitical.

::: {.definition}
[**Private authority**]{.term} — the capacity of non-state actors (firms, industry associations,
standards bodies, non-governmental organisations) to make rules that others treat as binding, even
though no state has enacted them and no court may enforce them directly.
:::

For a multinational, private authority is two things at once. It is a **risk**: a standard written
elsewhere, by actors the firm does not control, can become a condition of access to a market. And it
is a **lever**: a firm that shapes a standard shapes the market the standard governs, and may write
its own technology, its own processes and its own costs into the rules its competitors must follow.
The question this chapter asks is the one in the session's title: who writes the rules a
multinational actually follows, with whose power — and does trade spread those rules up or down?

## Where private authority comes from

For most of the twentieth century, private authority in international affairs was close to a
contradiction in terms: international relations studied states, and rule-making was what states did.
The concept entered the field in the 1990s, as globalisation multiplied the transactions that
crossed borders faster than states could agree on rules to govern them.

Susan Strange made the argument most sharply. In *The Retreat of the State* she contended that
markets, firms and a variety of non-state actors had come to exercise authority over outcomes —
prices, investment, technology, credit — that had once been the preserve of governments, and that
the study of international political economy had to follow power wherever it actually lay
[@strange1996]. @cutler1999 gave the concept its name in an edited volume on private authority in
international affairs, focusing on the inter-firm arrangements — industry norms, business
associations, private regimes — through which firms govern their own transactions. @hall2002
extended it to a broader set of actors, from credit-rating agencies to transnational NGOs and even
illicit networks, and asked a question that has never been fully settled: what makes private
authority *authority*, rather than simply market power or persuasion?

The usual answer is **legitimacy combined with consequences**. A rule has authority when those it
governs accept that it ought to be followed, and when failing to follow it has costs. Private rules
acquire those costs through markets rather than courts. A supplier that is not certified loses the
contract; a forest company whose timber is not certified loses access to the retailers whose
customers demand certification; a firm whose emissions report does not follow the recognised
protocol loses credibility with investors.

::: {.warn}
"Voluntary" does not mean "optional". A standard required by a firm's buyers, insurers or investors
is, for that firm, as compulsory as a law — and sometimes more so, because a law can be challenged
in court and a lost customer cannot.
:::

Voluntary describes how a standard is adopted — no state compels it. It does not describe how much
choice the firm has once its customers have adopted it.

## The governance triangle

If rules can come from states, firms and NGOs, it helps to have a map. Kenneth Abbott and Duncan
Snidal provide one [@abbott2009]. They study what they call regulatory standards-setting schemes —
arrangements that write standards for business conduct, monitor compliance, and sometimes certify it
— and place each scheme in a triangle whose three corners are the three types of actor that can
write rules.

::: {.definition}
[**Regulatory standards-setting (RSS) scheme**]{.term} — in Abbott and Snidal's terms, an
arrangement through which states, firms, NGOs or combinations of them set standards for the conduct
of firms, and often monitor or certify compliance. Its position in the governance triangle is given
by the share each of the three actor types has in its governance.
:::

Schemes near a **corner** are run by one type of actor. An industry code of conduct, drafted by
firms for firms, sits near the firm corner. A certification scheme created by an activist
organisation sits near the NGO corner. A traditional intergovernmental rule sits near the state
corner. Schemes in the **interior** are hybrids. The Forest Stewardship Council, founded in 1993,
brings together environmental organisations, social organisations and firms in a multi-stakeholder
structure, and sits on the edge between NGOs and firms. The UN Global Compact, launched in 2000 by
the UN Secretary-General as a set of principles to which firms commit, brings states, firms and
civil society together and is often placed near the centre. ISO itself is harder to place: its
members are national standards bodies, some of them government agencies and many of them private
associations whose committees are staffed largely by industry experts — which is why the placement
of ISO standards is a useful argument to have in class rather than a fact to memorise.

The triangle's most important feature is not on the diagram. Abbott and Snidal stress the **shadow
of the state**: even schemes run entirely by private actors are shaped by the possibility that
states will regulate if private actors do not, and by states' decisions to endorse, require,
reference or ignore private schemes. Firms write codes of conduct partly to pre-empt legislation;
NGOs create certification schemes partly because they have despaired of getting legislation; and
states frequently pick up private standards and give them legal force, at which point the scheme
moves across the triangle without a word of its text changing.

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · Brussels, 2022]{.archive-label}

For years, mobile phones in Europe were sold with a variety of incompatible chargers. The European
Commission encouraged manufacturers to converge voluntarily, and a memorandum of understanding among
major producers in 2009 led much of the market towards a common connector — but not all of it, and
not permanently, as connectors evolved and one large manufacturer kept its own.

Meanwhile an industry body, the USB Implementers Forum, had written the specification for a new
reversible connector, USB Type-C. It was a private standard in the purest sense: drafted by firms,
adopted voluntarily, and spreading because device makers found it convenient.

In 2022 the European Union adopted a directive amending its Radio Equipment Directive to require a
common charging port [@europeanunion2022]. From the end of 2024, new mobile phones and a range of
other portable devices sold in the EU had to charge through USB Type-C, with laptops following in
2026. A specification written by an industry forum became, by a single legislative act, a legal
requirement for access to a market of several hundred million consumers. Because few manufacturers
wish to design one version of a phone for Europe and another for everywhere else, the EU rule had
consequences far beyond the EU.

In the governance triangle the specification did not change at all. What changed was its position:
it moved from the firm corner towards the state corner, carried by the shadow of the state that had
been hanging over it for more than a decade. And it illustrates in one case both of the chapter's
main arguments — that private standards can become conditions of market access, and that the
jurisdiction which turns them into law extends its rules well beyond its borders.
:::

For a firm, the triangle is useful because a scheme's position predicts its behaviour. A scheme near
the firm corner is easier for firms to change, and correspondingly less trusted by NGOs and
regulators. A scheme with strong NGO participation is more legitimate in the eyes of activist
consumers and harder for any single firm to steer. A scheme that a state has begun to reference in
law is a scheme that may become mandatory, and a firm that ignores it is betting against the shadow.

## Who writes an international standard?

The triangle tells us what kinds of actor write rules. It does not tell us whose rules win when
actors from different countries meet in the same room. That is the question Walter Mattli and Tim
Büthe ask of the two principal international standards bodies for products, ISO and the
International Electrotechnical Commission [@mattli2003].

Some institutional background makes the question concrete. ISO was founded in 1947, and its members
are national standards bodies — one per country. Standards are drafted in technical committees and
their subcommittees and working groups, where experts nominated by the national bodies —
overwhelmingly engineers employed by firms — negotiate the text. Each national body takes positions
on drafts and votes on them. The process looks consensual and technical, and in one sense it is. But
the experts who sit at the table, the national positions they carry, and the timing with which they
bring proposals all determine whose existing practice becomes the international standard. A firm
whose product already conforms to the standard that emerges has a head start; a firm whose product
does not must redesign.

Mattli and Büthe compare three explanations of who wins. On the first, **technological
rationality**, the technically best solution wins whoever proposes it — the engineers' view of
themselves. On the second, the **primacy of power**, the standard favours the states with the
largest markets — the realist's view of everything. Mattli and Büthe argue for a third.

::: {.definition}
[**Institutional complementarity**]{.term} — in Mattli and Büthe's account, the fit between a
country's domestic standard-setting institutions and those of the international body. A
hierarchical, coordinated national system, which speaks with one voice and passes information
efficiently between firms and the national delegation, matches the structure of ISO and IEC and wins
more influence than a fragmented, decentralised one, regardless of market size.
:::

Their evidence pointed to the advantage of the European national standards bodies, which are
hierarchical and coordinated, over the fragmented, decentralised system of the United States, in
which many private standards developers compete and no single body aggregates national preferences.
The result was striking because it cut against the realist expectation: the country with the largest
market was not the most effective at shaping international product standards. Influence over
"technical" rules depends on how a country and its firms are organised to take part, not only on how
big they are. @buthe2011 later generalised the argument to accounting standards as well as product
standards, showing that the same logic of institutional fit shapes who wins in the private bodies
that write rules for global financial reporting.

The lesson for a firm is practical and somewhat uncomfortable. Participation is costly — it takes
the time of senior engineers over years — and its benefits are diffuse and delayed. It is easy to
conclude that someone else will look after the standard. But if a competitor's national standards
body is better organised than one's own, and that competitor's engineers are in the room, the
standard will reflect their technology, and the firm that stayed away will pay to comply with it. In
standards, absence is a decision.

## Standards as geopolitics

For most of the post-war period, the politics of standards was the politics of firms and of national
standards bodies, and it rarely reached foreign ministries. That has changed. The competition over
telecommunications standards, over battery and charging standards, over the rules for artificial
intelligence and for the flow of data, has become a competition between blocs, each trying to make
its domestic rules the world's.

The reason is that standards confer advantages that outlast the meeting at which they were agreed. A
standard that incorporates patented technology generates licence revenue for as long as it is used;
one that matches a country's industrial practice lowers its firms' costs and raises its
competitors'; one that embeds choices about data access or encryption shapes what governments can
see. And standards are sticky, so early influence compounds. When governments came to see technical
standards as a component of technological leadership, and technological leadership as a component of
national power, standard-setting acquired the stakes of geopolitics — and the bodies in which
standards are written, which had been designed for engineers, found themselves hosting a contest
between states.

The European Union's version of this contest has been described most fully by Anu Bradford as the
**Brussels effect** [@bradford2020]. The EU rarely sets out to impose its rules on the world. It
regulates its own market. But when a large market sets a strict rule, firms that sell in it face a
choice: produce two versions of their product, one for the strict market and one for everyone else,
or produce one version that meets the strictest rule and sell it everywhere. Where production cannot
easily be divided — because of economies of scale, because the product is a single global platform,
or because maintaining two compliance systems costs more than over-complying in lax markets — firms
adopt the strict rule globally, and then often lobby their home governments to adopt it too, so that
their less global competitors bear the same costs. Bradford identifies the conditions under which
the effect operates: a large market, the regulatory capacity to write and enforce rules, a
preference for strict rules, regulation of targets that cannot easily leave the market (consumers
rather than mobile capital), and non-divisibility of production. Data protection, chemical safety,
competition law and product standards are her main cases; the common charger is a small, clean
example.

The Brussels effect is a mechanism of *unilateral* regulatory globalisation, but it shares a logic
with private standards. In both cases, rules travel along the channels of trade, carried by firms
that find it cheaper to adopt one rule everywhere than to maintain many. That shared logic is
precisely what the big work of this session tests.

## Of, by, as: what standards do to organisations

Standards are usually associated with stability and sameness: a standard exists so that things will
be the same everywhere. Nils Brunsson, Andreas Rasche and David Seidl argue that this view misses
most of what is interesting about them [@brunsson2012]. They organise research on standards around
three perspectives.

The first is **standardisation of organisations**: standards that tell firms how to be organised.
ISO 9001 and ISO 14001 are the archetypes. They do not specify what a product must be like or how
much pollution a plant may emit. They specify that a firm must have a management *system* —
documented procedures, objectives, responsibilities, internal audits, management reviews and a
commitment to continual improvement. The second is **standardisation by organisations**: the bodies
that write standards, how they work, and how they compete with each other for adopters. The third is
**standardisation as organisation**: standards as a way of organising markets and fields without
formal hierarchy, coordinating the behaviour of thousands of firms that have no contract with each
other and no common owner.

Their central point is that standards are **dynamic**. They are revised, contested, interpreted
differently in different places, and — most important for anyone using certification as evidence —
**decoupled** from practice.

::: {.definition}
[**Decoupling**]{.term} — the separation between the formal structures an organisation adopts to
signal conformity to external expectations and the practices it actually follows. A firm certified
to a management standard may change its documents and its audits while leaving its operations
largely as they were.
:::

The idea is older than the study of standards. @meyer1977 argued that organisations adopt formal
structures because those structures are regarded as legitimate in their environment, not necessarily
because they make the organisation more efficient, and that organisations protect themselves by
decoupling those structures from the technical core of their work. A certificate can be a ceremony.

Whether ISO 14001 certificates are mostly ceremony is an empirical question, and the evidence is
mixed in an informative way. @potoski2005 found that US facilities certified to ISO 14001 reduced
their pollutant emissions somewhat more than comparable uncertified facilities — the standard's
"weak sword", in their phrase, could still cut, because third-party audits and the reputational
stakes of certification gave it some force. @christmann2006, studying firms in China, found that
whether certification was implemented substantively or symbolically depended on the firm's
circumstances, and in particular on whether its customers monitored what it actually did.
Certification backed by demanding buyers was more likely to change practice; certification sought
only for its signal was more likely to be decoupled. The implication for a multinational is direct.
If it relies on its suppliers' certificates as evidence of environmental performance, the
certificate's value depends on whether anyone — the multinational included — looks behind it.

## Agents and entrepreneurs

Private authority does not all arise in the same way, and its origin matters for how durable it is.
Jessica Green draws the key distinction [@green2013].

::: {.definition}
[**Delegated versus entrepreneurial private authority**]{.term} — *delegated* authority exists when
states hand rule-making or implementation to private actors, who act as their agents;
*entrepreneurial* authority exists when private actors create rules on their own initiative and
others choose to adopt them.
:::

Green's field is carbon governance, and it supplies clean illustrations of both. Under the Clean
Development Mechanism of the Kyoto Protocol, private bodies were accredited to validate and verify
emission-reduction projects: they exercised authority that states had delegated to them, within
rules states had written. The Greenhouse Gas Protocol, by contrast, was created by a research
institute and a business council without any state mandate, and became the dominant framework for
corporate carbon accounting because firms, investors and eventually regulators adopted it.

The distinction tells a firm what to watch. Delegated authority is as durable as the delegation: the
state that handed it over can take it back, rewrite it or abolish the programme, and when the Kyoto
mechanisms gave way to new arrangements under the Paris Agreement, the authority of the bodies that
served them changed with them. Entrepreneurial authority is as strong as the number of its users. It
grows through adoption and network effects, and it can be displaced by a competing standard that
attracts more adopters — or captured by a state that writes it into law, at which point it becomes
something closer to delegated authority after the fact.

Benjamin Cashore's analysis of forest certification shows how entrepreneurial schemes gain the
authority to make rules [@cashore2002]. He calls them non-state market-driven governance systems:
they have no state backing, and their authority derives from the market — from retailers and
consumers who demand certified products, and from the firms in the supply chain who must therefore
decide whether to comply. The Forest Stewardship Council, created by environmental groups with
stricter rules, was soon met by industry-backed schemes with laxer ones, and the competition between
them became a contest over which would be accepted as legitimate by the buyers whose purchases
conferred authority. Private authority, on this view, is not given; it is won, one supply chain at a
time, and it can be lost the same way.

## Race to the bottom, or trading up?

With the concepts in place, the session's central question can be stated precisely. Trade
intensifies competition among producers in different countries. Does that competition push standards
down or up?

The worry is the **race to the bottom**. If firms can produce anywhere and sell everywhere,
governments compete for investment and exports, and one way to compete is to lower the costs that
regulation imposes. Environmental standards are an obvious candidate: lax rules make production
cheaper, and countries that compete hard for exports may tolerate pollution that richer or less
trade-dependent countries would not. The argument predicts that greater openness to trade will be
associated with weaker environmental governance.

The alternative was named by David Vogel [@vogel1995].

::: {.definition}
[**California effect**]{.term} — the tendency of trade to spread *stricter* rules from strict to
less strict jurisdictions. When a large, rich market imposes demanding standards on the products
sold in it, producers elsewhere who wish to sell there adopt those standards, and may then press
their own governments to adopt them too. Trade becomes a mechanism of "trading up".
:::

The name comes from American environmental history. California, with severe air-quality problems and
a large car market, set vehicle emission standards stricter than federal ones; manufacturers that
wanted to sell in California built cars to meet them, and other states followed. Vogel contrasted it
with the **Delaware effect** — the race to the bottom in corporate chartering law, in which American
states competed to attract company registrations by offering laxer rules, and Delaware won. The two
effects are not contradictory. They describe different regulatory targets. Where the rule governs a
*product* that must cross a border to be sold, the importing market can impose it, and trade tends
to spread strict rules upward. Where the rule governs a *process* or a *legal form* that can be
relocated, jurisdictions compete for the mobile target, and trade can push rules down.

Environmental management standards sit in an interesting middle position. ISO 14001 governs a
process — how a plant manages its environmental impacts — and not a product. No importing government
can require it at the border. But buyers can, and that is where the question becomes empirical.

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · Rio and Geneva, 1992–1996]{.archive-label}

ISO 14001 was born of a particular moment. In the run-up to the UN Conference on Environment and
Development in Rio de Janeiro in 1992, business leaders organised to present a constructive business
response to global environmental concerns, and international standardisation was one of the avenues
they explored. ISO and the IEC set up an advisory group on the environment to consider whether
international standards for environmental management were needed.

They were not starting from nothing. The British Standards Institution had published BS 7750, a
national standard for environmental management systems, in 1992, building on the management-system
model that the ISO 9000 quality standards, first published in 1987, had made familiar to firms
worldwide. In 1993 the European Union adopted its own Eco-Management and Audit Scheme. ISO created a
new technical committee for environmental management, TC 207, in 1993, and in 1996 it published ISO
14001.

The standard reflected its parentage. Like ISO 9001, it specified a management system rather than a
performance level: a certified firm commits to comply with applicable law and to improve
continually, but the standard sets no emission limit of its own. That design made it adoptable
anywhere, by firms in any sector and any regulatory environment — and it made it, from the first,
vulnerable to the charge that certification and performance could diverge. It was revised in 2004
and again in 2015, and it became the most widely adopted environmental management standard in the
world.

The episode illustrates Mattli and Büthe's argument in miniature. The international standard grew
out of a European national standard and a European regulatory scheme, drafted in a committee where
coordinated national bodies with a working model to propose were well placed to shape it. Firms in
countries with that model already in place adopted the international standard more easily — a
pattern documented by @delmas2002, who traced how differences in institutional environments made
adoption faster in Europe than in the United States.
:::

Aseem Prakash and Matthew Potoski put the race-to-the-bottom hypothesis to a direct test
[@prakash2006]. ISO 14001 is a useful instrument because it goes beyond what many domestic laws
require, is adopted voluntarily, and is counted, country by country, every year. Using a panel of
108 countries over seven years, they asked whether a country's adoption of ISO 14001 depends on
adoption in its **export markets** — the countries to which it sells.

Their finding is that trade linkages **encourage** ISO 14001 adoption **when a country's major
export markets have themselves adopted it**. Exporters to markets where certification is widespread
certify more; exporters to markets where it is rare do not. Trade, on this evidence, does not drive
environmental governance down. It transmits the standards of customers to their suppliers — a
California effect operating through private rather than public regulation, with buyers rather than
governments doing the imposing. The argument was developed at book length in @prakash2006b, which
treats voluntary programmes like ISO 14001 as "green clubs" whose value to members depends on the
credibility of the club's standards and the visibility of membership to outsiders.

The mechanism is consistent with earlier work on the diffusion of quality certification. @guler2002
showed that ISO 9000 certificates spread across countries through trade and investment ties with
countries where certification was already common, alongside the influence of states and of imitation
among firms. @christmann2001 found, among firms in China, that environmental self-regulation was
more likely where firms were linked to multinational owners or exported to developed-country
customers. Across these studies the picture is consistent: private standards travel along supply
chains, from buyer to supplier, and the supply chains themselves determine where they go.

For the firm, the conclusion is one of the most practical in the course. A private standard can
become a de facto condition of access to a market without a single law changing. Monitoring
"regulatory" risk therefore means monitoring not only what governments legislate but what one's
customers' home markets adopt.

## Turning a certificate into data

The practice round rebuilds the two figures that carry Prakash and Potoski's argument: the diffusion
of ISO 14001 across countries over time, and each country's adoption against the adoption level of
its export markets. Every step of the construction involves a choice that affects what the figure
shows.

The data on certification come from **the ISO Survey of Management System Standard Certifications**,
which counts valid certificates by country and year for the main management-system standards. Two
features of the survey should temper any reading of it. It counts *certificates* issued by
certification bodies, not firms or sites, and its coverage of certification bodies has changed
across editions, so that a sudden jump in one country in one year may reflect a change in reporting
rather than a wave of adoption. And from 2025 it is compiled from the IAF CertSearch database, a
change of method that belongs in a provenance log. The 2015 revision of ISO 14001 adds a further
wrinkle: as certificates to the old version expired and were reissued, transition years can show
dips that have nothing to do with firms abandoning the standard.

A count of certificates is not an adoption rate. A large economy has more certificates because it
has more firms, and a map of raw counts is mostly a map of economic size. The practice round
therefore **normalises** by GDP, expressing adoption as certificates per billion US dollars of
output. That is one reasonable choice among several — per firm, per unit of manufacturing output,
per unit of exports would each tell a somewhat different story — and the scaling Prakash and Potoski
themselves use should be checked against the paper. If it differs, the difference is logged, not
hidden.

The trade side requires a measure of **export-market adoption** for each country: how much ISO 14001
adoption there is, on average, among the countries it sells to. The natural measure is a weighted
average of partners' adoption rates, with each partner weighted by its share of the country's
exports. Bilateral exports come from the IMF's International Trade in Goods by partner country
[@imf2026], the dataset already used in Session 04. The weighting is the substance of the argument,
not a technical detail. The California effect predicts that what matters is adoption among the
markets a country actually sells to, in proportion to how much it sells to them. An unweighted
average of all partners would give a country's smallest customers as much influence as its largest
and would test a different, much weaker hypothesis.

A scatter of own adoption against export-market adoption can show whether the two move together, not
why; the paper's panel model with controls and the replication's descriptive cloud differ precisely
there, and the final section returns to the gap.

The most instructive check is a **placebo**. ISO 9001, the quality-management standard, is demanded
by buyers everywhere and has no environmental content at all. If adoption of ISO 9001 tracks
export-market adoption of ISO 9001 just as closely as ISO 14001 tracks its own, then the
export-market pull may have little to do with environmental governance and a great deal to do with
the general practice of supply chains demanding certificates of their suppliers. That would not
overturn the finding that trade spreads private standards upward. It would change what the finding
means — from "trade spreads environmental governance" to "trade spreads certification", which is a
different and less encouraging claim.

## What private authority means for the multinational, channel by channel

The five channels through which the course traces geopolitical risk all run through private
authority, and in each the firm is both a rule-taker and a potential rule-maker.

**Market access** is the most direct channel. A certificate required by buyers is a condition of
entry as real as a tariff, and it is set by the buyers and, behind them, by their home markets. When
a large market writes a private standard into law, as the EU did with the common charger, the
condition of access hardens and its reach extends through the Brussels effect to markets far beyond
the one that legislated. A firm that monitors only government regulation will see these changes
late. The practical discipline is to track what one's customers' home markets adopt, both privately
and in law, because the evidence of this chapter says that is where one's own future requirements
will come from.

**Inputs** are where the multinational becomes the imposer rather than the imposed upon. A firm that
requires its suppliers to certify is exercising private authority over them, and the evidence on
decoupling says that the value of that requirement depends on whether the firm monitors behind the
certificate. A supplier's ISO 14001 certificate is evidence that a management system exists. It is
not, by itself, evidence that the supplier pollutes less, and a firm that treats it as such is
exposed when the gap between certificate and practice becomes public.

**Finance** increasingly runs on private standards too — carbon accounting, sustainability reporting
frameworks, credit ratings — each written by a private or hybrid body, each conditioning access to
capital, and several being absorbed into law, which moves them across the triangle and changes who
can change them.

**Assets and people** are exposed through the standards that govern labour and human rights in
supply chains. A plant certified under a scheme that later loses legitimacy is an asset whose value
in the eyes of customers has fallen overnight.

**Data and operations** are where standards have become most explicitly geopolitical.
Telecommunications standards, information-security standards, rules for artificial intelligence and
for cross-border data flows are now contested between blocs. A firm whose products embed one bloc's
standards may find them excluded from the other's procurement, and a firm whose engineers sit on the
committees writing the next generation of a standard is not only shaping its own costs but taking
part, whether it likes it or not, in a contest between states.

Across the five channels, the strategic choice is the same, and it has three options. The firm can
**adopt** a standard written elsewhere, accepting the cost of compliance as the price of access. It
can **shape** the standard, investing in participation in the bodies that write it — the lesson of
Mattli and Büthe is that this investment pays only if the firm and its national body are organised
to use it. Or it can **resist**, backing a competing standard or lobbying against legal adoption — a
bet that the rival standard will win enough users, or that the shadow of the state will not fall.
The choice depends on where the scheme sits in the governance triangle, on whether its authority is
delegated or entrepreneurial, and on whether the firm's own customers are among those who confer it.

## A short review of the literature

::: {.lit}
The study of private authority in international political economy begins with @strange1996, who
argued that authority over economic outcomes had shifted from states to markets and firms, and with
@cutler1999 and @hall2002, who gave the concept its name and extended it from inter-firm regimes to
NGOs, rating agencies and other non-state actors. @abbott2009 organised the resulting landscape into
the governance triangle and introduced the shadow of the state; @green2013 distinguished delegated
from entrepreneurial private authority and traced both in carbon governance; @cashore2002 showed how
non-state, market-driven schemes such as forest certification gain the authority to make rules
through the purchasing decisions of firms and consumers.

The politics of who writes international standards is the subject of @mattli2003, whose argument for
institutional complementarity over technological rationality and raw power was generalised in
@buthe2011 to the private regulation of products and financial reporting. @bradford2020 documented
the unilateral mechanism by which strict EU rules become global through firms' preference for a
single production standard; the EU's common-charger directive [@europeanunion2022] is a recent and
transparent instance of a private specification hardened into law.

On standards as organisation, @brunsson2012 set out the three perspectives — of, by and as
organisation — and the dynamism of standards in practice, drawing on the institutionalist insight of
@meyer1977 that formal structure may be decoupled from activity. Whether ISO 14001 changes behaviour
is addressed by @potoski2005, who find modest emission reductions among certified US facilities, and
by @christmann2006, who show that substantive rather than symbolic implementation depends on
monitoring by customers.

On the race to the bottom, @vogel1995 named the California effect and set it against the Delaware
effect. @prakash2006 provided the cross-national test at the centre of this session, finding that
trade spreads ISO 14001 from export markets to exporters, and @prakash2006b developed the theory of
voluntary programmes as clubs. The diffusion mechanism is corroborated for quality standards by
@guler2002, for Chinese firms by @christmann2001, and for the differing paths of Europe and the
United States by @delmas2002. The through-line is that private rules travel along supply chains, and
that the power to write them lies with those organised to take part.
:::

## What the map will not tell the firm

The practice round produces a map of ISO 14001 adoption that changes as the years advance, small
multiples of its diffusion by region, and a scatter of each country's adoption against the adoption
of its export markets. If the build goes well, the scatter will slope upward, as Prakash and
Potoski's argument predicts. The temptation will be to read that slope as proof that trade spreads
environmental governance. It is evidence of something narrower.

The scatter is a **correlation**, not a test. Countries whose export markets certify heavily are
richer, more industrialised and more embedded in multinational supply chains than others, and any of
those differences could produce the slope on its own; the paper's panel model controls for some of
them, the cloud of points controls for none. Certificates are **not performance**: the evidence on
decoupling says that a certified plant may or may not pollute less than an uncertified one, and the
figure says nothing about which. The data carry the scars of their **collection**:
certification-body coverage changes between survey editions, the 2015 revision produces transition
dips, and the switch to IAF CertSearch is a change of method. And the measure depends on choices —
normalising by GDP rather than by firms or exports, weighting partners by exports rather than
equally — that the comparison step asks you to break deliberately.

What the figures *can* show is whether the broad pattern the paper reports is visible in public data
with transparent choices; how unevenly a private standard has diffused across the world; and,
through the ISO 9001 placebo, whether the pull of export markets is specific to environmental
governance or general to certification. Each of these matters to a firm deciding whether to certify,
whether to require certification of its suppliers, and how much to trust the certificates it
receives.

The question for the catch slide is the course's usual one, applied to private rules. Suppose the
scatter slopes upward exactly as the paper says. What does it *not* license a multinational to
conclude? It does not license the conclusion that a certified supplier is environmentally better
than an uncertified one, that certification caused a country's export success, that a standard
voluntary today will stay voluntary, or that the rules the firm's markets follow were written by
anyone the firm can vote for. The answer you give — one sentence — is a judgement about where
authority lies, and it is the judgement a multinational must make every time it decides whether to
adopt, shape or resist a rule it did not write.
