Course themes The system of international economic order in which multinational firms operate

2.1 The rules a firm does not notice

A multinational firm lives inside a set of rules it rarely thinks about. When a Canadian manufacturer ships components to a plant in Mexico, assembles them, and sells the result in Germany, it assumes a great deal: that the tariff it pays in Germany is the same as its competitors pay; that a dispute over that tariff will be settled by procedure rather than by retaliation; that the dollars it earns can be converted and moved; that the patent on the component will be respected in the country where it is assembled. None of these assumptions is a law of nature. Each was written, by particular states, at particular moments, in their own interest, and each is held in place by something — power, shared interest, or institutions with momentum of their own.

This chapter is about that set of rules: the international economic order. Its question is the one the session poses. Who wrote the rules multinationals play by, and what happens to the rules when the writers’ share of the world shrinks? The answer has three parts. The first is historical: how the post-1945 order was built, in layers, by the United States and its allies. The second is economic, and comes from the session’s big work, Richard Baldwin’s The Great Convergence: how a fall in the cost of moving ideas after about 1990 moved industry and income from the countries that wrote the rules to a handful that did not. The third is political: why that convergence produced a backlash, and what the backlash means for a firm whose supply chain was built on the old assumptions.

2.2 What an order is

The word “order” is used loosely, so it is worth fixing.

International order — a set of rules, institutions and shared expectations governing relations between states, sustained by some distribution of power among them.

International economic order — the part of the international order that governs trade, money and investment: tariff commitments and their exceptions, exchange-rate and capital-account arrangements, the treatment of foreign investors and their property, and the procedures by which disputes about all of these are settled.

Three questions describe any economic order, and they are worth keeping in view for the rest of the course. Who wrote the rules? — which states designed the institutions, and in whose interest. What do the rules say? — how open they are to trade, to capital and to investment, and how disputes are resolved. What holds them in place? — whether it is the power of a dominant state, a common interest among many, or the inertia of institutions that are costly to abandon. The first question is about history, the second about law, and the third about politics. A firm’s exposure to geopolitical risk is largely a matter of the third: rules that are held in place by one state’s power are as durable as that power and that state’s willingness to use it.

The third question has a classic answer in political economy, and a classic rejoinder. Kindleberger (1973), writing about the Great Depression, argued that the interwar collapse of trade and finance happened because no state was both able and willing to stabilise the system — Britain was able no longer, and the United States was not yet willing. An open world economy, on this view, needs a stabiliser: a state that keeps its market open in a slump, lends counter-cyclically, and acts as lender of last resort. Krasner (1976) generalised the point into what became known as hegemonic stability theory: openness is most likely when a single state is economically dominant, because the dominant state gains most from openness and has the means to enforce it. The rejoinder came from Keohane (1984), whose After Hegemony argued that institutions, once established, can sustain cooperation even as the hegemon that created them declines, because they reduce the costs of making and monitoring agreements and states have reasons to keep them. Whether the post-1945 order outlives the relative decline of its authors is the empirical test of that debate, and it is being run now.

Hegemonic stability — the proposition that an open international economy is most likely to be created and maintained when a single dominant state has both the capacity and the interest to supply its rules and absorb the costs of enforcing them.

2.3 The post-1945 order, in four layers

The order that multinationals take for granted was assembled in layers over half a century. It is easier to understand as a stack than as a single design.

From the archive · Bretton Woods, 1944

In July 1944, while the war in Europe and the Pacific was still being fought, delegates of forty-four allied nations met at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. The two dominant figures were John Maynard Keynes for the United Kingdom and Harry Dexter White for the United States Treasury, and the conference largely ratified a compromise their governments had negotiated in advance. The British wanted a system that would protect a debtor country’s freedom to pursue full employment; the Americans, who would be the world’s creditor, wanted a system in which their money would not be used to finance others’ deficits indefinitely.

The conference created the International Monetary Fund, to oversee a system of fixed but adjustable exchange rates and to lend to countries facing temporary balance-of-payments difficulties, and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the core of what became the World Bank. Currencies were pegged to the dollar; the dollar was convertible into gold at a fixed price for foreign official holders. Capital controls were not merely tolerated but expected: the founders had watched short-term capital flows destabilise the interwar economy and did not intend to repeat the experience (Eichengreen 2019).

What is striking, read from the twenty-first century, is how much of the design reflected the balance of power in the room. The United States held most of the world’s monetary gold and was the only major economy undamaged by the war. The institution’s voting shares, its headquarters in Washington, and the central role of the dollar all followed. The rules were multilateral in form and American in their foundations — which is the observation that every later debate about the order starts from.

The second layer was trade. The planned International Trade Organization, which was to have stood beside the Fund and the Bank, was never established: its charter was negotiated but not ratified by the United States. What survived was a provisional agreement, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade of 1947, which turned out to be one of the most durable provisional arrangements in history. The GATT rested on two principles. Most-favoured-nation treatment required each member to extend to all members any tariff concession it gave to one; national treatment required that imported goods, once across the border, be treated no worse than domestic ones. On that basis, successive negotiating rounds cut tariffs on manufactured goods among the industrial countries.

The third layer is not an institution but a bargain, and the political scientist John Ruggie gave it its name.

Embedded liberalism — the post-war compromise in which states committed to multilateral openness in trade while retaining the domestic policy autonomy — macroeconomic management, social insurance, capital controls — needed to make that openness politically sustainable at home (Ruggie 1982).

Ruggie’s point was that the post-war order was not the liberalism of the nineteenth century restored. It was liberalism embedded in a domestic social settlement: governments would open their markets, but they would also cushion their citizens from the dislocations openness caused, and they would keep the instruments to do so. Free trade was managed; capital was controlled; full employment was a government responsibility. The bargain held for roughly a quarter of a century, and its unravelling is the background to most of what follows.

The fourth layer is the neoliberal turn and the creation of the World Trade Organization. The monetary pillar of Bretton Woods broke first.

From the archive · The end of gold convertibility, 1971

On 15 August 1971, President Nixon announced that the United States would suspend the convertibility of the dollar into gold for foreign official holders, and imposed a temporary surcharge on imports. The announcement was made without consulting the other members of the IMF. A realignment of exchange rates negotiated at the Smithsonian Institution in December 1971 tried to restore a system of pegs; by 1973 the major currencies were floating against one another, and they have floated since (Eichengreen 2019).

The episode is usually told as the end of Bretton Woods, and in its monetary design it was. But it is more instructive as a demonstration of the third question — what holds the rules in place. The system had depended on the United States’ willingness to subordinate its own policy to the gold parity. When that willingness ended, the rule ended, unilaterally, on a Sunday evening. The institutions survived: the IMF found new roles, and the dollar remained the centre of the system, now without the anchor. The order changed form without collapsing, which is the pattern to look for whenever someone announces the end of an order.

Through the 1980s and 1990s, capital controls in the industrial countries were dismantled, and many developing countries followed. In trade, the Uruguay Round of negotiations, launched in 1986, ended with the creation of the World Trade Organization in 1995. The WTO differed from the GATT in two ways that matter for firms. Its rules reached far beyond tariffs on goods — into services, into intellectual property through the TRIPS agreement, into investment measures through TRIMs — so that the order began to govern not only what crossed borders but what happened behind them. And it had a binding dispute-settlement mechanism, with an Appellate Body, so that rulings could no longer be blocked by the losing party. China joined in 2001, after fifteen years of negotiation, on terms that bound it to many of these rules.

How should the result be characterised? Ikenberry (2011) argues that the American-led order was easy to join and hard to overturn: open, rule-based, and institutionalised enough that rising states would gain more by joining it than by challenging it. On this reading, the order is not merely an expression of American power but a set of institutions that bind the hegemon too, and that therefore give others a reason to accept it. Session 03 sets the realist reply beside this view: that institutions reflect the distribution of power and will be revised when it shifts.

2.4 Firms as authors as well as subjects

It would be a mistake to read the order as something states wrote and firms merely obeyed. At several points, firms were among its authors, and the layers that matter most to multinationals today are the ones in which their influence was greatest.

The clearest case is intellectual property. Before the Uruguay Round, patents, copyrights and trademarks were governed by conventions administered outside the trade regime, with weak enforcement and wide latitude for national policy; many developing countries did not grant patents on pharmaceuticals at all. The TRIPS agreement changed that, by making minimum standards of intellectual-property protection a condition of WTO membership, enforceable through the dispute-settlement mechanism. Sell (2003) traces how this happened: a small coalition of chief executives from American pharmaceutical, software and entertainment firms organised in the 1980s, defined the problem as one of trade rather than of intellectual property, persuaded the United States government to adopt their framing, and worked with counterparts in Europe and Japan to build a joint position. Twelve corporations, in her account, effectively drafted the core of a public international law. The episode is the strongest evidence that the post-1945 order was not only American in its foundations but, in some of its later layers, corporate.

A second case is the protection of foreign investment, which the GATT and the WTO largely left aside. Here the instrument was the bilateral investment treaty. The first was signed between West Germany and Pakistan in 1959; the Convention establishing the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes, under World Bank auspices, followed in 1965; and in the 1990s the number of such treaties multiplied. Their distinctive feature is that they allow a foreign investor to bring a claim directly against a host state before an international tribunal, without needing its own government to take up the case. For a multinational, this is the layer of the order that most directly answers Kobrin’s political risk (Session 01): it is the legal response to expropriation and discriminatory treatment. It is also the layer that has attracted the most political contestation in the 2010s, from governments that came to see investor-state arbitration as a constraint on regulation — Rodrik’s trilemma, applied to a single clause.

The point for this course is twofold. First, the rules a firm relies on are not all of a piece: some are multilateral (tariff bindings), some are bilateral (investment treaties), some are private or semi-private (standards, the subject of Session 06). They are held in place by different things and they will fail in different ways. Second, if firms helped write some of the rules, they have a stake — and sometimes a voice — in how they are rewritten. A firm’s government affairs function is, in this sense, part of its risk function: the influence a firm exercises over the rules is one of the few geopolitical exposures it can partly manage from the inside.

2.5 Three costs, three unbundlings

Baldwin’s book offers a different way into the same history, one that starts not from institutions but from costs. Globalisation, he argues, has been shaped by three successive constraints: the cost of moving goods, the cost of moving ideas, and the cost of moving people (Baldwin 2016). Each time one of these costs fell sharply, it “unbundled” something that geography had previously held together.

Unbundling — in Baldwin’s account, the separation, made possible by a fall in a particular cost of distance, of activities that had previously had to take place in the same location: first production from consumption, then the stages of production from one another.

2.5.1 The first unbundling: goods move, ideas stay

From the nineteenth century, steam ships and railways made it cheap to move goods. Production no longer had to happen near consumption: a factory in Manchester could clothe customers in India. But the cost of moving ideas remained high, because coordinating complex production still required people to be close to one another. So industry clustered — in Britain, western Europe, North America, later Japan — where know-how could circulate cheaply within a local area. The result was the great divergence: the industrialising North pulled away from the rest of the world in income, because the knowledge that made factories productive stayed where it was invented. Trade grew, but it was trade in finished goods between nations, and the comparative advantage that governed it was a national property.

2.5.2 The second unbundling: ideas move, inside the firm

From about 1990, information and communication technology made it cheap to move ideas — to coordinate complex activities at a distance. The factory itself could now be unbundled. Stages of production that had to sit together in one place could be dispersed across countries, each stage located where its costs were lowest, and coordinated from headquarters by telecommunications and computers. A firm could combine its own technology, management and marketing with low-wage labour in a nearby developing country — for the United States, Mexico; for Germany, Poland and central Europe; for Japan, the rest of East Asia.

The crucial move in Baldwin’s argument is what travelled with the stages of production. When a Japanese firm moved assembly to Thailand, it did not move only the assembly. It moved the know-how to perform it: the process engineering, the quality systems, the managerial practice. Know-how that had been confined within national borders now flowed across them — but inside the firm’s own production network, under its control. This is why the second unbundling produced convergence rather than divergence. A handful of developing economies, those that joined the supply chains of northern firms, industrialised at a speed previously unknown, because they did not have to invent the know-how; they had it brought to them.

Global value chain — the international fragmentation of a production process into stages located in different countries and coordinated, usually by a lead firm, so that what crosses borders is increasingly intermediate goods, services and know-how rather than finished products.

Baldwin’s empirical picture of the shift emphasises its regional character. Supply-chain trade, Baldwin and Lopez-Gonzalez (2015) show, is organised around a few hubs — “Factory North America”, “Factory Europe”, “Factory Asia” — each pairing high-technology headquarter economies with lower-wage neighbours. The second unbundling was less a globalisation than a set of regionalisations, linked by the headquarters economies.

2.5.3 The third unbundling: people, virtually

The third constraint is the cost of moving people, and specifically of face-to-face interaction. Baldwin’s prospective third unbundling would come from telepresence and telerobotics: technologies that let a worker in one country perform services in another without travelling. If it arrives at scale, competition would reach occupations rather than sectors, and services would be traded as tasks, the way manufacturing stages are now. It is a forecast rather than a finding, and it is the least developed part of the argument.

2.5.4 The multinational as the vehicle

For this course, the key point of the book is a single sentence’s worth of argument: in the second unbundling, the multinational firm is the vehicle of convergence. The convergence of incomes between the old industrial economies and the new ones did not happen through anonymous markets in finished goods. It happened through supply chains that multinational firms built, owned or controlled, along which they moved technology and organisational capability.

That is why geopolitics now reaches firms so directly. If know-how, jobs and capabilities travel along supply chains, then supply chains are where states will intervene when they decide that the movement of know-how threatens their security. The export controls of the 2020s on advanced semiconductors and the equipment to make them are attempts to stop exactly the flow the second unbundling made cheap. The firm’s supply chain has become a geopolitical object, not because firms chose to make it one, but because it carries what states care about.

2.6 The target figure, and what it measures

Baldwin’s evidence for the convergence is a picture: the share of the G7 — Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom and the United States — in world income, which rose for over a century and then fell sharply from around 1990. The share lost by the G7 was gained mostly by a small group of developing economies, which he calls the “Industrialising Six”: China, Korea, India, Poland, Indonesia and Thailand. That figure is the session’s replication target.

The replication uses the World Bank’s World Development Indicators, and building it forces three decisions, each of which matters for interpretation.

The first is that the G7 is not in the data. The World Bank publishes the world total and many aggregates — income groups, regions, the euro area — but no G7. You must construct it by summing the seven members, and you must do so by their ISO codes rather than their names, because names change between releases and between datasets and codes are designed not to. The G7 is a political grouping, and any measure of it is a construction by the analyst.

The second is the unit of account. The target figure uses GDP in current US dollars at market exchange rates. That is the natural measure if the question is purchasing power on world markets — what an economy’s output can buy abroad, how large it looks to an exporter selling in dollars. But it means that the share moves with the dollar. When the dollar strengthens, every other economy shrinks in dollar terms and the US share rises, without any change in what anyone produces. Part of any year-to-year movement in the series is the exchange rate.

Purchasing-power parity (PPP) — a conversion of national output into a common unit using the relative prices of a common basket of goods and services rather than market exchange rates, so that an economy’s output is valued at what it would cost in a reference country.

The alternative is GDP at purchasing-power parity. Because many goods and services — especially non-traded services — are cheaper in lower-income economies, converting their output at market exchange rates understates its real volume. At PPP, the economies of China and India are much larger relative to the G7 than they are at market rates, and the G7’s share is correspondingly smaller. The World Development Indicators’ PPP series begins in 1990, so the comparison covers only the convergence period.

Which measure is right depends on the decision, which is the point the lecture puts to the room. If a board is deciding where the purchasing power for its exported product sits — how many dollars or euros a market can pay — market exchange rates are the relevant measure. If it is deciding where real production capacity and local markets for locally made goods are, PPP is closer. A firm that shows its board only one of the two has made a strategic choice without stating it. Measurement choices are strategy choices.

The third decision is about who is in the denominator. The world total is a World Bank aggregate that includes economies for which data are sparse or estimated, particularly in the early decades. Some members of the Industrialising Six lack values for parts of the 1970s and 1980s. A stacked chart of shares — G7, I6, rest of the world — will therefore show the “rest of the world” absorbing whatever is missing from the I6, which is an artefact of the data, not a fact about the world. The practice round asks you to find it and record it.

2.7 From convergence to backlash

The convergence was, by most measures of global welfare, a remarkable success: incomes in the economies that joined supply chains rose at a pace without historical precedent, within a single generation. It was also politically destabilising in the countries whose share was shrinking, and the most useful framework for understanding why comes from Dani Rodrik.

Rodrik’s trilemma — the proposition that a country can have at most two of three things: deep economic integration with the world economy, national sovereignty in policy-making, and democratic politics (Rodrik 2011).

The logic is that deep integration requires harmonised rules — on products, on investment, on capital flows — and those rules constrain what national governments can do. If the nation-state keeps its sovereignty and pursues deep integration, democratic choice must be constrained: governments adopt whatever policies markets reward, a condition Rodrik describes with Thomas Friedman’s phrase, the “golden straitjacket”. If integration and democracy are both kept, the rules must be made democratically above the state — a global federalism no one has built. If sovereignty and democracy are kept, integration must be shallow: the Bretton Woods compromise, with managed openness and room for national policy.

The trilemma is a reading of the history in the previous sections. Embedded liberalism was the third option: shallow integration that left room for democracy and sovereignty. The neoliberal turn and the WTO pushed towards deep integration — Rodrik calls it hyperglobalisation — and, for a while, the countries concerned accepted the straitjacket. The backlash of the 2010s and 2020s — Brexit, the tariff wars that began in 2018, the return of industrial policy, export controls, investment screening, “de-risking” — can be read as a sequence of choices of sovereignty and democracy over deep integration.

The economics behind the politics became clearer with evidence on the distribution of the gains. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson (2013) showed that US local labour markets more exposed to rising Chinese import competition after 2000 suffered larger falls in manufacturing employment and wages, and higher take-up of public transfers, with effects that persisted. Trade theory had always predicted losers as well as winners; what the “China shock” literature showed was that the losers were geographically concentrated, that adjustment was slow, and that the compensation the embedded-liberal bargain had promised did not reach them. A bargain that is not honoured stops being a bargain.

2.7.1 Slowbalisation

The phenomenon that the press calls slowbalisation is visible in a single series: world trade as a percentage of world GDP. It rose steeply from the early 1990s to 2008, fell in the financial crisis, and has broadly flattened since. Whether the flattening is a retreat from globalisation or a change in its form is contested. A retreat would show up as firms reshoring and supply chains shortening. A change of form would show up as trade in goods stagnating while trade in services and cross-border data flows — much of which is poorly measured in trade statistics — continue to grow. The practice round’s extend step asks you to plot the series and judge it; the honest answer is that the series alone cannot settle the question, because its denominator, GDP, has shifted towards services, which are traded less, in exactly the period in question.

2.7.2 The dispute mechanism

One institutional fact belongs in every firm’s risk register. The WTO’s Appellate Body has been unable to hear appeals since December 2019, when the terms of its members expired and the United States blocked the appointment of replacements. A panel ruling can now be appealed “into the void”, where no body exists to hear it, and so never become binding. The dispute-settlement mechanism that distinguished the WTO from the GATT therefore functions only partially. For a firm, that means that a discriminatory measure by a trading partner is now more likely to be resolved by bilateral bargaining, retaliation or negotiation between governments than by a legal ruling — that is, by power rather than by procedure.

2.8 Interdependence as an instrument

The first two parts of this chapter describe an order designed to make interdependence safe. The third must add that interdependence was never only an economic fact. Albert Hirschman, writing in 1945 about Nazi Germany’s trade with south-eastern Europe, showed that trade relations could be structured deliberately to create dependence: by trading with small partners for whom the trade was a large share of their total, a great power could make the threat of interrupting it a source of political influence (Hirschman 1945). The gains from trade, in his account, were also the measure of the power the trade conferred — the cost a partner would bear if it stopped.

Farrell and Newman (2019) brought the argument into the age of networks. The infrastructure of globalisation — financial messaging, dollar clearing, internet backbones, cloud platforms — is not flat; it has hubs, and the hubs sit disproportionately in a few jurisdictions. States that control the hubs can use them for surveillance, observing what flows through, and for exclusion, cutting adversaries off. Interdependence, on this reading, has been “weaponised”: the very networks that the economic order built become instruments of coercion. Sessions 08, 10 and 11 develop the argument for cyber, sanctions and space. Its relevance here is that the order’s openness and its vulnerability are the same thing seen from two sides.

For a multinational, the practical form of weaponised interdependence is jurisdictional reach. A firm that clears payments in dollars, stores data with a provider headquartered in one country, or uses components containing technology controlled by another, has placed part of its operations under that country’s law, wherever the firm itself is incorporated and wherever the transaction takes place. The order built after 1945 made it cheap and natural to route business through American banks, American platforms and American technology; the same routing now carries American regulatory authority with it, and other states — China, the European Union — have begun to build their own instruments of reach in response. A firm’s exposure to the rules of the international economic order is therefore not only a matter of where it sells and produces. It is also a matter of which networks it runs through, and whose law travels along them. That is a question few firms asked when the networks were built, because the order was designed to make it unnecessary.

2.9 What it means for the multinational

The history and the economics lead to four implications that a firm’s strategy team can act on.

Your market is no longer where the rules are written. For most of the post-war period, the largest markets and the rule-makers were the same countries. The convergence separated them. A European firm now earns a growing share of its revenue in markets whose governments did not design the WTO, did not participate in Bretton Woods, and do not feel bound by the spirit — as distinct from the letter — of rules they joined late. The firm’s legal environment and its commercial environment have come apart.

Your supply chain is a geopolitical object. If the second unbundling moved know-how along supply chains, then supply chains are where governments now intervene to stop it: export controls, outbound investment screening, restrictions on technology transfer, requirements to locate certain stages of production in trusted jurisdictions. The question for the firm is no longer only where a stage is cheapest, but where it is permitted, and by whom.

Multilateral protection is weaker. With the Appellate Body unable to hear appeals, the guarantee that a trading partner’s discriminatory measure could be challenged and removed through procedure has weakened. Firms that relied implicitly on that guarantee — exporters into markets with a history of trade remedies, for example — face disputes settled more often by negotiation and retaliation. The firm’s government’s bargaining power has become part of the firm’s risk profile.

Measurement choices are strategy choices. At market exchange rates, the G7 still looks large; at PPP, it looks much smaller. Which figure a board sees will shape where it builds, where it sells, and how it assesses the bargaining position of the governments it deals with. A risk analyst’s first duty is to show both and say which question each answers.

Mapped onto the five channels of this course, the order’s transformation runs mainly through market access — the tariffs, trade remedies and bloc-based diversion of Session 04 — and through inputs, where the know-how and components that the second unbundling dispersed are now subject to control. But it reaches finance and payments too, because the dollar-centred monetary system that survived 1971 is the platform on which financial sanctions operate.

2.10 What the figure does not license

The target figure is a powerful image, and its power is a reason for care. It does not license four conclusions that boards and commentators routinely draw from it.

A falling G7 share is relative. The G7 economies kept growing in absolute terms through the whole period; the denominator grew faster. A falling share does not mean the G7 market shrank, and a firm selling into the G7 did not lose customers because the line went down.

At current US dollars, the share moves with the dollar. Part of any year-to-year change is the exchange rate, not production. A strong dollar flatters the United States and with it the G7, and a reading in any single year can say as much about currency markets as about convergence.

Convergence is concentrated. Most of the gain went to a handful of economies, and above all to China. “The South” did not converge; specific economies that joined specific supply chains did. A firm that reads the figure as a story about emerging markets in general will over-invest in the markets that did not participate.

It shows income, not rule-making power. Whether a shrinking share of world GDP means a shrinking ability to set the rules is a question about the relation between economic size and political influence — the subject of Session 03. Hegemonic stability theory predicts that it does; institutionalists predict that rules can outlast the share. The chart cannot settle the dispute, because it does not measure the thing in dispute.

2.11 A short review of the literature

The political economy of the post-war order begins with the hegemonic-stability debate. Kindleberger (1973) attributed the interwar collapse to the absence of a stabilising power; Krasner (1976) generalised the argument to the structure of trade; Keohane (1984) replied that institutions can sustain cooperation after hegemony. Ruggie (1982) named the domestic bargain — embedded liberalism — on which post-war openness rested, and Ikenberry (2011) reinterpreted the American-led order as liberal, institutional and self-binding, and therefore “easy to join and hard to overturn”. The monetary history, from Bretton Woods through the end of gold convertibility, is told by Eichengreen (2019).

The economics of the new globalisation is organised by Baldwin (2016) around falling costs of moving goods, ideas and people, with the multinational’s supply chain as the conduit for the second unbundling; Baldwin and Lopez-Gonzalez (2015) map the regional structure of supply-chain trade. The distributional consequences in the rich countries are documented most influentially by Autor, Dorn, and Hanson (2013), and their political consequences are read through Rodrik (2011)’s trilemma of integration, sovereignty and democracy.

A third strand treats economic interdependence as a source of power rather than only of gains. Hirschman (1945) showed how asymmetric trade could be designed to create political dependence; Farrell and Newman (2019) extended the argument to the networked infrastructure of finance and communications, introducing “weaponised interdependence” — the strand that Sessions 08, 10 and 11 follow.

The through-line is that every account of the order answers the same question — what holds the rules in place — and that the answers differ chiefly in how much weight they give to the distribution of power. The great convergence is the event that puts that weight to the test.

2.12 Into the practice round

The practice round rebuilds Baldwin’s figure from the World Bank’s data: the G7’s share of world GDP in current dollars, from 1970 to the latest year, followed by a stacked picture of who gained. The steps are in the brief that follows. What this chapter adds is a set of questions to carry into it.

When you construct the G7, remember that you are building a political aggregate that the statistics do not contain, and that you build it by code rather than by name. When you find the peak and the latest value, ask how much of the movement between any two years is the dollar. When you switch to PPP, notice that the series starts in 1990 — just as the convergence begins — and ask which board decision each line informs. When the Industrialising Six have missing values in the early decades, record what that does to the “rest of the world”, because that is your first place where the data disagree with the book. And when you plot world trade as a share of GDP, ask whether the flattening after 2008 is a retreat or a change of form, and what evidence would tell the two apart.

Then write the catch, about your team’s firm rather than about Baldwin. A falling G7 share does not tell a multinational that its G7 customers are poorer, that the emerging markets in general are richer, or that the rules it relies on have already changed. It tells it that the economies that wrote the rules now produce a smaller share of the world’s income than when they wrote them — and that whether the rules follow the income is the question the next session takes up.

2.13 In the practice

The target. Baldwin (2016): the G7 share of world GDP, rising for a century and falling sharply after about 1990. You rebuild it from 1970 with World Bank data, then ask what changes when the dollars change.

2.13.1 0 · Form your team — 10 min

Teams of three or four, for the rest of the term. Agree on one multinational from your shortlists and register it on ZoneCours (first come, first served). Write the team name and firm at the top of your provenance log.

2.13.2 1 · Collect — 10 min

If you did the pre-session, your DataBank extract is in data/raw/. If not, build it now — ../data/README.md. One extract per team is enough; everyone builds their own workbook from it.

Copy templates/provenance-log.csv into data/ and fill one line per file. For WDI, the version is the “Last Updated” date DataBank shows for the database.

2.13.3 2 · Prepare — 20 min

  1. Connect → Microsoft Excel → your extract. Drag the data sheet to the canvas.
  2. If there are notes rows at the bottom (“Data from database…”, “Last Updated…”), tick Use Data Interpreter, or filter them out later. Write which you did.
  3. Pivot the years. Click the first year column, shift-click the last → right-click → Pivot. Two new columns appear.
  4. Rename Pivot Field Names → Year, Pivot Field Values → Value.
  5. Year is text like 1990 [YR1990]. Create a calculated field from the data source pane: Year (n) = INT(LEFT([Year], 4)). (If your extract has plain 1990, just change the type to Number (whole).)
  6. Value is text — DataBank writes missing data as ... Change its type to Number (decimal); the dots become nulls. Check the null count: is it plausible?
  7. Look at Country Code and Series Code (in the Route B CSV the second is Indicator Code, the years are plain 1990, and missing values are blank). From now on you filter on codes, never on names.

2.13.4 3 · Replicate — 25 min

2.13.4.1 Sheet 1 · “The G7 share of world GDP”

  1. Filters: Series Code = NY.GDP.MKTP.CD only.

  2. Create two calculated fields:

    G7 GDP     = IF [Country Code] IN ("CAN","FRA","DEU","ITA","JPN","GBR","USA") THEN [Value] END
World GDP  = IF [Country Code] = "WLD" THEN [Value] END

  3. And the share:

    G7 share   = SUM([G7 GDP]) / SUM([World GDP])

    Format it as a percentage (right-click → Default Properties → Number Format).

  4. Year (n) → Columns as a continuous dimension (green); G7 share → Rows.

  5. Annotate the peak year and the latest year with their values. Title the sheet with the finding, not the variable. Caption: World Bank, WDI, NY.GDP.MKTP.CD, last updated …, accessed ….

2.13.4.2 Sheet 2 · “Who gained?” — a stacked area of world shares

  1. Two more calculated fields:

    I6 GDP              = IF [Country Code] IN ("CHN","KOR","IND","POL","IDN","THA") THEN [Value] END
Rest of world GDP   = SUM([World GDP]) - SUM([G7 GDP]) - SUM([I6 GDP])

  2. Year (n) → Columns. Drag G7 GDP, I6 GDP and Rest of world GDP onto Rows as Measure Values (drop the second and third onto the first axis).

  3. Marks → Area. Measure Names → Colour.

  4. Right-click Measure Values → Quick Table Calculation → Percent of Total. Then Edit Table Calculation → Compute Using → Measure Names, so that the three shares sum to 100% within each year — not across years. Check one year by hand against Sheet 1.

2.13.5 4 · Compare and break — 15 min

  1. Compare. Put Sheet 1 beside Baldwin’s figure. Where does the curve peak in yours? How far has it fallen by Baldwin’s last year, and by the latest year? Write down one difference and its most likely cause (dollars, years, data revision).
  2. Missing data. Which I6 members have no GDP value in the 1970s or 1980s? What does that do to the I6 and “rest of world” areas in those years? This is your “the data disagreed” entry.
  3. Break — change the dollars. Duplicate Sheet 1 and switch the filter to NY.GDP.MKTP.PP.CD. The PPP series starts in 1990. How much smaller is the G7 at PPP? Which board decision would you use each line for?
  4. Extend — slowbalisation. New sheet: NE.TRD.GNFS.ZS for WLD — world trade in % of GDP (an index, so check the aggregation: there is one value per year; AVG or SUM give the same only because you filtered to one country — say so). Annotate 2008. Is the line after 2008 a retreat?

2.13.6 5 · The catch — 10 min

One slide (a dashboard exported as an image):

Original Ours The catch
Baldwin’s G7 share figure Your Sheet 1, plus the PPP line One sentence: what this figure does not license your team’s multinational to conclude

A member drawn at random presents in two minutes.

2.13.7 Hand in

On ZoneCours before leaving, one per team: the .twbx (File → Export Packaged Workbook), the provenance log, and the slide. Formative: commented, not marked; counts towards participation.

The one rule: your log records at least one place where the data disagreed with the book.

2.13.8 What loses credit

  • Filtering on country names (“Korea, Rep.” will bite you in Session 04)
  • A percent of total that sums to 100% across years instead of within each year
  • A share chart in current dollars with no mention of exchange rates
  • A catch about Baldwin’s book rather than about what your firm may conclude
