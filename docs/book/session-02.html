2.1.1 1. The reading — 60–75 min

Read the Introduction and Part I (the long history of globalisation in four phases) and Part II (the new globalisation and the “three cascading constraints”). Skim Part IV on policy. The book is on reserve at the HEC library; the e-book is available through the library catalogue.

Annotate four things:

What to look for
1 Baldwin’s three costs of moving goods, ideas and people — and which one fell in each unbundling
2 Why the first unbundling produced divergence and the second convergence
3 The figure showing the G7 share of world income — what is on the axis, what is measured
4 One claim a G7 multinational’s strategy team would contest, and why

The target figure is the G7 share of world GDP. Note its units (current US dollars? constant? PPP?), its start and end years, and its source. You will need all four.

2.2 The lecture

The session opens with four Risk Briefs (20 minutes); the lecture takes the next seventy.

2.2.1 1. What an “economic order” is

An international order is a set of rules, institutions and expectations that governs relations between states, backed by some distribution of power. An economic order is the part of it that governs trade, money and investment — the part a multinational lives inside without noticing, until it changes.

Three questions describe any economic order:

Question What to look at
Who wrote the rules? Which states designed the institutions, and in whose interest
What do the rules say? Openness to trade, to capital, to investment; how disputes are settled
What holds them in place? Hegemonic power, shared interest, institutions with their own momentum

2.2.2 2. The post-1945 order, in four layers

Bretton Woods (1944). Forty-four allied states create the International Monetary Fund (stable exchange rates, balance-of-payments support) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the core of the World Bank. Fixed but adjustable exchange rates anchored on the dollar, the dollar on gold.

The GATT (1947). A provisional agreement — the planned International Trade Organization never came into being — that cuts tariffs in successive rounds on the principle of most-favoured-nation treatment and national treatment.

Embedded liberalism. Ruggie (1982) named the bargain: openness abroad, made politically sustainable by social protection and macroeconomic autonomy at home. Capital controls were normal; free trade was managed.

The neoliberal turn and the WTO (1995). The dollar’s link to gold ends in 1971; capital controls are dismantled through the 1980s and 1990s; the Uruguay Round creates the World Trade Organization, with a binding dispute-settlement mechanism and rules reaching into services, intellectual property (TRIPS) and investment measures (TRIMs). China joins in 2001.

The liberal order as a political project. Ikenberry (2011, Liberal Leviathan) argues the order was American-led but easy to join and hard to overturn: open, rule-based, institutional enough that rising states gain more by joining than by challenging it. Session 03 sets the realist reply beside it.

2.2.3 3. Baldwin’s three unbundlings

Baldwin organises globalisation around three costs: of moving goods, moving ideas, and moving people.

Phase What became cheap What it unbundled Consequence
First unbundling (steam, from the 19th century) moving goods production from consumption: you could make things far from where they were bought Industry clustered in the North, where ideas stayed cheap to move locally. Great divergence between North and South
Second unbundling (ICT, from about 1990) moving ideas the factory itself: stages of production could be offshored while coordinated from headquarters Northern know-how moved South inside the multinational’s value chain. Great convergence
Third unbundling (prospective) moving people — virtually: telepresence, telerobotics the worker from the workplace Services trade in tasks; competition reaches occupations, not only sectors

The key point for this course. In the second unbundling, the multinational is the vehicle of convergence. Know-how is transferred through supply chains the firm controls. That is why geopolitics now reaches firms so directly: the supply chain is where technology, jobs and security dependence travel together.

Baldwin’s evidence is a picture: the G7’s share of world income, rising for over a century, then falling sharply after about 1990 — while a handful of developing economies (his “Industrialising Six”: China, Korea, India, Poland, Indonesia, Thailand) gain almost all of it. That is today’s target figure.

2.2.4 4. From convergence to backlash

Rodrik’s trilemma (Rodrik, 2011, The Globalization Paradox): a country can have at most two of deep economic integration, national sovereignty and democratic politics.

Choose Give up Looks like
integration + sovereignty democracy the “golden straitjacket”: policies dictated by markets
integration + democracy sovereignty global federalism: rules made above the state
sovereignty + democracy deep integration Bretton Woods compromise: shallow, managed openness

The trilemma reads the 2010s and 2020s: Brexit, tariff wars, industrial policy, export controls, and “de-risking” are choices of sovereignty and democracy over integration.

Slowbalisation. World trade in percent of GDP rose steeply from the 1990s to 2008 and has broadly flattened since. Whether that is a retreat from globalisation or a shift in its form — from goods to services and data — is contested. The extend step of the practice round lets you see the flattening and judge it.

When the writers’ share shrinks. If the G7 produced around two-thirds of world GDP when it wrote the rules, and much less today, two questions follow — and they open Sessions 03 and 04: will the rules be rewritten by the new powers (power transition), and will the world split into blocs with rules of their own (fragmentation)?

2.2.5 5. What it means for the multinational

Implication Example
Your market is no longer where the rules are written. A European firm earns a growing share of revenue in markets whose governments did not design the WTO and do not feel bound by its spirit
Your supply chain is a geopolitical object. The know-how the second unbundling moved is what export controls now try to stop
Multilateral protection is weaker. The WTO’s Appellate Body has been unable to hear appeals since December 2019; disputes are settled by power more often than by panels
Measurement choices are strategy choices. At market exchange rates, the G7 still looks large; at PPP, it looks much smaller. Which one a board sees shapes where it builds

2.2.6 6. What the figure does not license

  • A falling G7 share is relative: G7 economies kept growing. It does not mean the G7 market shrank.
  • At current US dollars, the share moves with the dollar: part of any year-to-year change is the exchange rate, not production.
  • Convergence is concentrated. Most of the gain goes to a handful of countries — above all China. “The South” did not converge.
  • It shows income, not rule-making power. Whether shrinking share means shrinking influence is the question of Session 03, not a reading of this chart.

2.2.7 7. The Tableau skill of the day

DataBank gives you years as columns. Tableau wants one row per country-year. Select the year columns in the data source pane → right-click → Pivot. Rename Pivot Field Names to Year and Pivot Field Values to Value. Change Year to a number or date and Value to a number — DataBank’s .. become nulls. Then build the G7 share with a calculated field, and check it with a percent of total table calculation. Full click-path: ../02-practice/README.md.

2.3 In the practice

The target. Baldwin (2016): the G7 share of world GDP, rising for a century and falling sharply after about 1990. You rebuild it from 1970 with World Bank data, then ask what changes when the dollars change.

2.3.1 0 · Form your team — 10 min

Teams of three or four, for the rest of the term. Agree on one multinational from your shortlists and register it on ZoneCours (first come, first served). Write the team name and firm at the top of your provenance log.

2.3.2 1 · Collect — 10 min

If you did the pre-session, your DataBank extract is in data/raw/. If not, build it now — ../data/README.md. One extract per team is enough; everyone builds their own workbook from it.

Copy templates/provenance-log.csv into data/ and fill one line per file. For WDI, the version is the “Last Updated” date DataBank shows for the database.

2.3.3 2 · Prepare — 20 min

  1. Connect → Microsoft Excel → your extract. Drag the data sheet to the canvas.
  2. If there are notes rows at the bottom (“Data from database…”, “Last Updated…”), tick Use Data Interpreter, or filter them out later. Write which you did.
  3. Pivot the years. Click the first year column, shift-click the last → right-click → Pivot. Two new columns appear.
  4. Rename Pivot Field Names → Year, Pivot Field Values → Value.
  5. Year is text like 1990 [YR1990]. Create a calculated field from the data source pane: Year (n) = INT(LEFT([Year], 4)). (If your extract has plain 1990, just change the type to Number (whole).)
  6. Value is text — DataBank writes missing data as ... Change its type to Number (decimal); the dots become nulls. Check the null count: is it plausible?
  7. Look at Country Code and Series Code (in the Route B CSV the second is Indicator Code, the years are plain 1990, and missing values are blank). From now on you filter on codes, never on names.

2.3.4 3 · Replicate — 25 min

2.3.4.1 Sheet 1 · “The G7 share of world GDP”

  1. Filters: Series Code = NY.GDP.MKTP.CD only.

  2. Create two calculated fields:

    G7 GDP     = IF [Country Code] IN ("CAN","FRA","DEU","ITA","JPN","GBR","USA") THEN [Value] END
World GDP  = IF [Country Code] = "WLD" THEN [Value] END

  3. And the share:

    G7 share   = SUM([G7 GDP]) / SUM([World GDP])

    Format it as a percentage (right-click → Default Properties → Number Format).

  4. Year (n) → Columns as a continuous dimension (green); G7 share → Rows.

  5. Annotate the peak year and the latest year with their values. Title the sheet with the finding, not the variable. Caption: World Bank, WDI, NY.GDP.MKTP.CD, last updated …, accessed ….

2.3.4.2 Sheet 2 · “Who gained?” — a stacked area of world shares

  1. Two more calculated fields:

    I6 GDP              = IF [Country Code] IN ("CHN","KOR","IND","POL","IDN","THA") THEN [Value] END
Rest of world GDP   = SUM([World GDP]) - SUM([G7 GDP]) - SUM([I6 GDP])

  2. Year (n) → Columns. Drag G7 GDP, I6 GDP and Rest of world GDP onto Rows as Measure Values (drop the second and third onto the first axis).

  3. Marks → Area. Measure Names → Colour.

  4. Right-click Measure Values → Quick Table Calculation → Percent of Total. Then Edit Table Calculation → Compute Using → Measure Names, so that the three shares sum to 100% within each year — not across years. Check one year by hand against Sheet 1.

2.3.5 4 · Compare and break — 15 min

  1. Compare. Put Sheet 1 beside Baldwin’s figure. Where does the curve peak in yours? How far has it fallen by Baldwin’s last year, and by the latest year? Write down one difference and its most likely cause (dollars, years, data revision).
  2. Missing data. Which I6 members have no GDP value in the 1970s or 1980s? What does that do to the I6 and “rest of world” areas in those years? This is your “the data disagreed” entry.
  3. Break — change the dollars. Duplicate Sheet 1 and switch the filter to NY.GDP.MKTP.PP.CD. The PPP series starts in 1990. How much smaller is the G7 at PPP? Which board decision would you use each line for?
  4. Extend — slowbalisation. New sheet: NE.TRD.GNFS.ZS for WLD — world trade in % of GDP (an index, so check the aggregation: there is one value per year; AVG or SUM give the same only because you filtered to one country — say so). Annotate 2008. Is the line after 2008 a retreat?

2.3.6 5 · The catch — 10 min

One slide (a dashboard exported as an image):

Original Ours The catch
Baldwin’s G7 share figure Your Sheet 1, plus the PPP line One sentence: what this figure does not license your team’s multinational to conclude

A member drawn at random presents in two minutes.

2.3.7 Hand in

On ZoneCours before leaving, one per team: the .twbx (File → Export Packaged Workbook), the provenance log, and the slide. Formative: commented, not marked; counts towards participation.

The one rule: your log records at least one place where the data disagreed with the book.

2.3.8 What loses credit

  • Filtering on country names (“Korea, Rep.” will bite you in Session 04)
  • A percent of total that sums to 100% across years instead of within each year
  • A share chart in current dollars with no mention of exchange rates
  • A catch about Baldwin’s book rather than about what your firm may conclude
