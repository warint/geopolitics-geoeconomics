7.1.3 3. Your corpus, and coding it — 45 min

One member retrieves the headlines (GDELT query in ../data/README.md), draws 40 at random, and shares the coding sheet. Two other members code all 40 — independently, without talking to each other — using the protocol below. The fourth member (or the first, in a team of three) is the adjudicator in class.

7.1.3.1 The coding protocol

Code the headline only. Assign one code: the category that best describes the headline’s main event. Categories follow Caldara and Iacoviello’s eight, plus a zero.

Code Category Assign when the headline reports…
0 Not geopolitical anything else — domestic politics, markets, company news, disasters
1 War threats a threat or risk of war between states
2 Peace threats a threat to a peace process, ceasefire or agreement
3 Military build-ups troop movements, exercises, arms build-ups, new deployments
4 Nuclear threats nuclear tests, weapons programmes, nuclear threats
5 Terror threats a threat or risk of a terrorist attack
6 Beginning of war the start of a war or armed conflict between states
7 Escalation of war an existing war intensifying
8 Terror acts a terrorist attack that happened

Decision rules.

  1. Acts beat threats: if a headline reports both, code the act (6, 7 or 8).
  2. Sanctions, export controls and trade wars are not in the eight categories: code 0, and write econ in a comment column. (That gap is one of today’s discussion points.)
  3. When in doubt, code 0 and write why in the comment column.
  4. Never revise your code after seeing your partner’s.

7.1.4 4. Self-check — 15 min

  1. Name the three components of the EPU index’s article filter.
  2. Why do Hassan et al. look for political bigrams near words like “risk” and “uncertainty”, rather than counting political words alone?
  3. Two coders agree on 80% of items. Why might that still be poor agreement?
  4. Name the steps in building a composite indicator. Which one do you think is most often skipped?

7.2 The lecture

7.2.1 1. The measurement chain

Every number in this course — GPR, a capability share, an ideal point, a sanctions count — is the end of a chain:

concept  →  indicator  →  source  →  coding rule  →  aggregation  →  presentation
"geopolitical    "share of      ten          the eight       monthly share,    a line with
 risk"           articles…"     newspapers   categories      base 1985–2019    spikes labelled

Two questions apply to every link:

  • Validity — does the indicator measure the concept? (Is newspaper attention geopolitical risk?)
  • Reliability — would someone else, following the same rules, get the same number? (Would a second coder put the same article in the same category?)

A measure can be perfectly reliable and invalid — a thermometer read identically by everyone that measures the wrong room. Today is about seeing every link, so you can audit a score you are sold and defend one you build.

7.2.2 2. Content analysis

Krippendorff (2018) defines content analysis as a research technique for making replicable and valid inferences from texts to the contexts of their use. Its steps:

Step In the GPR index In your coding today
Unitising — what is one unit? an article a headline
Sampling — which units? all articles in ten newspapers 40 headlines drawn at random from a GDELT query
Recording / coding — which category? a dictionary of words two human coders and a protocol
Reducing — how are codes summarised? monthly share counts and shares by category
Inferring — what do we conclude? geopolitical risk is high your firm’s countries are in the news for …

Manifest vs latent content. A dictionary sees manifest content — the words on the page. Human coders can see latent content — what an article is about. Dictionaries are cheap, fast and perfectly reliable; humans are slow and less reliable but can catch meaning. The best measures use both: a dictionary, audited by humans.

7.2.3 3. Three dictionary methods

GPR — Caldara & Iacoviello EPU — Baker, Bloom & Davis PRisk — Hassan et al.
Text newspaper articles newspaper articles earnings-call transcripts
Unit counted article with GPR words article with Economy and Policy and Uncertainty terms political two-word combinations (bigrams) near synonyms of risk or uncertainty
How the dictionary is chosen the authors’ eight categories the authors’ term sets, audited by hand learned: bigrams frequent in political texts and rare in non-political ones
Scaled by all articles all articles length of the transcript
Level world, 44 countries country firm × quarter
Validation narrative, hand audit large human audit of articles correlation with EPU; firms with high PRisk lobby and donate more; cut investment and hiring

What Hassan et al. add. Political risk becomes a firm-level variable — measured from what managers and analysts actually discuss. Their striking finding is that most of the variation in political risk is across firms at a given time, not common to all firms: aggregate indices like EPU, however useful, miss most of the political risk an individual firm faces.

7.2.4 4. Reliability: how much do coders agree?

Percent agreement — the share of items both coders put in the same category — is intuitive and misleading: two coders guessing at random will often agree, especially when one category dominates.

Cohen’s kappa corrects for chance:

\[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} \qquad p_o = \frac{\text{agreements}}{N} \qquad p_e = \sum_k \frac{n_{A,k}}{N}\cdot\frac{n_{B,k}}{N} \]

where \(n_{A,k}\) is the number of items coder A put in category \(k\).

A worked example. 40 headlines, three collapsed categories: not geopolitical (0), threat (1–5), act (6–8).

B: 0 B: threat B: act A total
A: 0 20 3 1 24
A: threat 2 8 0 10
A: act 0 1 5 6
B total 22 12 6 40
  • \(p_o = (20 + 8 + 5)/40 = 0.825\)
  • \(p_e = (24\cdot22 + 10\cdot12 + 6\cdot6)/40^2 = 684/1600 = 0.4275\)
  • \(\kappa = (0.825 - 0.4275)/(1 - 0.4275) \approx 0.69\)

An 82.5% agreement becomes a kappa of about 0.69 once chance is removed. Conventions differ: Landis and Koch’s widely used labels call 0.61–0.80 “substantial”; Krippendorff, for his alpha (the generalisation to more coders, missing data and other scales), asks for at least 0.800 for firm conclusions and accepts 0.667 only for tentative ones.

What to do with disagreement. Never average it away. Read each disagreement, decide whether the protocol was unclear or a coder erred, revise the protocol, and adjudicate. The disagreements are where the concept is fuzzy — they are information.

7.2.5 5. Big data: what it adds, what it hides

GDELT monitors news in many languages worldwide and turns it into searchable records. Its promise is coverage and speed: every country, every day, without a research assistant.

Its traps:

  • Selection. Only what is reported, by sources GDELT monitors, in languages it processes. Coverage changes over time: a rising count may be more sources, not more events.
  • Duplication. One wire story becomes hundreds of articles.
  • Machine coding. Automated event coding misreads irony, quotation, and historical references.
  • “N = all” is never all. A very large biased sample is still biased — and more confidently wrong.

The rule for a risk analyst: big data for detection (something is happening, here, now); small, carefully coded data for measurement (how much, compared with what).

7.2.6 6. Composite indicators — the OECD/JRC discipline

A composite indicator combines several indicators into one number — country-risk ratings, governance indices, your team’s exposure index. The OECD/JRC Handbook (2008) sets out ten steps; the five that decide most of the outcome are:

Step The choice The risk
Theoretical framework Which dimensions of risk, and why An index of whatever data were available
Missing data Drop, impute, or flag Countries silently disappear — usually the risky ones
Normalisation Min–max, z-score, rank, distance to a reference Different methods, different rankings
Weighting and aggregation Equal, statistical, expert; additive or geometric Additive aggregation lets a strength compensate for a weakness
Sensitivity analysis How much do results move when choices change? A ranking presented as fact that one weight would overturn

Min–max normalisation puts every indicator on 0–1, with the direction made consistent (higher = more risk):

\[ x^{*} = \frac{x - \min x}{\max x - \min x} \qquad\text{or, for an indicator where higher is safer,}\qquad x^{*} = \frac{\max x - x}{\max x - \min x} \]

Weighted additive aggregation: \(\;CI = \dfrac{\sum_j w_j\, x^{*}_j}{\sum_j w_j}\).

The point for the board. Weights are value judgements. Show them; let the reader move them; and report whether the ranking survives. A composite that hides its weights is an opinion dressed as a measurement.

7.2.7 7. The Tableau skill of the day

  • Tableau Prep (or the data source pane): parse a quarter string into a date; aggregate firm- quarters to quarters; union or join sources on a common key.
  • A highlight table of coder A × coder B, with grand totals: the agreement matrix, read straight off the screen.
  • Normalisation with LOD: { MIN([x]) } and { MAX([x]) } — a FIXED expression with no dimension computes over the whole table.
  • Parameters as weights, and RANK() to show how the ranking moves when the weights do.

7.3.1 (a) Firm-level political risk against EPU — 30 min

7.3.1.1 Prepare — choose a route

The firm file is firm × quarter; EPU is monthly. Both must reach a common quarter grain.

The quarter identifier in the firm file is a string such as 2010q1 in the vintages we have seen — check yours. Convert it to a date:

[Quarter date] = MAKEDATE( INT(LEFT([date], 4)),
                           (INT(RIGHT([date], 1)) - 1) * 3 + 1,
                           1 )

And for EPU (Year and Month in the current file; Year may arrive as text because of the citation rows at the bottom, hence the INT):

[Quarter date] = DATETRUNC('quarter', MAKEDATE(INT([Year]), INT([Month]), 1))

Route 1 · Tableau Prep (if your licence includes it)

  1. Input firmquarter_2022q1.csv → Clean step: add [Quarter date] → Aggregate step: group by [Quarter date]; AVG(PRisk), COUNTD([gvkey]).
  2. Input the EPU workbook → Clean: add [Quarter date], drop rows with no value → Aggregate: group by [Quarter date]; AVG([GEPU_current]) (or GEPU_ppp — log which).
  3. Join the two on [Quarter date] (inner) → Output as .hyper. Open it in Tableau Desktop.
  4. Every step is a visible, saved decision: export the flow (.tfl) with your hand-in.

Route 2 · the data source pane (Tableau Desktop or Tableau Public edition)

  1. Connect to both files. Drag the firm file to the canvas, then the EPU sheet next to it.
  2. In the relationship dialog, choose Create Relationship Calculation on each side and enter the two [Quarter date] formulas above.
  3. Relationships aggregate each table at its own level: AVG(PRisk) is averaged over firms, AVG(GEPU_current) over the months of the quarter — exactly what you want.

7.3.1.2 Replicate — Sheet “Firm-level political risk and EPU”

  1. [Quarter date] → Columns as continuous Quarter.
  2. AVG(PRisk) → Rows; the EPU measure → Rows; dual axis, not synchronised (different units).
  3. Annotate three peaks; check what happened in those quarters.
  4. Title with the finding; caption with both sources, vintages and access dates.

7.3.1.3 Compare

  • Do the two series move together? Where do they part company — and which one would you trust for a firm, and which for a country?
  • Add COUNTD of firms as a third view. Does the number of firms change over the period? Why does that matter for an average?
  • Filter hqcountrycode (2-letter codes) to exclude US. How many firms are left?

7.3.2 (b) Your coded index — 20 min

Connect to coded/coding_sheet.xlsx.

  1. The agreement matrix. coder_A → Rows, coder_B → Columns, CNT(id) → Text and Colour: a highlight table. Analysis → Totals → Show Row and Column Grand Totals.

  2. Percent agreement in Tableau:

    [Agree] = IF [coder_A] = [coder_B] THEN 1 ELSE 0 END      →   AVG([Agree]) = p_o

  3. Kappa, by hand. From the grand totals, compute \(p_e = \sum_k (n_{A,k}/N)(n_{B,k}/N)\) and \(\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)\). Write both on your slide. If categories are sparse, also compute kappa on three collapsed categories (0 / threat / act) — and say which you report.

  4. Adjudicate. The adjudicator reads every disagreement and fills final, with a one-line reason. List the protocol changes you would make.

  5. The index. From final: the share of headlines in each category, and the share coded geopolitical (≠ 0) by month. Mark econ comments separately: how many headlines about sanctions or export controls did the GPR categories force into 0?

7.3.3 (c) A composite exposure index with moving weights — 30 min

7.3.3.1 Select

Start from a spine: a sheet listing your firm’s key countries plus comparators — 8 to 12 countries, with iso3. Every input is left-joined to it, so missing values are visible rather than silently dropped.

Indicator Source Direction Preparation
Country GPR GPRC_xxx columns higher = riskier pivot the GPRC_ columns (not GPRHC_; values are % of articles); extract the code with RIGHT([Pivot Field Names], 3); average the last 12 months
Country EPU All_Country_Data.xlsx higher = riskier pivot country columns; add iso3 (the columns are country names such as UK, US, Korea, and China appears three times); average the last 12 months
Political stability WGI 2025, sheet pv, Governance score (0-100) higher = safer latest year; join on Economy (code)
Distance in UN voting from the firm’s home country Session 04 ideal points higher = riskier ABS([Ideal point] - [Home ideal point]), latest year

Each input must be one row per country before it is joined to the spine — otherwise the min and max below are taken over months, not countries. Reduce each input first: an Aggregate step in Tableau Prep, or — in Desktop — a small sheet (country × the averaged value) exported with Worksheet → Export → Crosstab into a derived file in data/derived/, logged as such. Raw files are never edited.

7.3.3.2 Normalise

Min–max across the spine countries, with a FIXED expression over the whole table:

[GPR n]   = ([GPR]   - {MIN([GPR])})   / ({MAX([GPR])}   - {MIN([GPR])})
[WGI n]   = ({MAX([WGI])} - [WGI])     / ({MAX([WGI])}   - {MIN([WGI])})      // inverted

…and the same for EPU and voting distance.

7.3.3.3 Weight

Create four parameters, w GPR, w EPU, w WGI, w Vote — integers 0 to 5, default 1 — and show their controls. Missing values must not count as zero risk, so reweight over the indicators that exist:

[Composite] =
  ( IF NOT ISNULL([GPR n])  THEN [w GPR]  * [GPR n]  ELSE 0 END
  + IF NOT ISNULL([EPU n])  THEN [w EPU]  * [EPU n]  ELSE 0 END
  + IF NOT ISNULL([WGI n])  THEN [w WGI]  * [WGI n]  ELSE 0 END
  + IF NOT ISNULL([Vote n]) THEN [w Vote] * [Vote n] ELSE 0 END )
  /
  ( IF NOT ISNULL([GPR n])  THEN [w GPR]  ELSE 0 END
  + IF NOT ISNULL([EPU n])  THEN [w EPU]  ELSE 0 END
  + IF NOT ISNULL([WGI n])  THEN [w WGI]  ELSE 0 END
  + IF NOT ISNULL([Vote n]) THEN [w Vote] ELSE 0 END )

That is a decision, not a fix: write it in your log (“composite renormalised over available indicators”).

Sheet: countries on Rows, sorted by SUM([Composite]), bars, with RANK(SUM([Composite])) as a label.

7.3.3.4 Test

Record each country’s rank under equal weights, then under each indicator alone (weight 1, others 0). In a small table: how many countries change rank by more than two places? Which country is most sensitive to the weights — and why?
