7  Measuring Risk: How a Political Judgement Becomes a Number

7.1 The thermometer in the wrong room

Every geopolitical risk score that reaches a board has a pedigree, and the pedigree is usually hidden. A consultancy reports that a country’s risk has “risen to 71”; a bank’s dashboard turns a cell from amber to red; an index published by a central bank spikes in the week an invasion begins. Each of these numbers is the last link of a chain that started with a concept nobody can observe directly — the probability that politics will damage a firm’s business — and passed through a sequence of choices about texts, words, coders, units, weights and scales. The choices were made by someone, for reasons, and most of them could have been made differently.

This chapter is about seeing that chain, so that a score one is sold can be audited and a score one builds can be defended. Two words carry most of the weight, and they are worth fixing at the start.

Validity — the degree to which an indicator measures the concept it claims to measure. Is the share of newspaper articles mentioning war threats a measure of geopolitical risk, or of editors’ attention to geopolitics, or of the number of journalists posted to a war zone?

Reliability — the degree to which the same procedure, applied by someone else to the same material, yields the same number. Would a second coder, following the same rules, put the same article in the same category? Would the same dictionary, run next year on the same archive, return the same count?

The two are independent in one direction only. A measure can be perfectly reliable and entirely invalid: a thermometer that everyone reads identically, hanging in the wrong room. It cannot be valid without being reasonably reliable, because a measure that returns different numbers for the same object is measuring, in part, the person who applied it. The history of measurement in the social sciences is largely a history of trading one against the other — cheap, mechanical procedures that are perfectly reliable and of uncertain validity, against slow human judgement that captures meaning and disagrees with itself.

7.2 The chain, link by link

Write the chain out once, so that its parts have names. A measure of geopolitical risk passes from a concept (“geopolitical risk”) to an indicator (the share of articles that discuss geopolitical tensions), drawn from a source (a fixed set of newspapers), processed by a coding rule (a list of words grouped into categories), summarised by an aggregation (a monthly share, rescaled so that a base period averages 100) and delivered in a presentation (a line with spikes labelled “9/11” and “Iraq invasion”). Every one of the risk measures in this book can be written in that form, and every link is a place where the number can part company with the concept.

At the concept link, the danger is that nobody has said what is being measured. “Political risk” in the business literature has meant expropriation, contract repudiation, currency inconvertibility, civil unrest, regulatory change and, more recently, sanctions and export controls — things with different causes, different probabilities and different remedies. An indicator that pools them may be a reasonable summary of the newspapers and a poor guide to any one of the decisions it is meant to inform.

At the indicator and source links, the danger is selection. Newspapers report what is new and dramatic, in the countries their readers care about, in the language they print in. The ten newspapers behind the Caldara–Iacoviello index are American, British and Canadian; a war in a country with no correspondents may barely register. That is a property of the index, not a flaw, but a user must carry it into every inference.

At the coding link, the danger is the gap between words and meaning: a rule that counts “war” cannot tell a war from a price war or a trade war. At the aggregation link, choices about scaling and weighting, innocuous one at a time, decide the result. And at the presentation link — the one most often forgotten — the labels on the spikes tell the reader what to see, and the absence of labels on the troughs tells them what not to.

From the archive · Inferences from enemy propaganda, 1941–1945

The systematic analysis of text as evidence about political intentions did not begin with computers. During the Second World War, analysts in the United States — among them a group at the Federal Communications Commission’s foreign broadcast monitoring service — read German radio propaganda not for what it said but for what its saying implied. If the propaganda ministry began preparing the public for a particular kind of news, what did the regime expect to happen? If a theme disappeared from broadcasts, what had changed in the regime’s own calculation?

Alexander George later reconstructed this work and asked how well the analysts’ inferences had held up once the German archives could be checked (George 1959). His account made two points that every risk analyst should know. The first is that the useful inferences were rarely about the manifest content — the frequency of a word — and usually about what the content revealed of the speaker’s situation and purposes, which required a model of the speaker. The second is that the analysts were most often wrong when they forgot that the text had been produced for an audience, by an actor with reasons to mislead it. A newspaper is not a propaganda ministry, and an earnings call is not a broadcast to a wartime public. But both are texts written by people with purposes, and an index built from them inherits those purposes.

7.3 Content analysis: from reading to inference

The discipline that formalised these practices is content analysis. Its early textbook definition called it a technique “for the objective, systematic, and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication” (Berelson 1952). The modern definition, Krippendorff’s, is broader and more demanding.

Content analysis — a research technique for making replicable and valid inferences from texts (or other meaningful matter) to the contexts of their use (Krippendorff 2018). Replicable: a second analyst following the same procedure should reach the same result. Valid: the inference must hold about the context, not merely about the texts.

The shift from “description” to “inference” matters. Nobody builds a geopolitical risk index to learn how often “missile” appears in newspapers; the texts are evidence, and the context is the object.

Krippendorff breaks the procedure into steps, and they map cleanly onto both the published indices and the small hand-coded indices that analysts inside firms build for themselves. Unitising decides what a single unit is: an article in the GPR and EPU indices, a transcript in Hassan and colleagues’ measure, a headline in a hand-coded index of the news about one country. Sampling decides which units enter: every article in ten newspapers, every earnings call of listed firms that hold one, a few dozen headlines drawn at random from a news query. Recording assigns each unit to a category, by dictionary or by coders following a protocol. Reducing summarises the codes. Inferring draws the conclusion about the context: geopolitical risk is high this month; this firm faces unusual political risk this quarter.

7.3.1 Manifest and latent content

The distinction that does most of the work in practice is between what a text says and what it is about. Manifest content is on the surface: the words, their frequency, their co-occurrence. Latent content requires interpretation: whether a headline reports a threat or an act, whether a statement is ironic, whether a reference to war is literal. Dictionaries read manifest content; human coders can read latent content. The trade-off between them is the trade-off between reliability and validity seen from another angle. A dictionary is perfectly reliable — run it twice and it returns the same count — and blind. A human coder sees meaning, and two human coders see slightly different meanings.

The best measures use both. They encode the analyst’s judgement in a dictionary that a machine can apply to millions of documents, and then audit the machine with humans who read a sample and check whether the dictionary’s classifications match what the texts are about. That design — a dictionary audited by people — is common to all three of the indices this chapter examines, and the quality of the audit is the best single guide to the quality of the index.

7.4.1 Geopolitical risk as newspaper attention

Caldara and Iacoviello define geopolitical risk as the threat, realisation and escalation of adverse events associated with wars, terrorism and tensions between states that affect the normal and peaceful course of international relations (Caldara and Iacoviello 2022). Their index counts, each month, the share of articles in a fixed set of newspapers that contain words from eight categories: war threats, peace threats, military build-ups, nuclear threats, terror threats, beginning of war, escalation of war and terror acts. The first five form a sub-index of threats, the last three a sub-index of acts. The series is rescaled so that its average over a base period equals 100, and national indices are built by requiring that the article also mention the country.

The theory of risk embedded in this procedure is explicit: geopolitical risk is what informed newspaper readers in a few large democracies are being told to worry about. The split between threats and acts is not a technical detail. It distinguishes the risk that something will happen from the fact that it has, and the two behave differently — threats can rise for months before an act, and an act can resolve uncertainty as well as create it. Any hand-coding protocol that borrows the eight categories needs a rule for the headline that reports both, and the natural rule is that acts beat threats.

The authors validate the index in two ways. Historically, the archive lets them extend the series back to 1900, and it spikes where a historian would want it to: around the two world wars, at the beginning of the Korean War, during the Cuban Missile Crisis and after 11 September 2001. Economically, higher readings foreshadow lower investment and employment and are associated with a higher probability of disaster and larger downside risks, and the adverse consequences come from threats as well as from acts. They also build industry- and firm-level versions, and find that investment falls more in industries exposed to aggregate geopolitical risk (Caldara and Iacoviello 2022). The first test is of face validity; the second, of predictive validity, is the more demanding.

7.4.2 Policy uncertainty as a three-way conjunction

Baker, Bloom and Davis built the Economic Policy Uncertainty index on a different logic (Baker, Bloom, and Davis 2016). An article counts only if it contains a term from each of three sets: one about the economy, one about policy and one about uncertainty. The conjunction is the clever part. A filter on “uncertainty” alone would capture weather and sport; a filter on “policy” alone would capture every legislative report. Requiring all three narrows the count to articles that discuss uncertainty about economic policy, at the cost of missing articles that discuss it without using any of the listed words.

What distinguishes the EPU work methodologically is the audit. Human readers coded a large sample of articles, and the policy term set was tuned to reduce errors in both directions against their judgements. The audit does not make the index perfect. It makes its error rates known, which is the more useful property. The global index is a GDP-weighted average of national indices, published in two versions — weighted by current-price GDP and by purchasing-power-parity GDP — and the choice between them is a small instance of the aggregation decisions discussed below: the second gives large emerging economies more weight, and therefore gives their politics more voice in what is called “global” uncertainty.

7.4.3 Political risk as what managers discuss

Hassan, Hollander, van Lent and Tahoun asked a question the newspaper indices cannot answer: how much political risk does an individual firm face, and does it matter for what the firm does (Hassan et al. 2019)? Their text is not newspapers but the transcripts of quarterly earnings conference calls, in which senior managers present results and answer questions from analysts. An earnings call is where a firm’s leadership, questioned by people paid to find weaknesses, talks about what worries it.

Their dictionary is not written by hand. It is learned. The authors took a body of political texts and a body of non-political texts and identified two-word combinations — bigrams — that are frequent in the first and rare in the second. They then counted, in each transcript, the political bigrams that appear close to a synonym of “risk” or “uncertainty”, weighted them by how distinctively political they are, and divided by the length of the transcript. The result, PRisk, is a firm-quarter measure of the share of the conversation devoted to political risk.

Dictionary method — a measurement procedure that assigns texts to categories by counting words or phrases from a pre-specified list. The list may be written by the analyst (GPR, EPU) or learned from labelled texts (PRisk). Either way it is a theory of what the concept looks like in language, and its validity depends on whether that theory holds in the texts to which it is applied.

The proximity requirement deserves a moment. Counting political bigrams alone would measure how much a firm talks about politics — which may reflect opportunity as much as risk, or simply a chief executive’s interests. Requiring that the political language appear near the language of risk and uncertainty turns a measure of political attention into a measure of political risk. It is the same logic as EPU’s three-way conjunction, applied at the level of a sentence rather than an article.

The dependence of a dictionary on its domain is not a hypothetical worry. Loughran and McDonald showed that the general-purpose word lists then in common use classified much of the ordinary vocabulary of corporate filings as negative — words such as “liability”, “tax” or “cost” that in a financial report are neutral descriptions — and built a finance-specific list in response (Loughran and McDonald 2011). Tetlock’s earlier work on newspaper pessimism and stock prices had shown that a dictionary measure of tone could carry real information about markets (Tetlock 2007). Together they make the point that runs through this chapter: a dictionary works only for the texts it was built for.

7.5 What the firm-level measure found

Hassan and colleagues validated PRisk in three ways. First, its aggregate across firms moves over time with the EPU index, as it should if both capture the same variation in political uncertainty. Second, it varies intuitively across sectors and over time. Third, and most persuasively, firms with high PRisk behave as theory predicts firms facing political risk should behave: they cut investment and hiring, and they become more active politically, lobbying more and donating more to politicians. A measure that predicted nothing about behaviour would be a curiosity. A measure that predicts the behaviour the concept implies has passed a test of validity that no amount of reliability could substitute for.

The finding that matters most for a multinational concerns where the variation lies. Most of the variation in political risk is not common to all firms at a given time, nor common to all firms in a sector; it is specific to the firm. Two companies in the same industry, in the same quarter, can face very different political risk, because they sell to different governments, depend on different regulations, source from different countries or hold different licences. Two further results sharpen the point. The dispersion of political risk across firms widens when aggregate political risk is high, so that the moments at which a national index rises are precisely the moments at which it says least about any one firm. And when the measure is broken down by topic, firms that talk more about the risks of a given political topic increase their lobbying on that topic, and not on others, in the following quarter (Hassan et al. 2019). Managers do not merely worry in general; they worry about something, and act on it.

The implication is uncomfortable for anyone who buys a country-risk score. An aggregate index measures what is common; the political risk that reaches a particular firm is mostly what is not. A board that tracks a national index and believes it is tracking its own exposure is watching the weather report for a region while the risk is in the plumbing of its own building.

This is where the five channels used throughout this book earn their place. Political risk reaches a multinational through market access (who may sell what to whom), inputs (what can be bought, and from where), finance (who will lend, insure, settle), assets and people (what can be seized, damaged or detained) and data and operations (what can be stored, transferred or shut down). A newspaper index measures, at best, the climate in which these channels operate; PRisk measures how worried a firm’s own managers are, which integrates the channels from the inside. Neither tells the firm which channel is exposed. That is the job of a composite exposure index built for a named firm, channel by channel, and the arithmetic of such indices is the subject of the second half of this chapter.

7.6 Two coders and a protocol

The obvious measure of reliability is percent agreement: the share of items on which two coders chose the same category. It is intuitive and misleading. Two coders who assigned codes at random, in proportion to how often each category occurs, would agree often. In a sample where four headlines in five are not about geopolitics at all, two coders who both write “not geopolitical” for every item will agree on most of them, while having measured nothing.

Cohen’s coefficient corrects for this by comparing observed agreement with the agreement that chance alone would produce, given each coder’s own distribution of codes (Cohen 1960).

Cohen’s kappa — a measure of agreement between two coders on a nominal scale, corrected for chance: \(\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)\), where \(p_o\) is the observed share of agreements and \(p_e\) the agreement expected if the two coders assigned categories independently at their own marginal rates. A kappa of 1 is perfect agreement; 0 is agreement no better than chance; negative values are worse than chance.

If coder A puts \(n_{A,k}\) of \(N\) items in category \(k\) and coder B puts \(n_{B,k}\) there, the expected agreement is

\[ p_e = \sum_k \frac{n_{A,k}}{N}\cdot\frac{n_{B,k}}{N}. \]

Take forty headlines, collapsed into three categories — not geopolitical, threat, act — with coder A’s codes in the rows and coder B’s in the columns:

B: 0 B: threat B: act A total
A: 0 20 3 1 24
A: threat 2 8 0 10
A: act 0 1 5 6
B total 22 12 6 40

The coders agree on the diagonal, \(20 + 8 + 5 = 33\) items, so \(p_o = 33/40 = 0.825\). Chance agreement is \((24 \cdot 22 + 10 \cdot 12 + 6 \cdot 6)/40^2 = 684/1600 = 0.4275\). Kappa is therefore \((0.825 - 0.4275)/(1 - 0.4275) \approx 0.69\). An agreement rate of better than four in five shrinks by almost a fifth once chance is removed, because both coders put more than half the headlines in the dominant category, where much of their agreement was to be expected.

What counts as good enough is a convention, and the conventions differ. Landis and Koch proposed a set of labels that have become widely used, under which values between 0.61 and 0.80 are “substantial” (Landis and Koch 1977). Krippendorff, for his alpha — a generalisation that handles more than two coders, missing data and ordinal or interval scales — asks for at least 0.800 before data are relied on, and accepts values from 0.667 only for tentative conclusions (Krippendorff 2018; Hayes and Krippendorff 2007). By the first convention the worked example is respectable; by the second it would support only a tentative claim.

7.6.1 What disagreement is for

The worst thing to do with disagreement is to average it away. Disagreements are where the concept is fuzzy, and they are information. Some will be errors: a coder misread a headline, or forgot a decision rule. Others will reveal that the protocol did not anticipate a case — a headline about sanctions, a cyber operation, a military exercise that might be a build-up or might be routine. Those are the valuable ones. The right response is to revise the protocol, write down the new rule, and then adjudicate.

Any protocol modelled on the GPR categories contains a gap of exactly this kind. Sanctions, export controls and trade wars are not among Caldara and Iacoviello’s eight categories, so a protocol that follows them faithfully must assign such headlines to “not geopolitical” — at best with a flag. Yet a headline about export controls on semiconductors may matter more to a firm’s inputs than one about a distant military exercise. The gap is not a mistake in the GPR index, which measures what it says it measures; it is a reminder that an index built to track war and terrorism may not be the index a firm needs.

7.6.2 Machines as coders

The newest temptation is to hand the coding to a machine: a classifier trained on headlines people have already coded, or a general-purpose language model asked in plain words to apply the protocol. Such systems read latent content better than any dictionary and cost little per headline, but they relocate the problem rather than escape it. A machine coder is a third coder, and its agreement with the humans can be measured with the same coefficient. Automated methods, in Grimmer and Stewart’s words, are no substitute for careful thought and close reading, and require extensive and problem-specific validation (Grimmer and Stewart 2013). A classifier that agrees with people on headlines about elections may disagree on headlines about export controls; only a fresh human-coded sample will say.

7.7 Big data: detection, not measurement

Hand-coded samples are often drawn from automated news monitors, of which the GDELT Project, which turns news media worldwide in many languages into searchable records, is the best known. It is the most visible example of big data in political risk: a source that promises coverage — every country, every day — and speed, without a research assistant reading anything.

The promise is real, and the traps are four. The first is selection. GDELT records what is reported, by the sources it monitors, in the languages it processes. Its coverage of sources has changed over time, so a rising count of articles about a country may mean more sources rather than more events. The second is duplication: one wire-service story republished by hundreds of outlets is counted hundreds of times. The third is machine coding, which misreads irony, quotation and historical reference, and errs in ways that are not random. The fourth is the most seductive: “N = all” is never all, and a very large sample drawn by a biased process is still biased — only more confidently so.

The fourth trap has been formalised. Meng showed that the error of an estimate from a large non-random sample depends on the correlation between being in the sample and the quantity being measured, and that even a tiny such correlation can make a sample of millions worth less than a small random sample (Meng 2018). The parable everyone cites is the story of a search-based model of influenza that, after an initial success, substantially overestimated flu prevalence for long stretches, because the behaviour generating the data changed while the model did not (Lazer et al. 2014). Neither is about geopolitics; both describe what can happen to an event count built from online news.

More data is not better measurement. Millions of machine-coded news records are a sample drawn by a process nobody controls. Forty headlines drawn at random from a well-defined query and coded by two people with a written protocol can be more valid for a specific question — not because forty is more than millions, but because the analyst knows what the forty are.

The working rule for a risk analyst follows. Use big data for detection: something is happening, here, now, and it is worth a closer look. Use small, carefully coded data for measurement: how much is happening, compared with what, and whether it has changed. A monitoring system that flags a spike in news about a firm’s supplier country is doing detection well. A board paper that reports that “risk in the supplier country has doubled” on the strength of the same spike is doing measurement badly.

7.8 Composite indicators and the arithmetic of judgement

The third measurement task is the one that a multinational is most likely to perform itself: combining several indicators into one number. Country-risk ratings, governance indices, supplier-risk scores and a firm’s own exposure index are all composite indicators. They are attractive for the same reason they are dangerous: they compress several dimensions into one figure that a board can read at a glance, and they hide, in the compression, a series of decisions that determine the ranking.

The standard guide is the handbook produced by the OECD and the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre (OECD and European Commission Joint Research Centre 2008). It sets out ten steps, from the theoretical framework to the presentation of results. Five of them decide most of the outcome.

The theoretical framework says which dimensions of risk the index captures and why. Without one, the index becomes a collection of whatever data were available — which is to say, an index whose content was chosen by the data suppliers rather than by the analyst. For a firm’s exposure index, the natural framework is the five channels: each indicator must be defended as a measure of exposure through a named channel, or it does not belong. An index of a firm’s exposure to sanctions risk, for example, might draw on the share of revenue earned in sanctioned or sanctioning jurisdictions (market access), on the share of critical inputs sourced from them (inputs) and on the banks through which the firm settles in them (finance) — each defensible, and each measuring a different route by which the same political event would reach the accounts.

Missing data is where indices quietly lie. The country GPR indices cover 44 countries; the country EPU indices about twenty. A composite that drops every country missing an indicator will cover only large, well-documented economies — and the countries it silently drops are, very often, the risky ones, about which fewer people write. Imputing a value is a claim about a country nobody measured; flagging the gap is honest and less tidy.

Normalisation puts indicators measured in different units on a common scale. The simplest method, min–max, maps each indicator to the interval from 0 to 1:

\[ x^{*} = \frac{x - \min x}{\max x - \min x} \qquad\text{or, where higher is safer,}\qquad x^{*} = \frac{\max x - x}{\max x - \min x}. \]

The second form matters more than it looks. The Worldwide Governance Indicators’ measure of political stability is scored so that higher means more stable; in a risk index it must be inverted, or a stable country will score as risky. The indicators also changed scale in their 2025 edition, moving to a 0–100 score and recalculating their history, so that values from different editions cannot be mixed; the methodology behind the indicators is set out in Kaufmann, Kraay, and Mastruzzi (2011). They are themselves composites, pooling many sources in an unobserved-components model that reports a margin of error for every estimate — a discipline most commercial risk scores do not impose on themselves. Other normalisations — z-scores, ranks, distance to a reference country — produce different rankings from the same data, because they treat outliers and gaps differently. Min–max is sensitive to the extremes: a single country with an extraordinary value compresses everyone else into a narrow band.

Weighting and aggregation combine the normalised indicators. The usual form is a weighted average,

\[ CI = \frac{\sum_j w_j \, x^{*}_j}{\sum_j w_j}, \]

and the weights can be equal, derived statistically (from principal components, say), or set by experts. Each choice is defensible and none is neutral. Equal weights are a judgement that every dimension matters equally, which is rarely what anyone believes. Statistical weights reward the indicators that move together, which may simply be the redundant ones. Expert weights encode the expert.

The aggregation rule carries a further assumption that is easy to miss.

Compensability — the property of an aggregation rule under which a high score on one dimension can offset a low score on another. Additive aggregation is fully compensatory: a country with excellent governance and an active war can average to “medium”. Geometric aggregation is partially compensatory, penalising unbalanced profiles; non-compensatory rules rank countries without allowing any trade-off at all.

For a risk index, full compensability is often the wrong assumption. A firm does not experience the average of a country’s risks; it experiences the worst of them that touches its operations. Excellent contract enforcement does not compensate for a plant inside a war zone. Additive aggregation remains the default because it is transparent, but whoever uses it should show the profile behind each score.

From the archive · The Human Development Index, 1990 and 2010

The most influential composite indicator of the past half-century was designed to argue with a single number. The first Human Development Report, published by the United Nations Development Programme in 1990 under the direction of Mahbub ul Haq, proposed the Human Development Index as an alternative to ranking countries by income per head (United Nations Development Programme 1990). It combined three dimensions — a long and healthy life, knowledge and a decent standard of living — each normalised against minimum and maximum values, and averaged them with equal weights. It was a simplification built to change a conversation, and it did.

Twenty years later the 2010 report changed the aggregation from an arithmetic to a geometric mean of the three dimension indices and revised the indicators of education and income (United Nations Development Programme 2010). The reason given was compensability: under the arithmetic mean, a country could make up for poor health with high income, one for one; under the geometric mean, poor performance in one dimension weighs more heavily. The change re-ranked countries. Ravallion then showed that the new formula implied trade-offs between longevity and income that few would accept if they were stated openly — in effect, valuations of an extra year of life that varied enormously with a country’s income, and were very low in the poorest countries (Ravallion 2012).

The weights and the aggregation rule were value judgements; changing them changed the ranking; and the implicit prices they carried became visible only when someone did the algebra. A firm’s exposure index embeds trade-offs of the same kind, and the board deserves to see them stated.

7.9 Sensitivity: the ranking that one weight overturns

The last of the decisive steps is the one most often skipped. Sensitivity analysis asks how much the results move when the choices change: a different normalisation, different weights, a different treatment of missing data, a different aggregation rule. Saisana, Saltelli and Tarantola set out how to do this systematically, by treating each methodological choice as an uncertain input and examining the distribution of ranks it produces (Saisana, Saltelli, and Tarantola 2005). The simplest version needs no special software. Record the ranking under equal weights; then under each indicator alone; then count how many countries move by more than a few places. A country that stays near the top under every reasonable choice is robustly high-risk. A country whose rank swings from fifth to twenty-fifth depending on one weight is not “fifth”; it is a country about which the index has no firm opinion.

This is why a composite worth showing to a board should be presented with weights the reader can move, or at least with the rankings that alternative weights would produce. The point is not interactivity for its own sake. It is that a composite which hides its weights is an opinion dressed as a measurement, and a composite whose weights are visible and adjustable is an argument the reader can test. A board that sees a supplier country fall from first to ninth when one weight is halved has learnt that the decision rests on a judgement about that indicator, not on the index.

7.10 A short review of the literature

Content analysis as a social-science method was codified in the mid-twentieth century. Berelson (1952) gave the classic definition centred on manifest content, and George (1959) reconstructed the wartime analysis of enemy propaganda that showed both the power and the dangers of inference from text. Krippendorff (2018) is the standard modern treatment, recasting the method around replicable and valid inference and setting out the reliability apparatus; Hayes and Krippendorff (2007) make the case for Krippendorff’s alpha as a general standard. The chance-corrected agreement coefficient is Cohen (1960), and the labels most often attached to its values are Landis and Koch (1977).

Grimmer and Stewart (2013) is the essential guide to automated analysis of political texts, and its insistence on problem-specific validation is the spine of this chapter. Gentzkow, Kelly, and Taddy (2019) survey text as data in economics. In finance, Tetlock (2007) showed that dictionary measures of media tone carry information about markets, and Loughran and McDonald (2011) showed how badly general-purpose dictionaries misread specialised documents.

The three indices at the centre of the chapter are Caldara and Iacoviello (2022) for geopolitical risk, Baker, Bloom, and Davis (2016) for economic policy uncertainty and Hassan et al. (2019) for firm-level political risk. On big data, Lazer et al. (2014) is the cautionary tale and Meng (2018) the formal account of why sample size does not rescue a biased selection process.

Composite indicators have their own methodological literature. OECD and European Commission Joint Research Centre (2008) is the practitioner’s handbook and Saisana, Saltelli, and Tarantola (2005) the statement of sensitivity and uncertainty analysis as a quality test. Kaufmann, Kraay, and Mastruzzi (2011) document the Worldwide Governance Indicators, a composite of composites. The Human Development Index (United Nations Development Programme 1990, 2010) and the critique of its implicit trade-offs in Ravallion (2012) are the clearest case study of how aggregation rules carry value judgements.

7.11 What the numbers do not license

Each measure in this chapter comes with a sentence it does not license. Where two of them part company, the question is not which is right but which link of which chain explains the gap.

The line of average firm-level political risk does not tell a particular multinational how much political risk it faces. It is an average over American listed firms, and the central finding of the paper that built it is that most political risk is specific to the firm — precisely the variation an average removes. A board can use the line to see when political uncertainty was high for corporate America as a whole; it cannot use it to see its own exposure, which would require scoring its own calls, or at least those of its closest peers.

A hand-coded index of the news about a country, however carefully its coders agree, does not license a claim about how geopolitical that country’s situation is. A few dozen headlines from a news monitor that leans towards English-language media, coded against a protocol that places sanctions outside geopolitics, measure what one slice of the press said, filtered through one theory of what counts. What a good kappa licenses is a statement about how reliably two trained readers can apply that protocol — and the list of disagreements licenses something more valuable, which is a map of the places where the protocol must change before anyone relies on it.

A composite exposure index does not license a ranking presented as fact. It licenses a ranking under stated weights, together with the weight that would overturn it. That is less than a board usually wants, and more than it usually gets.

The argument of this chapter can be compressed into a five-part audit, to be put to every risk score a multinational builds or buys: what concept, from which sources, coded by whom or by what, normalised and weighted how, and how sensitive to each of those choices. None requires a statistician; each requires only that the score’s makers have written down what they did. The scores that survive the audit are not thereby true. They are measurements — numbers whose errors can be named and whose disagreements with other numbers can be traced to a decision someone took. A score that cannot answer the five questions is somebody’s opinion, with decimals; and the danger of such a score is not that the opinion is wrong, but that the decimals make it impossible to argue with.
