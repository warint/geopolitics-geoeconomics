E.2 Participation — 10%

Marked against a published, absolute bar — never ranked:

Mark What it looks like across the term
9–10 Regularly moves the discussion forward with reference to the reading; asks real questions after Risk Briefs; fully engaged in every practice round
7–8 Contributes often; engaged in practice rounds; occasional questions
5–6 Present and engaged when asked; rarely volunteers
< 5 Disengaged, or absent from practice rounds without reason

Practice rounds are formative: commented on, not marked. What is recorded is that you engaged and handed in the .twbx and provenance log.

E.3 Late work and missed assessments

  • Quiz missed with a documented reason: its weight moves to the take-home. Without: zero.
  • Risk Brief missed with a documented reason: rescheduled. Without: zero.
  • Team memo: −5% of its mark per day late.
  • Take-home: submissions after the window closes are not accepted, per HEC regulations.

E.4 Quizzes

Twenty minutes, at the start of Sessions 04, 07 and 10, on paper, closed book, individual.

Quiz Session Covers
Quiz 1 04 Sessions 01–03: what geopolitical risk is; the international economic order; anarchy and the balance of power
Quiz 2 07 Sessions 04–06: trade, conflict and fragmentation; international institutions; private authority and standards
Quiz 3 10 Sessions 07–09: measuring risk; cyber geopolitics; economic diplomacy

E.4.1 The format — 10 points each

Part Points What it asks
A · Concepts 4 Four short definitions or distinctions, one or two sentences each
B · The big work 3 The research question, the data and the central finding of one of the session readings — and one limitation
C · Read the chart 3 An unseen Tableau chart: say what it shows, what decision it was built with, and what it does not license a firm to conclude

E.4.2 Specimen — Quiz 1

A · Concepts (4 points, 1 each)

  1. Distinguish risk from uncertainty in Knight’s sense, and give one geopolitical example of each.
  2. What is the difference between Kobrin’s macro and micro political risk?
  3. In the GPR index, why are counts of geopolitical articles divided by the total number of articles?
  4. What does “anarchy” mean in the realist tradition — and what does it not mean?

B · The big work (3 points)

Caldara and Iacoviello (2022) distinguish GPR Threats from GPR Acts. (a) Which categories enter each? (b) Why does the distinction matter for a firm’s investment decision? (c) Name one limitation of the index for a firm whose operations are mainly in sub-Saharan Africa.

C · Read the chart (3 points)

[A stacked area chart: shares of world GDP, current US dollars, 1970–2023, for the G7, China, and the rest of the world.]

  1. State the finding in one sentence. (b) The chart uses current US dollars. How would it change at purchasing-power parity, and why? (c) What does the chart not tell a Canadian manufacturer deciding where to build its next plant?

E.4.3 Specimen answers (Part A)

  1. Risk: probabilities estimable from experience (piracy losses on a shipping route). Uncertainty: probabilities not estimable (whether a given strait will be blockaded within five years).
  2. Macro risk affects all foreign firms in a country (a general expropriation, currency inconvertibility); micro risk affects one sector or firm (a mining-code change, a contract cancellation).
  3. Because newspapers’ total output changes over time; the share removes the effect of size, so the index measures relative attention to geopolitical tension.
  4. The absence of a government above states — no authority to enforce agreements. It does not mean chaos or constant war.

E.5 The Risk Brief

Four minutes. One development. One view. One firm. One recommendation.

Each student delivers one Risk Brief, at the start of one of Sessions 02–11. Sign-up opens at the end of Session 01; four briefs per session.

E.5.1 The brief

You are the geopolitical risk analyst of a named multinational. Something happened in the last four weeks. Brief your chief risk officer.

Minute What you say
0:00–0:30 The development. What happened, where, who decided it. One sentence of context, no history lecture.
0:30–2:00 The view. One Tableau chart, built from data you collected at source, that puts the development in perspective — is it large, new, accelerating, concentrated?
2:00–3:00 The exposure. Through which channel does it reach your firm — market access, inputs, finance, assets and people, data and operations? How much of the firm sits in the path?
3:00–4:00 The recommendation. One decision, and the indicator and threshold that would make you change it.

One question follows (one minute), from the room or the instructor. Four briefs fill the first twenty minutes of the session.

The firm. Any real multinational with public information on its footprint (annual report, 10-K, sustainability report). It may — but need not — be your team’s firm.

The data. Any source in DATA-SOURCES.md or any other public source, cited with version and access date. Ideally the concept or dataset of a session you have already had.

E.5.2 What you hand in

Before class on the day: the .twbx (or a Tableau Public link) and your provenance log, on ZoneCours.

E.5.3 Rubric (15 points)

Criterion Points Full marks look like
The development 2 Precisely dated, sourced, and genuinely geopolitical (between states or political actors)
The view 4 Data collected at source; correct aggregation; title states the finding; source caption; the chart does work in the argument
The exposure 4 A specific channel; evidence from the firm’s own disclosures; an order of magnitude
The recommendation 3 One decision, an indicator and a threshold that would reverse it
Time and question 2 Four minutes, not six; answers the question asked

Automatic deductions: no source caption (−2); over five minutes (stopped at five); a development older than four weeks (−2).

E.6 Team work

Design the geopolitical risk assessment a real multinational should be running — build it, and defend the method behind it to its board.

Teams of three or four, formed at the start of Session 02. Each team adopts one named multinational for the whole term — no two teams the same firm.

E.6.1 What you deliver

Deliverable Points When
The dashboard — a Tableau Public workbook part of the 15 below one week after Session 12
The memo to the board — 2,500 words, plus appendices 15 (dashboard + memo together) one week after Session 12
The presentation and defence — 12 minutes + 8 minutes of questions 5 Session 12

E.6.1.1 The dashboard

At least four datasets collected in the practice rounds, from at least three of the six families of indicators (Session 01): event-based, text-based, expert-based, structural, market-based, firm-level. It must contain:

  1. The firm’s footprint — where its revenue, assets, suppliers and people are (from its own disclosures), as a map or a table.
  2. A composite exposure index for the firm’s main countries, built with the method of Session 07: indicators chosen and justified, normalised, weighted by parameters the board can move.
  3. At least two channel views — e.g. sanctions exposure (S10), dependence on satellite services (S11), bloc alignment of key markets (S04), cyber-incident exposure of its sector (S08).
  4. A watchlist — three indicators, each with a threshold that triggers a named decision.

E.6.1.2 The memo

Section Words Content
Executive summary 250 The three risks that matter, and what to do
The firm and its footprint 300 Where it is exposed, and through which channels
Method 700 Which indicators, why these, how combined, what each cannot see — the Session 07 discipline
Findings 800 What the dashboard shows, risk by risk, interaction by interaction
Recommendations and watchlist 450 Decisions, triggers, owners
Appendices — Provenance log for every dataset; data decisions; contribution statement

E.6.2 Rubric (20 points)

Criterion Points Full marks look like
Conceptual grounding 3 The risks are framed with the course’s concepts — order, anarchy, institutions, diplomacy, sanctions, interdependence — not newspaper vocabulary
Data and provenance 3 Four or more datasets collected at source, every one logged; decisions named
Method of the composite index 4 Indicators justified; normalisation and weighting explained; sensitivity to weights shown
Analysis of interactions 3 Shows how risks compound or offset — e.g. sanctions and input concentration and bloc alignment
Recommendations 2 Decisions with triggers and owners, not “monitor the situation”
Presentation and defence 5 Twelve minutes, the dashboard driven live, questions answered by any member

Individual accountability. Each memo ends with a contribution statement signed by all members. At the end of term each student submits a confidential peer rating (four dimensions, 1–5). The team mark is multiplied by an individual factor (default 1.00, range 0.80–1.05) only when the peer ratings and the instructor’s observation of the practice rounds independently agree.

E.7 Take-home examination

Three hours of work, within a 48-hour window, during the final examination period. Individual. Open book, open notes, open internet. No collaboration of any kind.

E.7.1 The format

At the opening of the window you receive, on ZoneCours:

  • a scenario — a geopolitical development, real or plausible, dated;
  • a firm — a real multinational, with an extract from its disclosures;
  • a data instruction — one or two sources from DATA-SOURCES.md to collect from, with the variables to use.

You hand in, before the window closes:

Part Points What
A · Concepts in context 12 Three short essays (≈ 300 words each) applying the course’s concepts to the scenario: e.g. what realism and liberal institutionalism each predict; whether sanctions in this case are likely to work; how the firm’s standards exposure changes
B · The evidence 14 One packaged Tableau workbook (.twbx) built from data you collected, with its provenance log, and a 400-word reading of it
C · The assessment 14 A 900-word memo to the firm’s chief risk officer: the exposure, the interactions between risks, the decision, the watchlist with thresholds

Length limits are strict. Words beyond the limit are not read.

E.7.2 Marking criteria

Criterion Weight
Correct and precise use of the course’s concepts 30%
Data collected at source, correctly prepared, honestly logged 25%
Analysis of interactions between risks and their implications for the firm 25%
A decision with triggers, defensible from the evidence 20%

E.7.3 On language models and integrity

You may use a language model to check syntax or to help you read a codebook. You may not use it to produce any text you submit. Every submission includes a one-paragraph statement of the tools you used and how. Undeclared use, or collaboration with another person, is a violation of HEC Montréal’s academic integrity regulations.

E.7.4 Specimen scenario

Dated 15 March. The European Union and the United States announce coordinated export controls on advanced battery-manufacturing equipment to a major Asian economy; that economy responds within a week with an export licensing regime on refined graphite.

Firm: a European automotive manufacturer with an EV assembly plant in Central Europe and a joint venture in the affected Asian economy.

Data instruction: IMF International Trade in Goods by partner country (XG_FOB_USD); the Global Sanctions Data Base; UN General Assembly ideal points (Bailey, Strezhnev & Voeten); the firm’s last annual report.

Part A (example): Using Farrell and Newman’s concept of weaponised interdependence, explain which side holds the stronger chokepoint and why. Part B: Build a view of goods trade between the EU, the United States and the affected economy since 2000, set against the sanctions cases in force between them and the distance between their UN voting positions. Part C: Advise the chief risk officer on the joint venture and on sourcing for the European plant.
