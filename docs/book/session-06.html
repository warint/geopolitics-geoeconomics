6  Private authority, standards and global regulation

Session 6 · Who writes the rules a multinational actually follows?
Pre-session slides · Lecture slides · Practice slides · Session folder · Data instructions

Most of the rules that govern a multinational’s daily operations were not written by a government. Who wrote them, with whose power — and does trade spread them up or down?

Big work Prakash, A., & Potoski, M. (2006). Racing to the bottom? Trade, environmental governance, and ISO 14001. American Journal of Political Science, 50(2), 350–364. doi:10.1111/j.1540-5907.2006.00188.x
Companion readings Abbott & Snidal (2009), The governance triangle · Mattli & Büthe (2003), Setting international standards · Brunsson, Rasche & Seidl (2012), The dynamics of standardization · Green (2013), Rethinking Private Authority
Target figure The diffusion of ISO 14001 across countries — a map and small multiples over time — and each country’s adoption against the adoption level of its export markets
Data you collect ISO Survey of certifications (ISO 14001, ISO 9001); IMF bilateral exports (IMTS); World Bank GDP (WDI)
Tableau skill Maps · geocoding by ISO code · fixing unmatched countries · the Pages shelf · small multiples
Course themes The impact and role of international institutions — here private and hybrid ones — in the geopolitical risks on which multinationals base their decisions

6.2 The lecture

6.2.1 1. The rules a firm actually follows

Session 05 looked at intergovernmental institutions. But walk through a multinational’s supply chain and count the rules it obeys: quality management (ISO 9001), environmental management (ISO 14001), information security (ISO/IEC 27001), forest and fisheries certification, labour codes of conduct, carbon accounting (the GHG Protocol), sustainability reporting standards, telecom technical specifications. Most were not written by a government. They are written by private or hybrid bodies, adopted voluntarily — and then made compulsory by buyers, investors and export markets.

That is private authority: the capacity of non-state actors to make rules that others treat as binding. For a multinational it is both a risk (a standard written elsewhere becomes a condition of market access) and a lever (a firm that shapes the standard shapes the market).

6.2.2 2. The governance triangle — Abbott and Snidal (2009)

Abbott and Snidal map regulatory standards-setting (RSS) schemes in a triangle whose corners are the three types of actor that can write rules:

                       STATE
                        /\
                       /  \
                      /    \
                     /      \
                    /        \
                   /__________\
                FIRM           NGO
  • Schemes near a corner are run by one actor type: an industry code of conduct (firms), an activist certification (NGOs), an intergovernmental rule (states).
  • Schemes in the middle are hybrids: firms and NGOs together (multi-stakeholder certification); states, firms and NGOs together (the UN Global Compact is often placed here).
  • The shadow of the state: even private schemes are shaped by the possibility that states will regulate if private actors do not — and by states choosing to endorse, require or ignore them.

Why it matters for a firm. Where a scheme sits predicts how it behaves: who can change it, how legitimate NGOs and governments will find it, and whether a state may one day make it mandatory.

6.2.3 3. Standards and power — Mattli and Büthe (2003)

Who wins when an international product standard is written at ISO or the IEC? Mattli and Büthe compare three explanations:

Explanation Prediction
Technological rationality The technically best solution wins, whoever proposes it
Primacy of power The standard favours the states with the largest markets
Institutional complementarity (their argument) Countries whose domestic standard-setting system is hierarchical and coordinated — matching the structure of ISO/IEC — are more effective at shaping international standards than countries with fragmented domestic systems

Their evidence points to institutional complementarity: the coordinated European national standards bodies were better placed than the fragmented, decentralised US system. The lesson: influence over “technical” rules depends on how a country — and its firms — are organised to take part.

Today, that is geopolitics. The competition over telecommunications standards, over battery and charging standards, over AI and data rules, is a competition between blocs, each trying to make its domestic rules the world’s. Bradford’s Brussels effect (2020) describes how EU rules become global because firms find it cheaper to adopt the strictest rule everywhere than to run two production lines.

6.2.4 4. Three perspectives on standardization — Brunsson, Rasche and Seidl (2012)

Brunsson and colleagues organise research on standards around three aspects:

  • standardization of organizations — standards that tell firms how to be organised (management system standards such as ISO 14001);
  • standardization by organizations — the bodies that write standards and how they work;
  • standardization as organization — standards as a way of organising markets and fields without formal hierarchy.

Their key point is that standards, usually associated with stability and sameness, are dynamic: they are revised, contested, translated locally, decoupled from practice. A firm “certified” to ISO 14001 may run its plants exactly as before — the certificate and the practice can diverge.

6.2.5 5. Delegated or entrepreneurial — Green (2013)

Green distinguishes two sources of private authority:

Delegated Entrepreneurial
Origin States hand rule-making to private actors Private actors create rules on their own, and others adopt them
Illustration (carbon governance, Green’s field) Private bodies accrediting and verifying projects under the Kyoto Protocol’s Clean Development Mechanism Voluntary carbon accounting and offset standards, such as the GHG Protocol
What a firm should watch The state that delegated can take it back Adoption — the rule is as strong as the number of users

6.2.6 6. Race to the bottom, or trading up? — Prakash and Potoski (2006)

The worry. Trade intensifies competition; governments lower environmental standards to attract investment and win exports — a race to the bottom.

The alternative. Vogel’s (1995) California effect: exporters to strict markets adopt strict standards, and trade spreads them — trading up.

The test. ISO 14001 is a voluntary environmental management standard that goes beyond what many domestic laws require. Prakash and Potoski use a panel of 108 countries over seven years and ask whether adoption in a country depends on adoption in its export markets.

The finding. Trade linkages encourage ISO 14001 adoption when a country’s major export markets have themselves adopted it. Trade spreads private standards upward — towards the standards of the customers.

For the firm. A private standard can become a de facto condition of access to a market without a single law changing. Monitoring “regulatory” risk means monitoring what your customers’ home markets adopt.

6.2.7 7. The Tableau skill of the day — maps and Pages

  • Give iso3 the geographic role Country/Region (right-click → Geographic Role). Tableau recognises ISO 3166 codes; check the “unknown” indicator at the bottom right of the map and fix what it could not place (Map → Edit Locations).
  • Filled map of certificates per billion US$ of GDP; colour on a sequential palette, never a rainbow.
  • Year on the Pages shelf: a play button animates the diffusion.

6.3 In the practice

The target. Prakash & Potoski (2006): ISO 14001 spreads across countries, and a country adopts more when its export markets have adopted more. Two views: the diffusion (a map and small multiples over time), and the trade channel (own adoption against export-market adoption).

In your teams. One workbook per team; everyone builds at least one sheet.

6.3.1 1 · Collect — 10 min

Your three files should already be in data/raw/ (../data/README.md). Finish the provenance log:

file source url version / release accessed licence notes
iso_survey_14001.xlsx or iso_survey_transcribed.xlsx ISO / IAF CertSearch … survey edition date free / account full tables or transcription; coverage changes?
imf_imts_exports.csv IMF data.imf.org — IMTS download date date free exports FOB, US$; partner codes used
wdi_gdp.csv World Bank api.worldbank.org/… last updated date CC BY 4.0

6.3.2 2 · Prepare — 20 min

  1. ISO table. If years are columns, pivot them (select the year columns → right-click → Pivot), rename to Year and Certificates. Add or check iso3. Keep 14001 and 9001 as two columns, or as a Standard dimension — your choice, logged.

  2. WDI GDP. Pivot the year columns too. Keep Country Code (it is ISO3) and the value. From the Metadata_Country_… file in the same zip, bring in Region, and drop the aggregates (World, regions, income groups): they are the rows with an empty Region.

  3. Join ISO to GDP on iso3 and Year (both join clauses). Check the row count.

  4. Calculated field:

    [ISO 14001 per $bn GDP] = [Certificates 14001] / ([GDP current US$] / 1e9)

  5. Log: which countries did not match, and why.

6.3.3 3 · Replicate — 30 min

6.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “ISO 14001 spread” — a map

  1. iso3 → Geographic Role → Country/Region; double-click it. Check the unknown indicator, bottom right; fix with Map → Edit Locations.
  2. Mark type Map; [ISO 14001 per $bn GDP] → Colour, sequential palette. Consider a log-like scale: the distribution is very skewed (try Edit Colors → Stepped with 5 steps).
  3. Year → Pages. Press play. Title it with what you see.

6.3.3.2 Sheet 2 · “Diffusion by region” — small multiples

Year → Columns (continuous), Region → Rows, MEDIAN([ISO 14001 per $bn GDP]) → Rows. One small line per region. Which region adopted first? Which caught up?

6.3.3.3 Sheet 3 · “The pull of export markets” — the trade channel

This is the core of Prakash and Potoski: does a country’s adoption track its export markets’ adoption?

  1. New data source. Start from imf_imts_exports.csv (reporter × partner × year × exports). In the IMF layout reporter and partner are COUNTRY and COUNTERPART_COUNTRY, in ISO3 codes (rename them Reporter iso3 and Partner iso3; TIME_PERIOD → Year, OBS_VALUE → Exports). Drop aggregate partners (codes starting with G or TX). If your download shows names instead, build a concordance. Kosovo is KOS at the IMF but XKX at the World Bank.

  2. Join the ISO + GDP table to it twice (Tableau lets you add the same table again; rename the copies):

    • as Partner: Partner iso3 = iso3 and Year = Year
    • as Reporter: Reporter iso3 = iso3 and Year = Year

  3. Calculated fields:

    [Weighted partner adoption]  = [Exports] * [Partner ISO 14001 per $bn GDP]

[Export-market adoption]     = SUM([Weighted partner adoption]) / SUM([Exports])

[Own adoption]               = { FIXED [Reporter iso3], [Year] :
                                 AVG([Reporter ISO 14001 per $bn GDP]) }

    The FIXED matters: the reporter’s own value is repeated on every partner row, so it must not be summed.

  4. Scatter: [Export-market adoption] → Columns, AVG([Own adoption]) → Rows, Reporter iso3 → Detail, Year → Colour (or filter to one year). Log scales on both axes (right-click axis → Edit Axis → Logarithmic). Add a trend line (Analytics → Trend Line → Linear) — read it as a description, not a test: Session 09 returns to what its R² means.

6.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 20 min

  1. Differ. Your scatter versus the paper’s argument. The paper estimates a panel with controls; you have drawn a cloud. What can your cloud show, and what can it not?
  2. The placebo. Rebuild Sheet 3 with ISO 9001 — the quality standard, demanded by buyers everywhere, with no environmental content. If the export-market pull is just as strong, what does that say about why firms certify to 14001?
  3. Break the weighting. Replace the export-weighted average with an unweighted average of partners (AVG instead of the weighted ratio). Does the relationship survive? Why should weighting matter for the California-effect argument?
  4. Break the normalisation. Raw counts instead of per $bn GDP on Sheet 1. Which countries jump to the top — and why is that misleading?

6.3.5 5 · The catch — 10 min

Original Ours The catch
Prakash and Potoski’s finding Your Sheet 3 (and Sheet 1) What it does not license a multinational to conclude — for instance that a certified supplier is environmentally better, or that adoption caused export success

A member drawn at random presents in two minutes.

6.3.6 Hand in

.twbx + provenance log on ZoneCours. Formative; counts towards participation.

The one rule: your log records at least one place where the data disagreed with the paper.

Team project. Which private standards do your firm’s buyers or home market require? Which of its key countries are below the median adoption of its export markets? Note it for the dashboard.
