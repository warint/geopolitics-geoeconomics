1.2.2 2. Risk, uncertainty, and three kinds of risk

Risk versus uncertainty. Knight (1921) separated risk — outcomes whose probabilities can be estimated — from uncertainty, where they cannot. Most geopolitical events sit uncomfortably between the two: wars and coups recur, but each is unlike the last. Everything in this course is an attempt to move a question from the second category towards the first, and to be honest about how far it moves.

Question it answers Typical unit Example
Political risk Can the government of the host country change the rules on me? firm × host country expropriation, contract renegotiation, sudden tax, licence withdrawal
Country risk Will this country pay, convert and transfer? country sovereign default, capital controls, currency inconvertibility
Geopolitical risk Will relations between states and political actors move against me? the international system, or a dyad war, sanctions, export controls, bloc formation, state-sponsored cyber operations

Kobrin (1979) made the classic distinction between macro risk, which hits every foreign firm in a country, and micro risk, which hits one sector or one firm. Geopolitical risk adds a third layer: risk that originates outside the host country — in Washington, Beijing, Brussels or Moscow — and reaches the firm through its suppliers, customers, banks or networks.

1.2.3 3. Six families of indicators

Family What it counts Examples you will use Strength Blind spot
Event-based Things that happened, coded from reports UCDP, cyber-incident data (S08), sanctions episodes (S10) Concrete, datable Only what was reported and coded
Text-based How much the press or firms talk about a risk GPR (today), EPU, firm-level political risk (S07) Timely, long, high frequency Measures attention, not exposure
Expert-based Scores assigned by analysts or surveys World Governance Indicators, commercial country-risk ratings Covers latent concepts Opaque, sticky, perception-driven
Structural Slow-moving features of the system Material capabilities (S03), trade dependence (S04, S11) Explains why Rarely tells you when
Market-based Prices that embed risk sovereign CDS spreads, implied volatility Forward-looking, daily Mixes risk with risk appetite
Firm-level Risk as perceived by firms themselves earnings-call text (S07), disclosed exposures Speaks to the decision Self-reported, listed firms only

A good risk assessment — the team project — triangulates across at least three families. The quarrel between them is informative: when the text says calm and the market says danger, someone knows something.

1.2.4 4. The GPR index: how it is built

Caldara and Iacoviello (2022) define geopolitical risk as the threat, realisation and escalation of adverse events associated with wars, terrorism and any tensions among states and political actors that affect the peaceful course of international relations.

The construction, in four steps:

  1. Corpus. The electronic archives of ten leading newspapers (US, UK and Canadian) for the recent index from 1985; three US newspapers for the historical index from 1900.
  2. Dictionary. Articles are counted if they contain words from eight categories — war threats, peace threats, military build-ups, nuclear threats, terror threats, beginning of war, escalation of war, terror acts — usually in combination with words that pin the article to geopolitics.
  3. Share. For each month, the number of GPR articles divided by the total number of articles. Dividing by the total removes the effect of newspapers growing or shrinking.
  4. Normalisation. The share is rescaled so that the 1985–2019 average equals 100.

Threats versus acts. The first five categories form GPR Threats, the last three GPR Acts. The distinction matters to a firm: the paper finds that threats, not only realised acts, depress investment.

Validation. The authors audit samples of articles by hand, compare the index with historical narrative, and show it spikes at the events it should — the World Wars, Korea, the Cuban missile crisis, the Gulf War, 9/11, the 2003 invasion of Iraq, the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine.

Country-specific indices. For several dozen countries, the same counts are restricted to articles that also mention the country — a measure of how much the anglophone press associates that country with geopolitical tension.

1.2.5 5. Why a firm should care

The real-options channel. When an investment is costly to reverse and the future is uncertain, waiting has value (Bernanke, 1983; Bloom, 2009). A GPR spike raises the value of waiting: firms postpone hiring, capital expenditure and entry. Caldara and Iacoviello find that higher GPR predicts lower investment and employment, and that the effect is stronger in industries more exposed to geopolitical risk.

Five channels from the system to the P&L, which return all term:

Channel Example Session
Market access tariffs, bloc-based trade diversion 04
Inputs a critical service from one satellite operator 11
Finance and payments sanctions on banks, asset freezes 10
Assets and people conflict, expropriation, evacuation 03, 05
Data and operations state-sponsored cyber operations 08

1.2.6 6. What GPR does not tell you

  • It measures attention in the anglophone press, not the risk faced by a subsidiary in Abidjan or a supplier in Shenzhen.
  • It is global: one number for the world. The country indices help, but only for countries the newspapers write about.
  • Attention fades even while risk persists: a frozen conflict may be a constant risk and a falling index.
  • It is not a forecast. It rises with an event; it does not tell you the next one.

That list is the template for “the catch” — the last step of every practice round.

1.3 In the practice

The target. Caldara & Iacoviello (2022), Figure 1: the monthly GPR index since 1985, its spikes labelled with the events behind them. Then its historical counterpart since 1900.

Teams are not yet formed: today you work in pairs. Everyone builds their own workbook.

1.3.1 1 · Collect — 15 min

If you did the pre-session, data_gpr_export.xls is already in data/raw/. If not, download it now from matteoiacoviello.com/gpr.htm. Details: ../data/README.md.

Start your provenance log — copy templates/provenance-log.csv into data/ and fill one line:

file source url version / release accessed licence notes
data_gpr_export.xls Caldara & Iacoviello matteoiacoviello.com/gpr.htm the “last updated” date on the page today free, cite AER 2022 anything odd

1.3.2 2 · Prepare — 15 min

  1. Tableau Desktop → Connect → Microsoft Excel → data_gpr_export.xls.
  2. Drag the data sheet (Sheet1) to the canvas. Look at the grid at the bottom. The last two columns, var_name and var_label, are the authors’ notes, not data.
  3. Check the type icon of month. It must be a date (calendar). If it is text (Abc) or a date-time, click the icon and change it to Date.
  4. Check GPR, GPRT, GPRA, GPRH are numbers (#). Empty cells in early years of the recent index are expected: it starts in 1985.
  5. Write in your log what Tableau got wrong on its own, if anything.

1.3.3 3 · Replicate — 30 min

1.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “GPR since 1985”

  1. month → Columns. Right-click the pill → choose the continuous Month (green pill, the second “Month” in the menu — the one showing May 2015, not May).
  2. GPR → Rows. Keep the aggregation SUM — there is one row per month, so the sum is the value.
  3. Filter month to January 1985 onward.
  4. Analytics pane → drag Reference Line onto the view → Table → Constant 100, label “1985–2019 average = 100”.
  5. Find the five highest peaks. Right-click each → Annotate → Point and write the event: Gulf War (1991), 9/11 (2001), Iraq invasion (2003), Paris attacks (2015), Russia invades Ukraine
    1. — but check the months yourself; do not trust this list.
  6. Title the sheet the way the paper would, and put the source in a caption: Source: Caldara & Iacoviello (2022), matteoiacoviello.com, accessed …

1.3.3.2 Sheet 2 · “GPR since 1900”

Duplicate Sheet 1, replace GPR with GPRH, remove the 1985 filter. Relabel the reference line: GPRH is normalised to its 1900–2019 average = 100, not 1985–2019. Annotate the two World Wars, Korea and the Cuban missile crisis.

1.3.3.3 Sheet 3 · “Threats and acts”

Put GPRT and GPRA on the same axis: drag GPRA onto the GPRT axis until the double-ruler icon appears (Measure Values). Colour by Measure Names.

1.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 20 min

Put your Sheet 1 beside Figure 1 of the paper.

  1. Where do they differ? Your series runs further: the index is updated monthly, and the authors also revise it. Are past values exactly the paper’s? Write down one difference.
  2. Threats versus acts. Find one episode where threats rose long before acts did. What would a firm watching only acts have missed?
  3. Break one decision. Replace the monthly view by a yearly average (change the pill to continuous Year, aggregation AVG). Which spikes disappear? What does that tell you about the frequency at which a risk dashboard should run?
  4. Extend. Add a GPRC_ country index for a country of your choice (Sheet 4). It is a percent of articles, not an index to 100: use a dual axis. Is the country index correlated with the global one? When does it diverge?
