9.2 The lecture

The session opens with four Risk Briefs (20 min). The lecture follows.

9.2.1 1. What economic diplomacy was, and what it has become

Economic diplomacy is the use of the instruments of foreign policy for economic ends — and of economic instruments for foreign-policy ends. In the post-1990 order (Session 02) it was mostly the first: negotiate trade agreements, open markets, promote exports, attract investment. Diplomats served firms.

Three shifts have redefined it:

Shift What changed Consequence for the firm
Securitisation Trade, investment and technology are now treated as matters of national security Firms face screening, export controls and “trusted vendor” rules from their own side
From promotion to coercion The same networks used to promote exports are used to deny access (Session 08’s chokepoints; Session 10’s sanctions) Home-state support can become home-state constraint overnight
Digitalisation Diplomacy is conducted in public, on social media, aimed at domestic and foreign publics at once Firms are drawn into public diplomatic quarrels — boycotts, hashtags, official denunciations

9.2.2 2. Putnam: two-level games

Putnam (1988) models international negotiation as two simultaneous games:

  • Level I — negotiators bargain with each other to reach a tentative agreement;
  • Level II — each negotiator’s domestic constituencies decide whether to ratify it.

The win-set is the set of Level I agreements that would be ratified at Level II. Three lessons:

  1. Larger win-sets make agreement more likely — but leave a negotiator open to being pushed.
  2. A small win-set is a bargaining asset: “my parliament will never accept that” is credible when it is true.
  3. Negotiators play both boards at once: they try to shape the other side’s Level II and to manage their own.

For the firm. Multinationals are Level II actors in several countries at once. An export industry that lobbies for a trade deal enlarges the win-set at home; a firm exposed abroad may be a hostage at the other side’s Level II.

9.2.3 3. Bjola and Manor: the two-level game goes digital

Bjola and Manor (2018) revisit Putnam through the negotiations over the Iran nuclear agreement (2015). Their argument: digital diplomacy — ministries and leaders communicating directly with publics on social media — gives negotiators a new tool to act on Level II, their own and the other side’s. Governments used digital channels to build domestic support for ratification and to reach the counterpart’s public, reshaping win-sets during the negotiation itself.

Two implications for multinationals:

  • Speed. Domestic opinion now moves within the negotiation, not after it — so the regulatory outcome a firm plans for can change faster.
  • Exposure. A firm’s brand can be pulled into a digital campaign by either government.

9.2.4 4. Rose: embassies as export promotion

Rose (2007) asks whether the foreign service earns its keep. He combines the number of embassies and consulates that a set of exporting countries keep abroad with their bilateral exports, in a gravity framework that controls for the size of and distance between partners. His finding: exports are higher where a country keeps more posts, and the association survives the standard gravity controls. He reports that an additional consulate is associated with a further rise in exports of several percent — read the size off his tables, and note which kind of post matters most.

Three objections, which the practice round tests:

Objection The worry How to probe it
Reverse causality Countries open posts where they already trade Do posts predict future trade growth, or only follow past trade?
Omitted variables Large, rich, close partners get more posts and more trade Divide trade by partner GDP; colour by distance or region
Measurement A post is not a budget; an honorary consulate is not an embassy Separate embassies from consulates

Rose takes reverse causality seriously and argues that the evidence is consistent with posts having an effect, but a cross-section cannot settle it. That is the catch of the day.

9.2.5 5. The firm and the state: bargaining, screening, and diplomacy by firms

Triangular diplomacy. Stopford and Strange (1991) argued that diplomacy is no longer only state–state: it is also state–firm (governments bargain with multinationals over investment) and firm–firm (alliances). Vernon’s obsolescing bargain (1971) adds the dynamic: once a firm’s capital is sunk, its bargaining power falls.

Investment screening is economic diplomacy aimed at firms: the US Committee on Foreign Investment (CFIUS), the EU framework regulation on FDI screening (Regulation 2019/452), and the national-security review under the Investment Canada Act. Screening has widened from defence to data, critical minerals, infrastructure and technology.

Commercial diplomacy — the toolbox:

Instrument Example of the type
Embassies and consulates trade commissioners, market intelligence, introductions
Trade missions and state visits ministers travelling with business delegations
Export credit and insurance export credit agencies covering political risk
Investment promotion agencies incentives, aftercare
Treaties investment treaties with dispute settlement

When to lean on the home state — and when that is itself a risk: in a dispute, home-state backing helps; in a confrontation between states, being seen as an instrument of one may make the firm a target of the other.

9.2.6 6. The Tableau skill of the day

9.3 In the practice

The target. Rose (2007)’s central relationship: for each exporter–partner pair, exports against the number of diplomatic posts the exporter keeps in the partner — on log scales, with a trend line — rebuilt with the Lowy Global Diplomacy Index (2023 data) and IMF trade.

Work in your project teams. Everyone builds their own workbook; the team hands in one slide.

9.3.1 1 · Collect — 15 min

In data/raw/ (see ../data/README.md):

  • DataDownload.xlsx — Lowy Global Diplomacy Index;
  • IMF IMTS — exports of goods, FOB, US$, 2023, for Canada and at least four other Lowy countries × all partners;
  • WDI NY.GDP.MKTP.CD — GDP, current US$.

Provenance log — one line per file, including which side of the trade flow you used (exporter- reported exports) and the IMF indicator name exactly as shown.

9.3.2 2 · Prepare — 25 min

This is the hard part today. Take your time.

  1. Lowy. Connect to DataDownload.xlsx, sheet 2023. The columns for sending country, host country and post type are COUNTRY, POST COUNTRY and TYPE OF POST. Filter out posts that are not bilateral: TYPE OF POST = Permanent Mission (missions to the UN, EU, OECD…). Look at what Other holds (representative and trade offices, e.g. in Taipei) and log your choice.

  2. IMF. Add the IMF file as a second table on the canvas. Keep only the five exporters and the year 2023; drop aggregate partners (“World”, regional totals — codes starting with G or TX). Rename OBS_VALUE → Exports.

  3. Concordance. Lowy has country names only; the IMF file has ISO3 codes (COUNTRY, COUNTERPART_COUNTRY). Make a small sheet name → ISO3 for the Lowy COUNTRY and POST COUNTRY names (watch the typos listed in the data README) and add ISO3 to Lowy.

  4. Relate, don’t join. Drag the IMF table first, then the Lowy table, and create a relationship on exporter ISO3 = sending ISO3 and partner ISO3 = host ISO3. A relationship keeps the export totals at their own grain; a plain join would repeat each export value once per post and inflate it.

  5. Build the zeros. Calculated field Posts:

    ZN(COUNT([POST CITY]))

    (POST CITY is never empty in the 2023 sheet). Partners with exports but no post now get 0.

  6. Make logging possible. Calculated field Posts + 1 = [Posts] + 1. Write this decision in the log: it is the same compromise most gravity papers make, and it has consequences.

  7. Exports per partner GDP. Add WDI GDP (relationship on partner ISO3, year 2023) and create Exports / partner GDP (‰) = SUM([Exports]) / SUM([GDP]) * 1000.

9.3.3 3 · Replicate — 20 min

9.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “More posts, more exports”

  1. Posts + 1 → Columns (as a measure; aggregation SUM at the dyad level).
  2. Exports → Rows.
  3. Exporter ISO3 and partner ISO3 → Detail — one mark per pair.
  4. Exporter → Colour.
  5. Right-click each axis → Edit Axis → Logarithmic.
  6. Analytics → Trend Line → Linear. Right-click the line → Describe Trend Model. Note the slope, R² and p-value (one line per exporter if colour splits them — try both ways).
  7. Title states the finding; caption cites Lowy (2024 edition, 2023 data) and IMF IMTS, with access dates.

9.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 20 min

  1. Differ. Rose used an earlier period and a different set of exporters. Is your slope steeper or flatter than the relationship he reports? Name one reason.
  2. Break: size. Replace Exports by Exports / partner GDP (‰). What happens to the slope and the R²? What did the raw chart owe to the sheer size of partners?
  3. Break: type of post. Filter TYPE OF POST to Embassy / High Commission only, then to Consulate / Consulate-General only. Which carries the relationship? Does that match Rose?
  4. Break: the zeros. Exclude partners with Posts = 0. How much does the trend line move? Why should a careful analyst never drop them silently?
  5. Extend — reverse causality (fast teams). Add the Lowy 2017 sheet and IMF exports for
    1. For each pair, compute the change in posts and the growth of exports 2017→2023. Do pairs that gained posts show faster export growth — or did posts open where exports had already grown? What would you need to tell the two apart?

9.3.5 5 · The catch — 10 min

Original Ours The catch
Rose’s posts–exports relationship Your Sheet 1 One sentence: what this trend line does not license a multinational — or a foreign ministry — to conclude

A member drawn at random presents in two minutes.

9.3.6 Hand in

On ZoneCours before leaving: .twbx and provenance log, including your concordance fixes. Formative; counts towards participation.

The one rule: your log records at least one place where the data disagreed with the paper.

9.3.7 What loses credit

  • A join that inflated exports, unnoticed (check one pair’s total against the IMF file)
  • Zeros dropped without saying so
  • “R² = 0.6, so embassies explain 60% of exports” — they explain nothing; they co-vary
  • Unmatched countries silently missing from the chart
