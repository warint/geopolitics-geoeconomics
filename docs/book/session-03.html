3.1.1 1. The reading — 60–75 min

Allison, G. (2017). Destined for War: Can America and China Escape Thucydides’s Trap? Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. The Introduction and Chapter 2 (Athens vs. Sparta), and the appendix listing the sixteen cases.

Annotate four things:

What to look for
1 Mearsheimer’s five assumptions — and which one does the most work
2 How each author measures power: what counts, what is left out
3 Allison’s sixteen cases — how many ended in war, and how a case was chosen
4 One claim a multinational with operations in both the US and China should take seriously, and one it should doubt

  4. Allison counts how many cases of a rising power challenging a ruling one, and how many ended in war? Write the numbers down — then write one reason to doubt the ratio.

3.2 The lecture

The session opens with four Risk Briefs (20 minutes); the lecture takes the next seventy.

3.2.1 1. Anarchy: the starting point

In international relations, anarchy means the absence of a government above states — no world authority that can make rules binding, enforce contracts or guarantee survival. It does not mean chaos or constant war: most of the time, most states are at peace and trade under rules.

What follows from anarchy, in the realist tradition:

  • Self-help. Each state must ultimately ensure its own survival.
  • Uncertainty about intentions. A state cannot know what another will do with its power tomorrow.
  • Relative gains. Because today’s partner may be tomorrow’s adversary, states care not only what they gain from cooperation but how much more the other gains (Grieco, 1988).

Why a firm should care. Relative-gains thinking is why trade becomes a security question: a deal that makes both richer but the rival richer faster may be refused, restricted or reversed. It is the logic behind export controls on advanced semiconductors.

3.2.2 2. Two realisms

Defensive realism — Waltz (1979), Theory of International Politics. The structure of the system — anarchy plus the distribution of capabilities — shapes behaviour. States seek an appropriate amount of power: enough for security. Too much provokes balancing coalitions, so expansion is usually self-defeating. Waltz judged bipolar systems more stable than multipolar ones: fewer miscalculations.

The security dilemma (Jervis, 1978). What one state does to make itself secure — arming, alliances — makes others less secure, and they respond. Both end up worse off without anyone intending aggression.

Offensive realism — Mearsheimer (2001). Five bedrock assumptions:

  1. The international system is anarchic.
  2. Great powers possess some offensive military capability.
  3. States can never be certain of others’ intentions.
  4. Survival is the primary goal of great powers.
  5. Great powers are rational actors.

From these, Mearsheimer derives that great powers maximise relative power; the ideal is to be the regional hegemon and to prevent any other region from having one. His prediction for China: it will try to dominate Asia as the United States dominates the Western Hemisphere, and the United States will try to stop it. “China cannot rise peacefully.”

Defensive (Waltz) Offensive (Mearsheimer)
How much power do states want? enough for security as much as possible, relative to others
What restrains them? balancing by others only the limits of their capabilities
Expected US–China outcome managed competition intense security competition

3.2.3 3. Power transition and the Thucydides’s Trap

Power transition theory (Organski, 1958; Organski & Kugler, 1980) reverses Waltz’s emphasis: hierarchy, not balance, keeps the peace. A dominant power sets the international order. War is most likely when a dissatisfied challenger approaches parity with the dominant power — a threshold often operationalised as the challenger reaching around 80% of the leader’s capabilities.

Allison (2017). “It was the rise of Athens and the fear that this instilled in Sparta that made war inevitable” (Thucydides). Allison and the Harvard Thucydides’s Trap project identify sixteen cases in five hundred years in which a rising power challenged a ruling one; twelve ended in war.

The critics.

  • Case selection. The cases are chosen on the outcome of interest; peaceful transitions (the UK to the US) may be under-counted; the definition of “rising” and “ruling” is elastic.
  • Nuclear weapons change the expected cost of great-power war in a way no pre-1945 case can capture.
  • Interdependence. The US and China are bound by trade and finance far more tightly than Athens and Sparta, or Britain and Germany — though Britain and Germany were also major trading partners in 1914 (Session 04 takes this up).
  • Measuring “rise”. Beckley (2018) argues gross indicators — GDP, population, CINC — overstate China’s power because they ignore the costs of governing, feeding and protecting a population.

3.2.4 4. Measuring power: the CINC

The Correlates of War Composite Index of National Capability (Singer, Bremer & Stuckey, 1972):

  1. Take six indicators for every state in the system each year: total population, urban population, iron and steel production, primary energy consumption, military personnel, military expenditure.
  2. For each indicator, compute the state’s share of the system total that year.
  3. Average the six shares. CINC is a number between 0 and 1; the system sums to about 1 (not exactly: a state missing a component is averaged over the ones it has).

What it rewards. Size and heavy industry. Two of six components are population and two are smokestack industry. CINC therefore shows China passing the United States much earlier than military spending or GDP at market exchange rates do. One reality, three measures, three dates: the break step of the practice round is to see it.

What it misses. Technology, quality of forces, alliances, financial power, control of networks — the dollar, SWIFT, the cloud — which Session 08 and Session 11 show to be the instruments of coercion that reach firms most directly.

3.2.5 5. What it means for the multinational

The balance of power sets the rules of trade Who can impose sanctions, export controls and market-access conditions depends on who holds capabilities others need
A transition changes which rules you follow A firm in both the US and China increasingly faces two compliance regimes — and penalties for following one in the other’s jurisdiction
Relative gains turn commerce into security Products that make a rival stronger — chips, AI, batteries, aerospace — move from the trade ministry to the security council
The date of the transition depends on the measure Board slides that say “China has overtaken the US” should say on what
Security dilemmas escalate without intent A defensive move by one side (an export control, a military exercise) is read as offensive and triggers a response that reaches your supply chain

3.2.6 6. What the figure does not license

  • CINC is a measure of material capabilities, not of influence, intentions or likelihood of war. A crossover on the chart is not a prediction of conflict.
  • It overweights size: population and heavy industry. It cannot see semiconductors, alliances or finance.
  • It ends in 2022: the most recent shifts are not in it.
  • It describes states, not markets: a firm’s exposure depends on where its revenue and suppliers sit, not on the system’s distribution of power.

3.3 In the practice

The target. The shares of world material capabilities (CINC) of the major powers, 1816–2022 — the picture behind Mearsheimer’s and Allison’s argument — and the year China passes the United States. Then: the same transition measured two other ways, dated two other years.

Work in your team. Everyone builds their own workbook; the team hands in one.

3.3.1 1 · Collect — 15 min

NMC v7.0 and the SIPRI workbook should already be in data/raw/; your Session 02 WDI extract too. If not: ../data/README.md.

Provenance log: three lines — NMC (version 7.0, and the codebook’s date), SIPRI (the file name gives the release), WDI (reused; same version as Session 02).

3.3.2 2 · Prepare — 20 min

3.3.2.1 NMC

  1. Connect → Text file → NMC-70-abridged.csv (from NMC-v7-abridged.zip, inside NMCv7.zip). Drag it to the canvas.
  2. Check the columns against the codebook. In v7.0 they are unchanged from v6.0: stateabb (three letters), ccode (number), year, the six components milex, milper, irst, pec, tpop, upop, then cinc and version. Missing component values are coded -9: before you use any component, exclude them (a filter >= 0), or every sum is wrong.
  3. year should be a number (whole), not a date: keep it that way for now.
  4. Sanity check before you build anything: new sheet, year → Columns (continuous), SUM(cinc) → Rows, no filter. Every year should sum to about 1 — in v7.0, between 1.000 and about 1.075. Which years are furthest from 1, and why? (The codebook’s CINC section explains it: the average is taken over the components a state has.)

3.3.2.2 SIPRI

  1. Connect → Microsoft Excel → the SIPRI workbook. Use the Constant (2024) US$ sheet (US$ millions, constant 2024 prices).
  2. Tick Use Data Interpreter: the sheet has five rows of notes above the header (Country, an unnamed column, Notes, 1949 … 2025). Region headings (Africa, North Africa, …) remain as rows with no values: filter them out.
  3. Pivot the year columns (as in Session 02). Rename to Year and Milex. Make Year a whole number with the same name as NMC’s year field — it will be the link.
  4. Milex arrives as text: SIPRI writes ... for unavailable data and xxx for a country that did not exist (the notes at the top of the sheet; the Footnotes sheet explains the † § ¶ marks in Notes). Change to Number (decimal) and check what became null.
  5. SIPRI identifies countries by name, not code. Write down the exact strings for China, the United States and Russia. This is a provenance-log entry.

3.3.3 3 · Replicate — 25 min

3.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “Shares of world capabilities, 1816–2022”

  1. year → Columns (continuous); SUM(cinc) → Rows.
  2. stateabb → Colour. Filter to the major powers: USA, UKG, FRN, GMY, RUS, JPN, CHN, ITA — check each code in the COW state list (COW-country-codes.csv; see ../data/README.md). Russia/USSR is one continuous series (RUS). Germany is not: GMY stops in 1945 and returns in 1991, with GFR and GDR in between. JPN also has a gap (1946–1951). Look for them, and decide what your line does there.
  3. Highlight the US and China (strong colours), grey the rest.
  4. Annotate 1945, 1991, and the year China passes the US.
  5. Title with the finding; caption: Correlates of War, NMC v7.0 (Singer, Bremer & Stuckey 1972), accessed ….

3.3.3.2 Sheet 2 · “How close is the challenger?”

  1. Create → Parameter Challenger: data type String, List, Add values from stateabb. Default CHN.

  2. Calculated fields:

    Challenger CINC = IF [stateabb] = [Challenger] THEN [cinc] END
US CINC         = IF [stateabb] = "USA" THEN [cinc] END
Power ratio     = SUM([Challenger CINC]) / SUM([US CINC])

  3. year → Columns; Power ratio → Rows. Right-click the parameter → Show Parameter.

  4. Analytics → Reference Line: constant 1 (“parity”) and constant 0.8 (“power transition zone”, Organski & Kugler).

  5. Switch the parameter to GMY, JPN and RUS. When did each enter the transition zone against the US — or against the UK, if you also make the leader a parameter?

3.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 20 min

  1. Compare. Put Sheet 1 beside the readings’ claims. In which year does China’s CINC first exceed the US’s in your data? Does that date surprise you, given what you read?
  2. Break — change the measure of power. Build two more ratios, China ÷ US:
    • military expenditure from SIPRI (constant dollars), and
    • GDP from your WDI extract (current US$ and PPP).
    Put them on one sheet with the CINC ratio by blending on the year: on a sheet that uses NMC, drag a SIPRI field onto the view; check that the link icon is active on the year field (Data → Edit Blend Relationships if Tableau did not find it). Blending links fields only by name and type: if the year is year in NMC, Year in SIPRI and Year (n) in your Session 02 WDI extract, rename them to the same name (right-click → Rename) and make all three whole numbers before you blend, or use Edit Blend Relationships → Custom to pair them by hand. Use a dual axis (right-click the second axis → Dual Axis → Synchronize Axis) so both ratios share one scale, with the parity line at 1.
  3. Read it. Three measures, three transition dates (or no transition yet). Which date belongs in a board presentation, and for which decision?
  4. Extend. Use the NMC components: which of the six drives China’s CINC? Build one component’s share (e.g. iron and steel) with a percent-of-total table calculation computed across states, restarting every year.

3.3.7 What loses credit

  • SUM of a ratio across years or states
  • “China overtook the US in …” without saying on what measure
  • A catch about the theory rather than about what the firm may conclude
