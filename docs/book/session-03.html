3  Anarchy and the Balance of Power: Who Sets the Terms When No One Governs

3.1 A system with no government

The previous chapter described the international economic order as a set of rules, written by particular states and held in place by something. This chapter asks what that something is when it is not law. Inside a country, a contract is enforced by courts, and the courts are backed by a state that holds a monopoly of legitimate force. Between countries there is no such state. There is no world government that can make a rule binding, enforce a contract between two governments, or guarantee that a state which disarms will not be invaded. That absence is the starting point of the realist tradition in international relations, and it is the reason this book cannot treat geopolitics as a branch of regulation.

The question that follows is direct. In a system with no government above states, who sets the terms of trade — and how would a multinational know that the balance is shifting? The realist answer to the first half is blunt: the terms are set, in the end, by those who hold the capabilities others need, and they are revised when the distribution of capabilities changes. The second half is a measurement problem, and the chapter answers it with two centuries of data on the distribution of material capabilities among the major powers, asking when, and on what measure, China overtook the United States.

The chapter defines anarchy; sets out the defensive and offensive realist theories; turns to power transition theory and Graham Allison’s “Thucydides’s Trap”; and examines how power is measured, and what the standard measure rewards and misses.

3.2 Anarchy, and what it does not mean

Anarchy — in international relations, the absence of any authority above states capable of making binding rules, enforcing agreements between them, or guaranteeing their survival. It describes the structure of the system, not the level of violence within it.

The word is a trap for newcomers, because in ordinary language it means chaos. In realist theory it means nothing of the kind. Most of the time, most states are at peace with most others; they trade, sign treaties, join organisations and settle disputes by negotiation. Anarchy is compatible with all of that. What it denies is that any of it is guaranteed by a higher authority. A treaty is kept because the parties find it in their interest to keep it, not because a court can compel them; a trading relationship continues because both sides value it, not because a sovereign above them will punish defection.

Three consequences follow, in the realist reading, and each matters for firms.

The first is self-help. Because no one else guarantees a state’s survival, each state must ultimately provide for its own security. It may ally with others, but alliances are arrangements of convenience, and a state that relies entirely on an ally has placed its survival in someone else’s hands.

The second is uncertainty about intentions. A state can observe another’s capabilities — its army, its industry, its population — but it cannot observe its intentions with confidence, and intentions can change faster than capabilities. A peaceful neighbour with a large army is a neighbour whose government could change. Because the cost of misjudging a hostile state is so high, states tend to plan for capabilities rather than for stated intentions.

The third is concern for relative gains. Because today’s partner may be tomorrow’s adversary, states care not only about how much they gain from cooperation, but about how much more the other side gains. A trade agreement that makes both countries richer but makes the rival richer faster shifts the balance of capabilities, and in an anarchic system that shift can be a threat.

Relative gains — the concern of a state with its gains from cooperation relative to those of its partner, rather than only with its own absolute gains, arising from the possibility that the partner’s gains will be converted into capabilities used against it (Grieco 1988).

Joseph Grieco’s 1988 article made this the central point of difference between realists and liberal institutionalists such as Keohane (Chapter 2): institutions can solve the problem of cheating, Grieco argued, but they cannot solve the problem that cooperation may help a rival more than oneself. Powell (1991) sharpened the argument by showing that the weight a state places on relative gains is not fixed but depends on circumstances — in particular, on whether the use of force is a live possibility between the two states. Where force is unthinkable, as between Canada and the United States, relative gains matter little and cooperation is easy; where it is conceivable, relative gains matter a great deal.

That is why the concept belongs in a book on international business. Relative-gains thinking is the logic by which trade becomes a security question. A sale of advanced semiconductors to a rival, or of the equipment to make them, may be profitable for the seller and beneficial for the buyer, and still be prohibited, because it narrows a capability gap the seller’s government wishes to preserve. Export controls on advanced chips are not a failure of economic reasoning; they are the application of a different calculus, in which the question is not “are we better off?” but “are we better off relative to them?”. A firm that sells into the gap between two powers’ capabilities should expect its business to be judged by the second question.

3.3 Two realisms

Realists agree that anarchy shapes behaviour. They disagree about how much power states want, and the disagreement has direct implications for what to expect from the relationship between the United States and China.

3.3.1 Defensive realism: enough is enough

Kenneth Waltz’s Theory of International Politics (1979) is the founding text of structural, or neo-, realism. Its claim is that the behaviour of states is shaped less by their domestic characteristics — their regimes, their leaders, their ideologies — than by the structure of the system they inhabit: anarchy, plus the distribution of capabilities among the units (Waltz 1979). States are alike in their basic function, which is to survive, and they differ in how well equipped they are to do it.

Balance of power — the distribution of capabilities among the major states of a system, and the tendency, in realist theory, of states to respond to a concentration of power by building their own capabilities or forming coalitions against the stronger state (“balancing”).

In Waltz’s version, states seek an appropriate amount of power: enough to be secure. Seeking more is usually self-defeating, because a state that grows too strong provokes others to balance against it, and ends up less secure than before. The system therefore tends towards equilibrium. Waltz also argued that a bipolar system — two great powers, as in the Cold War — is more stable than a multipolar one, because with only two principal actors there are fewer relationships to manage, fewer opportunities for miscalculation, and no uncertainty about who the main threat is.

Waltz’s theory sits on an older idea that is essential for understanding how conflict can arise without anyone wanting it.

Security dilemma — the situation in which measures a state takes to increase its own security, such as arming or forming alliances, reduce the security of others, who respond in kind, leaving all less secure than before even though none intended aggression (Herz 1950; Jervis 1978).

John Herz named the dilemma in 1950. Robert Jervis’s 1978 article gave it its modern form by asking when it is most severe. His answer turned on two variables: whether offensive and defensive weapons and postures can be distinguished, and whether offence or defence has the advantage. Where defensive preparations look defensive and defence is strong, states can secure themselves without threatening others, and the dilemma is mild. Where they cannot be told apart and offence is favoured, every precaution looks like preparation for attack, and the dilemma is acute.

The security dilemma is the most useful single idea in this chapter for a risk analyst, because it describes escalation without intent. A government imposes an export control to protect a technological lead; the other side reads it as an attempt at containment and responds with restrictions on a critical mineral; the first reads that as coercion and tightens the control. Each step is defensive in the mind of the state that takes it. A firm caught in the sequence does not need either government to want a confrontation in order to lose a market or a supplier.

3.3.2 Offensive realism: as much as possible

John Mearsheimer’s The Tragedy of Great Power Politics (2001) accepts Waltz’s starting point but reaches a darker conclusion. It rests on five bedrock assumptions (Mearsheimer 2001):

  1. The international system is anarchic.
  2. Great powers inherently possess some offensive military capability.
  3. States can never be certain about other states’ intentions.
  4. Survival is the primary goal of great powers.
  5. Great powers are rational actors.

None of these, Mearsheimer notes, is individually alarming. Together they imply that great powers fear one another, cannot rely on anyone else for their security, and conclude that the best guarantee of survival is to be as powerful as possible relative to potential rivals. States are therefore not satisfied with an appropriate amount of power. They maximise relative power, and the ultimate goal is hegemony.

Global hegemony, however, is practically impossible, because of what Mearsheimer calls “the stopping power of water”: large bodies of water make it extremely difficult to project land power across oceans. The realistic goal is therefore regional hegemony — domination of one’s own region — together with preventing any other great power from dominating its region. The United States, on this account, is the only regional hegemon in modern history, having achieved dominance in the Western Hemisphere in the nineteenth century, and its foreign policy in the twentieth century can be read as a series of efforts to prevent the emergence of a hegemon in Europe or in North-East Asia.

The prediction for China follows mechanically. If China continues to grow economically, it will seek to dominate Asia as the United States dominates the Western Hemisphere, and the United States, which has spent a century preventing exactly that, will try to stop it. Mearsheimer’s answer to the question of whether China can rise peacefully is no: the result will be intense security competition, with a considerable possibility of war.

Defensive realism (Waltz) Offensive realism (Mearsheimer)
How much power do states want? enough for security as much as possible, relative to others
What restrains them? balancing by others; expansion is self-defeating only the limits of their capabilities
Expected US–China outcome managed competition intense security competition

A caution belongs here. Realism predicts competition, not war. In Waltz’s version it often predicts restraint, since overexpansion provokes balancing. Even Mearsheimer’s version predicts security competition, of which war is one possible outcome among several. A risk analyst who reads realism as a forecast of war has misread it; one who reads it as a forecast that the economic relationship will be subordinated to the strategic one has read it correctly.

3.4 Power transition and the Thucydides’s Trap

Balance-of-power theory treats an equal distribution of capabilities as the condition of stability. A different tradition reverses the claim.

Power transition theory — the theory, due to A. F. K. Organski, that peace is most secure when a dominant power holds a clear preponderance and that war is most likely when a dissatisfied challenger approaches parity with it (Organski 1958; Organski and Kugler 1980).

Organski’s World Politics (1958) described the international system as a hierarchy rather than a balance. At the top sits a dominant power that built the existing international order and benefits from it; below are great powers, some satisfied with the order and some not. Industrialisation drives the rise of new powers, and their growth is uneven. Danger arises when a challenger that is dissatisfied with the order — that did not write its rules and does not benefit from them as much as it believes it should — draws close to the dominant power in capabilities. At that point the challenger has both the motive and, increasingly, the means to revise the order, and the dominant power has reason to act before its lead disappears. In empirical work, parity is often operationalised as the challenger reaching around 80 per cent of the dominant power’s capabilities. On the standard capability index discussed below, China crossed that threshold relative to the United States in 1975, two decades before it passed it — which says as much about the index as about the transition.

Organski and Kugler (1980) took the theory to data in The War Ledger, and a substantial literature followed, including the essays collected in Kugler and Lemke (1996). The tradition connects to Robert Gilpin’s account of hegemonic war, in which the order established by a dominant power becomes misaligned with the underlying distribution of power as others grow, until a war redistributes both (Gilpin 1981). It connects too to Paul Kennedy’s history of the great powers, which argued that dominant powers tend to overextend their commitments relative to their economic base (Kennedy 1987).

Power transition theory has an obvious affinity with Chapter 2. There, the question was whether the rules of the economic order would survive the shrinking share of their authors. Here, the theory supplies a mechanism: a rising power that did not write the rules is likely to be dissatisfied with them, and as its capabilities approach those of the rule-writer, its ability to revise them grows.

3.4.1 Allison’s sixteen cases

Graham Allison’s Destined for War (2017) brought the argument to a mass audience under a classical name. Thucydides, the historian of the war between Athens and Sparta in the fifth century BCE, wrote, in the translation Allison uses, that “it was the rise of Athens and the fear that this instilled in Sparta that made war inevitable” (Allison 2017).

From the archive · Thucydides, fifth century BCE

The Peloponnesian War began in 431 BCE and ended, after a long truce and its collapse, with the defeat of Athens in 404. Its historian, Thucydides, was an Athenian general who had been exiled after failing to prevent the loss of a city to Sparta’s forces, and who spent his exile collecting the accounts of both sides.

The sentence Allison made famous comes early in the history, where Thucydides distinguishes the immediate grievances that both sides publicly cited — disputes over Corcyra and Potidaea, over alliances and trade — from what he calls the truest cause, the one least spoken of: the growth of Athenian power and the alarm it caused in Sparta. That distinction between the stated occasion and the structural cause is the analytical move every realist since has borrowed.

It is worth reading what Thucydides does not say. He does not say that rising powers always fight ruling ones; he says this war had this cause. He was writing about a particular conflict between two particular cities, one a maritime and commercial empire and the other a land power with a very different political system. The generalisation to a law of history is Allison’s, and the critics’ first objection is to the generalisation, not to Thucydides.

Allison and the Thucydides’s Trap project at Harvard identify sixteen cases in the past five hundred years in which a rising power challenged a ruling one. Twelve ended in war. The four that did not — among them the United States overtaking Britain in the early twentieth century, and the Cold War rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union — are presented as evidence that war is not inevitable, but that avoiding it requires deliberate and painful adjustment by both sides.

From the archive · Britain and the United States, 1895–1903

At the end of the nineteenth century the United States overtook Britain as the world’s largest economy, and its navy was growing. The two powers had serious quarrels. In 1895 a boundary dispute between Venezuela and British Guiana brought a sharply worded American demand that Britain accept arbitration, and talk of war on both sides of the Atlantic. Britain accepted arbitration, and a tribunal issued its award in 1899. In 1901 the Hay–Pauncefote Treaty released the United States from an earlier agreement to share control of any canal across Central America, clearing the way for an American-controlled canal at Panama. In 1903 a tribunal settled the boundary between Alaska and Canada largely in the American favour, with the British member siding with the Americans against the Canadian members’ objections.

In each dispute, the ruling power gave way. Britain, facing a rising Germany closer to home and overstretched across its empire, chose to accommodate the rising power in the Western Hemisphere rather than contest it. The case is the strongest evidence that a transition can be peaceful. It is also a reminder of the price: the accommodation was made, in part, at the expense of a third party — Canada — whose interests the ruling power judged less important than the relationship with the rising one.

For a firm, the analogy is uncomfortable and useful. A peaceful transition between great powers is not costless for everyone else. The terms on which it is reached may be paid by actors who were not at the table.

3.4.2 The critics

The Thucydides’s Trap has been criticised on four grounds.

Case selection. The cases were assembled with knowledge of the outcome. What counts as a “rising” power, a “ruling” power, and a “challenge” is elastic enough that peaceful transitions — which leave less of a trace in history than wars — may be under-counted, inflating the proportion that ended in war. A ratio of twelve to sixteen is only as informative as the rule that produced the sixteen.

Nuclear weapons. No pre-1945 case involved two powers each capable of destroying the other. Nuclear deterrence changes the expected cost of great-power war so drastically that it is not clear any pre-nuclear case is informative about the present. The Cold War, the one transition-like rivalry between nuclear powers in the dataset, did not end in war between the principals.

Interdependence. The United States and China are bound by trade, investment and finance far more tightly than Athens and Sparta were. Liberal theory holds that interdependence raises the cost of conflict and so makes it less likely. The realist reply is that Britain and Germany were also major trading partners in 1914, and fought anyway; Copeland (2015) argues that what matters is not the level of trade but leaders’ expectations of its future, so that interdependence restrains only as long as it is expected to last. Chapter 4 takes up the debate with data.

Measuring “rise”. The trap depends on knowing when a challenger is approaching parity, and that depends on how power is measured. Beckley (2018) argues that the conventional gross indicators — GDP, population, composite indices of capability — systematically overstate the power of large, populous countries, because they ignore the costs a state bears in governing, feeding, policing and protecting its population. A country with a large GDP and a large population may have much less surplus available for projecting power than the gross figures imply. Beckley proposes measures that account for such costs, and on them the gap between the United States and China looks larger and the transition further off.

The last criticism can be examined directly, because it turns on the choice of measure.

3.5 Measuring power

Every theory in this chapter turns on a quantity — the distribution of capabilities, parity, relative power — that none of them can observe directly. Power is a latent concept. What can be observed are its supposed ingredients: people, money, industry, arms. The standard way of combining them in quantitative international relations was devised in the 1960s at the University of Michigan by J. David Singer and his collaborators on the Correlates of War project.

Composite Index of National Capability (CINC) — a yearly index of a state’s material capabilities, equal to the unweighted average of its shares of the state-system total on six indicators: total population, urban population, iron and steel production, primary energy consumption, military personnel and military expenditure (Singer, Bremer, and Stuckey 1972).

The construction has three steps. For every state in the international system in a given year, take six indicators, grouped into three dimensions: demographic (total population, urban population), industrial (iron and steel production, primary energy consumption) and military (military personnel, military expenditure). For each indicator, compute the state’s share of the system total that year. Average the six shares. The result is a number between 0 and 1; summed over all states, it comes to about 1 — not exactly, because a state with a missing component is averaged over the components it has. In the current release, the National Material Capabilities dataset version 7.0, the coverage runs from 1816 to 2022, one row per state-year.

The design has real virtues. It is transparent. It is long: two centuries of comparable data, which is what allows Organski’s and Mearsheimer’s arguments to be confronted with evidence at all. And by using shares of the system total, it sidesteps the problem of comparing military spending in nineteenth-century pounds with twenty-first-century dollars — each year is compared only with itself. Mearsheimer’s own measures of latent power in the Tragedy rely on similar industrial ingredients for the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

3.5.1 What it rewards

The design also embodies a theory of power, and it is a theory of the 1960s. Two of the six components are population and two are heavy industry. CINC therefore rewards size and smokestacks. A large, populous, industrialising country — one that produces a great deal of steel and burns a great deal of energy — will score highly even if its technology is behind, its productivity low and its military poorly equipped.

The consequence is visible in the data. In version 7.0 of the National Material Capabilities dataset, China’s CINC first exceeds that of the United States in 1995; the order reverses for a single year in 2002, and China has led in every year since 2003. By 2022 its score was nearly twice the American one. Other measures tell a different story. At purchasing-power parity, China’s GDP passed that of the United States in 2014, according to the World Bank’s data. At market exchange rates it has never passed it: the ratio peaked at about 77 per cent in 2021 and stood at about 64 per cent in 2024. On military expenditure the gap is wider still. The Correlates of War series itself puts China’s spending in 2022 at about a quarter of the American figure, and the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute’s database, in constant 2024 dollars, puts it at about a third in 2025 — with the Chinese figures marked as SIPRI’s own estimates. One underlying reality, four measures, four answers: 1995, 2014, not yet, and not close. The date of the “transition” is a property of the measure, not only of the world.

The size bias is visible even earlier in the series. In the 1860s, 1870s and most of the 1880s — decades in which China was losing wars to European powers and ceding territory and treaty ports — its CINC exceeded that of the United States, because its population was vastly larger and American industry had not yet grown to its later scale. No historian of the period would call Qing China the more powerful of the two. The index does, for a quarter of a century, and for the same reason that it dates the modern transition early.

3.5.2 What it misses

CINC is equally instructive for what it cannot see. It has no component for technology — the quality of weapons, the sophistication of industry, the capacity to innovate. It has no component for alliances, although a state with many capable allies commands far more power than its own figures suggest. It has nothing on financial power: the role of a state’s currency in international payments, the depth of its capital markets, its control of the institutions through which money moves. And it has nothing on control of networks — the payment systems, cloud platforms and communications infrastructure that Farrell and Newman (2019) identify as instruments of coercion (Chapter 2). These are precisely the forms of power that reach firms most directly, and Chapters 8, 10 and 11 return to them.

The choice of measure is not a technicality, and Beckley’s critique shows why. If power is gross resources, China caught up long ago. If it is net resources after the costs of a large population, or military capability weighted by quality, or control of the networks through which the world economy runs, the picture changes. Each measure is defensible. None is neutral.

3.6 Reading two centuries in one chart

The long-run chart of capability shares is a compressed history of the modern state system, and reading it well requires knowing where its seams are, so that the anomalies are recognisable as anomalies.

The first seam is that the chart is not a chart of a fixed set of countries. The Correlates of War data describe the members of the state system in each year — some 17,000 state-years in the current release — and membership changes. China and Japan enter the data in 1860. Germany, coded GMY, disappears in 1945 and returns in 1991; in between, the Federal Republic and the Democratic Republic appear as separate states, GFR and GDR. Japan is absent from 1946 to 1951, under occupation. The Soviet Union, by contrast, is coded as RUS throughout, so that Russia, the USSR and Russia again form one continuous series. Each produces a break or a continuity that has nothing to do with power and everything to do with coding. The codes themselves look like ISO codes and are not — the United Kingdom is UKG, France FRN — so that any combination with the World Bank data of Chapter 2 has to be made through a concordance rather than by matching letters.

The second seam is arithmetic. Missing values of the six components are coded as -9, not left blank, and the index is averaged over whatever components a state has. Summed over all states, CINC should equal one; in practice the yearly total runs from 1.000 to about 1.075, highest in 1860 and generally above one in the nineteenth century, when the components are most often missing.

The third seam is the joining of sources over time. Between 2016 and 2017 the urban population recorded for the United States falls from about 198 million to about 110 million, while its total population continues to rise; the American share of the world’s urban population, as the dataset counts it, drops from about 8 per cent to about 3 per cent in a single year. No migration explains that. It is a change in source or definition, and because urban population is one of six components, it removes nearly a tenth of the United States’ CINC score — from 0.133 to 0.122 — overnight. China’s urban population jumps in the same year in the opposite direction. A reader who saw the gap between the two powers widen in 2017 would be reading a revision, not a shift in power.

The fourth thing is the substantive story, and read with these cautions it is clear. Britain holds the largest share from 1816 until the 1890s; the United States passes it briefly in 1892 and for good in 1897, in the years when, as the archive above recounts, Britain chose to accommodate it. The two World Wars violently rearrange the distribution: the American share peaks in 1945 at nearly 40 per cent of the system’s capabilities, the highest any state reaches in two centuries. The Cold War appears as Waltz’s bipolarity made visible — two powers far above the rest — and on this index the Soviet Union actually leads from 1971 to 1988, a reminder of how much weight the measure gives to steel, energy and soldiers and how little to technology. From the 1990s, China’s share rises past both, to about 23 per cent in 2022 against about 12 per cent for the United States.

3.6.1 Bipolar, multipolar, or something else?

The chart also bears on a question that has occupied realists since the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991: what is the polarity of the system now? The 1990s were widely described as a unipolar moment, with the United States unmatched. Waltz expected that unipolarity would not last, because other states would balance against a power with no counterweight. Whether the present system is returning to bipolarity — the United States and China — or moving to a multipolar one with several significant powers is contested, and the answer depends, once again, on the measure.

The question matters to firms because the two configurations imply different environments. A bipolar system tends to produce two blocs with clear membership, so that the firm’s problem is being made to choose a side. A multipolar system produces more hedging states — governments that trade with both sides and bargain between them — and therefore more room for firms to locate operations in third countries open to both. The geography of “connector” economies, which Chapter 4 examines with trade data, is a direct consequence of which of the two worlds firms are in.

3.7 How a firm would know the balance is shifting

The second half of the chapter’s question — how would a multinational know that the balance is shifting? — has two answers, a structural one and a behavioural one.

The structural answer is the one the data above supply: track the distribution of capabilities on several measures, and watch the ratios. A challenger’s share approaching the dominant power’s on one measure, while still far behind on others, is a transition in progress and a transition contested; it is the situation in which the security dilemma is most acute, because each side can read the evidence as it wishes. Structural indicators of this kind, in the typology of Chapter 1, explain why tension is building. They do not say when it will matter.

The behavioural answer is to watch what states do with the rules. A shifting balance shows up first not in war but in the instruments of economic statecraft: export controls, investment screening, sanctions and counter-sanctions, restrictions on data, rules about where a firm may source a component. These are the measures by which states convert relative-gains concerns into policy, and they are observable — in official gazettes, in company disclosures, in the event datasets of later chapters — long before any military confrontation. For a firm, the most reliable signal that the balance is shifting is a change in which rules it is being asked to follow.

3.8 What it means for the multinational

The realist frame leads to three implications for a firm, each of which recurs later in the book.

The balance of power sets the rules of trade. Who can impose sanctions, export controls and market-access conditions depends on who holds capabilities others need: the largest market, the dominant currency, the critical technology. A firm’s exposure to a state’s economic statecraft is a function of how much of its business depends on capabilities that state controls.

A transition changes which rules a firm follows. A firm operating in both the United States and China increasingly faces two compliance regimes — and, since China adopted its own instruments of counter-sanction in 2020 and 2021, the possibility of penalties in one jurisdiction for complying with the other’s rules. The question “is this transaction legal?” has acquired a second clause: “legal where, and under whose law?”.

Relative gains turn commerce into security. Products that would make a rival stronger — advanced semiconductors, the tools that make them, artificial intelligence, batteries, aerospace — move from the trade ministry’s jurisdiction to the security establishment’s. For firms in these sectors, market access is no longer negotiated mainly on economic grounds, and commercial arguments about mutual benefit carry less weight than they did.

Mapped onto the five channels of this book, the balance of power runs through every one, but most directly through market access (which markets will remain open to firms from which bloc) and assets and people (where conflict, if it comes, will fall). It reaches inputs through export controls on critical goods, finance and payments through the dominant currency, and data and operations through the control of networks.

3.9 A short review of the literature

Realism’s modern structural form begins with Waltz (1979), who explained state behaviour by anarchy and the distribution of capabilities and judged bipolarity the more stable configuration. The security dilemma that underlies much of it was named by Herz (1950) and given its analytical form by Jervis (1978), who related its severity to the offence–defence balance. Grieco (1988) made relative gains the realist objection to liberal institutionalism, and Powell (1991) showed that the weight states put on relative gains depends on whether force is a live option between them. Mearsheimer (2001) pushed the structural argument to its offensive conclusion — power maximisation and regional hegemony — and applied it to China.

A parallel tradition locates danger in hierarchy rather than balance. Organski (1958) introduced power transition theory; Organski and Kugler (1980) and the contributions in Kugler and Lemke (1996) tested it on data. Gilpin (1981) gave a systemic account of hegemonic war, and Kennedy (1987) a historical account of how great powers rise and decline relative to their economic base. Allison (2017) reframed the transition debate for the US–China relationship through sixteen historical cases.

Measurement runs through all of it. Singer, Bremer, and Stuckey (1972) introduced the Composite Index of National Capability and the Correlates of War data on which much of the quantitative literature rests. Beckley (2018) argued that gross indicators overstate the power of populous states and proposed net measures, which change the dating of the US–China transition. Farrell and Newman (2019) identified a form of power — control over network hubs — that no capability index was designed to capture.

The through-line is that every theory of the balance of power is also a claim about how to measure it, and that the measurement is where theories can be checked against the world — and where they most often disagree.

3.10 Which kind of power the firm depends on

Realism begins from an absence. There is no government above states, and so the rules on which firms rely — the open market, the convertible currency, the procedure for settling a dispute — are kept only as long as the states that hold the relevant capabilities find it in their interest to keep them. That is not a prediction of war. Defensive realists expect restraint; offensive realists expect security competition; power transition theorists expect danger only where a dissatisfied challenger nears parity. What all three share is the claim that the economic relationship between great powers is, in the end, subordinate to the strategic one. Export controls, investment screens and counter-sanctions are what that subordination looks like before anyone fires a shot.

The evidence of this chapter adds a second claim, about measurement. Every theory of the balance of power turns on a quantity — capabilities, parity, relative power — that cannot be observed directly, and the answer to the question “has the transition happened?” depends on how the quantity is built. On an index that rewards population and heavy industry, China overtook the United States in 1995, and Qing China was ahead of it in the 1870s. On output at purchasing-power parity it did so in 2014; at market exchange rates it has not; on military spending it is far behind. Each number is correctly computed; none is the balance of power. A board slide that says “China has overtaken the United States” without saying on what has said nothing a decision can use.

For a multinational, the useful question is therefore not which measure is right in general, but which kind of power its own exposures depend on. A firm whose risk lies in market access needs to know who holds the largest market and the willingness to close it. A firm whose risk lies in inputs needs to know who controls the technology, the mineral or the equipment that cannot be replaced quickly. A firm whose risk lies in finance needs to know whose currency clears its payments, and a firm whose risk lies in data needs to know whose networks its operations run through. On some of these dimensions the transition is far advanced; on others it has barely begun; on the forms of power that reach firms most directly — currency, networks, critical technology — the standard capability index does not look at all.

A crossover on a capability chart, then, does not tell a multinational that war is coming — power transition theory makes war likelier near parity only if the challenger is dissatisfied, and dissatisfaction is not on the chart. Nor does it say that the firm’s Chinese revenue is at risk or its American suppliers safe. It says that, on one measure built in the 1960s around steel, energy and soldiers, and ending in 2022, the distribution of material capabilities has shifted. Whether that shift reaches the firm depends on whether the measure can see the kind of power the firm actually depends on — and, as the chapters on trade, sanctions, cyber operations and space will show, the kinds that matter most are often the ones it cannot.
