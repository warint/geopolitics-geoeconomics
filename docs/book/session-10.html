10.2 The lecture

The session opens with Quiz 3 (20 min) and four Risk Briefs (20 min). The lecture is therefore shorter today.

10.2.1 1. The instrument of first resort

Economic sanctions are restrictions on economic relations imposed by one or more senders on a target — a state, a government, a firm or an individual — to change its behaviour, weaken it, or signal disapproval. Their use has grown markedly since the end of the Cold War; the Global Sanctions Data Base documents the rise and its changing composition.

Dimension Categories
Sender unilateral (one state) · plurilateral (the EU, a coalition) · multilateral (the UN Security Council)
Type trade · financial · travel · arms · military assistance · other — the GSDB’s classification
Scope comprehensive (a whole economy) vs targeted or “smart” (named persons, firms, sectors)
Objective change a policy · destabilise a regime · end a war or a territorial conflict · prevent war · fight terrorism · human rights · democracy

10.2.2 2. Do they work? The great debate

Hufbauer, Schott, Elliott and Oegg (2007) — the third edition of a study first published in 1985 — code each episode on two components: how far the policy outcome sought was achieved, and how far sanctions contributed to it. Combining the two, they judge roughly a third of cases at least partly successful, with success more likely when goals are modest, the target is small, weak and friendly to the sender, and sanctions are imposed quickly and decisively.

Pape (1997) re-examined the cases counted as successes and argued that most do not survive scrutiny: in many, the outcome was achieved by force, was not really conceded, or cannot be attributed to sanctions. His conclusion: economic sanctions rarely achieve major political goals. Elliott (1998) replied that the glass is half full rather than empty — the debate turns on coding.

The selection effect (Drezner, 2003). If targets that would concede do so when sanctions are only threatened, then the cases we observe as imposed are the hard ones. Imposed sanctions will look ineffective even if the instrument works. This is why TIES records threats as well as impositions — and why the practice round compares the two.

The lesson for an analyst. “Do sanctions work?” has no answer independent of how a case, an objective and a success are coded. Every number in this debate is a coding decision.

10.2.3 3. From comprehensive to smart sanctions

The humanitarian cost of the comprehensive sanctions on Iraq in the 1990s pushed senders towards targeted measures: asset freezes and travel bans on named individuals, sectoral restrictions, financial measures. Farrell and Newman (Session 08) explain why financial sanctions became the weapon of choice: the dollar-clearing system and financial-messaging networks are chokepoints under the jurisdiction of a few senders.

Targeted does not mean contained. Sanctions on banks, insurers and shippers propagate to every firm that depends on them.

10.2.4 4. The firm in the middle

The compliance perimeter. A firm must screen customers, suppliers, banks and vessels against sanctions lists — in the US, the Treasury’s OFAC lists; in the EU, Council regulations; in Canada, regulations under the Special Economic Measures Act and related statutes. Ownership rules extend listings to entities owned by listed persons, so the perimeter is wider than the list.

Secondary sanctions threaten third-country firms with penalties — loss of access to the sender’s market or financial system — for dealing with the target. They make a sender’s foreign policy binding on firms that are not its nationals.

Overcompliance and de-risking. Because penalties are severe and the rules complex, banks and firms often withdraw from whole markets or sectors the law does not close. The effective scope of sanctions is set by compliance departments as much as by governments.

The exit decision: Russia 2022. Following the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Western sanctions and public pressure led many multinationals to suspend, scale back or leave Russian operations. Exits were complicated by countermeasures — approval requirements and discounts on sales of foreign assets, and in some cases temporary state management of foreign-owned firms. The firm’s options:

Option Cost Risk
Stay reputational, compliance burden new sanctions; asset seizure; home-state pressure
Suspend idle assets write-off if the situation persists
Sell forced discount, approval delays buyer later sanctioned; buy-back clauses
Walk away full write-off —

10.2.5 5. What a risk dashboard should track

  1. Exposure — the firm’s revenues, assets and suppliers in sanctioned or at-risk jurisdictions;
  2. Pipeline — threats and announced measures (the TIES lesson);
  3. Propagation — banks, insurers, logistics providers whose own exposure would cut the firm off;
  4. Counter-measures — what the target can do to foreign firms on its soil.

10.2.6 6. The Tableau skill of the day

A year scaffold joined with a range condition (Year ≥ begin_year and Year ≤ end_year, in the GSDB’s column names) turns episodes into active cases per year; a Gantt chart draws each episode as a bar from start to end. Step by step: ../02-practice/README.md.

10.3 In the practice

The target. The Global Sanctions Data Base’s descriptive picture (Felbermayr et al., 2020): active sanctions cases per year, by type, from 1950 to today, and the distribution of outcomes by objective — rebuilt from Release 5, then confronted with the threats in TIES.

Work in your project teams. The last 20 minutes are the team dashboard skeleton review.

Licence. GSDB data may not be redistributed. Hand in on ZoneCours only. Do not publish this workbook to Tableau Public.

10.3.1 1 · Collect — 5 min

Everything should already be in data/raw/: the GSDB files as e-mailed, TIES v4.0, both codebooks, and years.csv (see ../data/README.md). Fill the provenance log — for the GSDB, the release number and the date of the e-mail.

10.3.2 2 · Prepare — 20 min

  1. Connect → Text file / Excel → the GSDB case-level file. Confirm in the codebook, and log, the names used below: case_id, sanctioned_state, sanctioning_state, begin_year, end_year, the six type columns, objective, success.

  2. Pivot the types. The six types are separate 0/1 columns (trade, financial, travel, arms, military, other): select them in the grid → right-click → Pivot. Rename Pivot Field Names → Type, Pivot Field Values → Type flag. Filter later on Type flag = 1.

  3. Build the years. Add years.csv to the canvas and join it to the GSDB table with two clauses:

    • Year >= begin_year
    • Year <= join calculation IFNULL([end_year], 2025) (click the right-hand field of a clause → Create Join Calculation). Each case now has one row per active year — and, after the pivot, one per type. (In the R5 file ongoing cases already carry end_year = 2025; the IFNULL is a guard for releases that leave it empty.)

  4. Check the grain. Note the row count before and after. Write in the log what one row now is.

  5. Outcomes. Outcomes are not indicator columns: success is one text field holding one code per objective, in the order of objective (e.g. objective = human_rights,democracy, success = failed,ongoing). Take the first objective and its outcome:

    Objective  =  TRIM(SPLIT([objective], ",", 1))
Outcome    =  CASE TRIM(SPLIT([success], ",", 1))
              WHEN "success_total"   THEN "Success"
              WHEN "success_part"    THEN "Partial success"
              WHEN "nego_settlement" THEN "Negotiated settlement"
              WHEN "failed"          THEN "Failure"
              WHEN "ongoing"         THEN "Ongoing"
              ELSE "Not coded" END

    Log the decision: cases with several objectives count here under their first one only.

10.3.3 3 · Replicate — 25 min

10.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “Active sanctions cases per year, by type”

  1. Year → Columns (continuous).
  2. COUNTD([case_id]) → Rows — count distinct, never SUM, because the pivot and the scaffold multiply rows.
  3. Filter Type flag = 1; Type → Colour; mark type Area or Bar.
  4. Note in a caption: a case with several types is counted once in each type, so the stacked total exceeds the number of cases. Then build a version with no colour to show the true total.
  5. Annotate 1990–91, 2014 and 2022. What explains the shape?

10.3.3.2 Sheet 2 · “How do cases end, by objective?”

  1. Use the case-level grain: duplicate the data source without the year scaffold, or use {FIXED [case_id] : MIN([Outcome])}.
  2. Objective → Rows, COUNTD([case_id]) → Columns, Outcome → Colour.
  3. Quick Table Calculation → Percent of Total; Edit Table Calculation → Compute using Outcome so each objective sums to 100%.
  4. Exclude ongoing cases from the denominator in a second version. How much does the success share move? (That is a coding decision too.)

10.3.3.3 Sheet 3 · “One target’s history” — a Gantt chart

  1. Filter sanctioned_state to one target (Iran, Russia, Cuba, North Korea…) — with a calculated filter CONTAINS([sanctioned_state], "Iran"), because some cases list several targets.
  2. begin_year → Columns (continuous); case_id (or sanctioning_state + case_id) → Rows.
  3. Mark type Gantt Bar; calculated field Duration = IFNULL([end_year], 2025) - [begin_year] → Size.
  4. Type → Colour. What does the target’s history look like — layers added, never removed?

10.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 10 min

  1. Differ. Put Sheet 1 beside the GSDB paper’s figure. Release 5 runs years further and has added and recoded cases: write one difference.
  2. Threats vs impositions. Open TIES. Using the manual, separate threatened-only cases (imposition = 0) from imposed cases (imposition = 1) and count each per startyear (1945–2005). Put the counts beside GSDB impositions for the same years. What does the gap say about Drezner’s selection effect?
  3. Extend — the firm. For your team’s firm, list its top five countries. How many active GSDB cases name each as the sanctioned state in the latest year?
