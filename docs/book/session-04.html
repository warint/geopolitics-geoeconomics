4  Trade, Conflict and Geoeconomic Fragmentation: From Commercial Peace to Rival Blocs

4.1 An old promise, and an old embarrassment

The idea that commerce keeps the peace is older than the discipline that now tests it. Montesquieu put it in a sentence in 1748 — peace is the natural effect of trade — and the sentence did a great deal of work for the next century and a half. Richard Cobden built a political career on it: the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 and the Anglo-French commercial treaty of 1860 that carries his name were sold, in part, as instruments of peace between nations that had spent much of the previous century fighting each other. The argument was not that merchants are nicer than generals. It was that exchange creates gains, that war destroys them, and that a state which stands to lose a great deal from fighting will think harder before it fights.

The argument’s most famous restatement came at the worst possible moment.

From the archive · Angell, 1910

In 1910 Norman Angell, a British journalist, published The Great Illusion (Angell 1910). Its claim is usually remembered as “war has become impossible”, which is not what it said. Angell’s claim was narrower and more interesting: that in an economy of credit, contracts and cross-border investment, conquest no longer pays. A victor cannot seize the wealth of an industrial country, because that wealth consists of claims, trading relationships and productive organisation that collapse when they are seized. The economic case for war, he argued, rested on an illusion — the belief that national wealth is a stock of things that can be carried off.

The book sold in enormous numbers and was translated widely. Four years later Britain and Germany — two of each other’s most important trading partners, bound by finance, shipping and insurance as well as by goods — were at war. The war went on for more than four years and destroyed exactly the wealth Angell said it would destroy.

The episode is usually told as a refutation. It is better read as a lesson about which claim was refuted. Angell was largely right that the war would be ruinous for the victors as well as the vanquished. What failed was the inference from war does not pay to states will therefore not make war. Leaders in 1914 were not computing the net present value of conflict, or were computing it with expectations about the war’s length and cost that turned out to be badly wrong. Angell received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1933, as the next European war was being prepared. The commercial peace has lived with the embarrassment of 1914 ever since, and every serious version of the argument is a version that tries to explain it.

This chapter takes the argument seriously, sets out the realist reply, asks what a century of evidence has settled, and then turns to the question that has replaced it. The old question was whether trade pacifies. The question that matters to a multinational in the 2020s is different: if states have begun to trade more with friends than with rivals, how does a firm find out which side each of its markets is on, and how fast the lines are hardening? That question has an empirical answer, assembled from public data, and the second half of the chapter shows how the answer is built and how far it can be trusted.

4.2 Four reasons trade might pacify

The modern version of the argument is less a single claim than a family of mechanisms, and they are worth keeping apart because they fail in different ways.

Commercial peace — the proposition that economic interdependence between two states lowers the probability that they will come into militarised conflict with each other. It is a claim about dyads — pairs of states — and it is probabilistic: it predicts fewer disputes, not none.

The first mechanism is opportunity cost. Conflict disrupts trade, and the more a dyad trades, the more it stands to lose. This is Angell’s mechanism with the conquest argument stripped out, and it was formalised by Polachek (1980), who modelled conflict as an activity whose price rises with the gains from trade that it puts at risk. Its weakness is the weakness of 1914: it assumes leaders weigh those costs, and weigh them accurately.

The second is signalling. Interdependent states have a menu of costly actions short of force — threatening, restricting or suspending economic ties — that allow them to reveal how much they care about an issue. A state that is willing to lose trade to make a point is telling its rival something credible about its resolve, and credible information reduces the risk of miscalculation that rationalist accounts of war identify as its main cause. On this account trade matters not because it makes war expensive but because it makes communication possible.

The third is domestic coalitions. Trade creates exporters, importers, port operators, banks and their employees, all of whom have something to lose from a rupture with the partner. They lobby. A government contemplating confrontation faces an organised constituency on the other side, and the larger the trade, the larger the constituency. This mechanism is the one most familiar to a multinational, because the multinational is frequently the constituency.

The fourth is institutions. Trade agreements create committees, forums and dispute-settlement procedures through which grievances can be processed before they escalate, and they create repeated interaction among officials who come to know each other. Mansfield and Pevehouse (2000) found that pairs of states belonging to the same preferential trading arrangement are less prone to militarised disputes, and that the effect strengthens as trade between them grows — a result that has made institutions the most robust strand of the liberal argument.

Statistical work on dyads from the 1990s onwards gave these mechanisms empirical weight. Oneal and Russett (1997), using dyads from 1950 to 1985, titled their findings with some confidence — the classical liberals were right — and the programme culminated in Russett and Oneal (2001), which set interdependence alongside democracy and shared membership of international organisations as the three legs of a “Kantian” peace. Dyads that trade more relative to their GDP experience fewer militarised disputes. That correlation is real. What it means is a different question.

4.3 The realist reply: interdependence as exposure

The realist critique does not deny that trade creates gains. It denies that gains are what states care about most. In an anarchic system — the world described in Chapter 3, with no authority above states to enforce agreements — a state’s first concern is survival, and every economic relationship is evaluated for what it does to security.

Seen that way, interdependence is a polite word for vulnerability. If a state depends on a rival for food, fuel or critical components, the rival holds a lever over it. Kenneth Waltz drew the conclusion that great powers will try to limit such dependence, and that where they cannot, they may be tempted to secure supplies by other means (Waltz 1979). The relationship that liberals see as a bond, realists see as a hostage.

The argument has a precise early statement in economics, and it was written by someone watching it happen.

Albert Hirschman, a refugee economist writing during the Second World War, analysed how Germany in the 1930s had organised its foreign trade with the smaller states of south-eastern Europe (Hirschman 1945). The terms were often generous. Germany bought their agricultural exports in quantities no one else would, through bilateral clearing arrangements that tied payment to purchases of German goods. The result was that a large share of each small country’s trade came to depend on a single partner, while each of them was a trivial share of Germany’s. Hirschman separated two effects. The supply effect is the gain in resources that trade brings a state. The influence effect is the power a state acquires over partners for whom the relationship matters much more than it does to the state itself — because breaking it would cost them dearly and cost it little. The influence effect depends not on the volume of trade but on its asymmetry and its concentration, and to measure concentration Hirschman proposed an index that, after a later rediscovery by Herfindahl, became the standard measure of market concentration in competition policy. The intellectual ancestor of every modern dependency dashboard was designed to measure geopolitical leverage.

Two further realist arguments sharpen the point. The first concerns relative gains. Chapter 3 showed why a state in an anarchic system may reject a deal that makes it better off if the deal makes its rival better off by more. Trade usually benefits both partners, but rarely equally, and a state that expects to face its partner as an adversary must worry that today’s gains will fund tomorrow’s threat.

The second is Joanne Gowa’s argument about security externalities (Gowa 1994).

Security externality — the effect of trade on the military potential of the partner. Because the gains from trade raise a partner’s economic capacity, and economic capacity can be converted into military power, trade with an ally strengthens one’s own side while trade with an adversary strengthens the other. States should therefore trade more with allies than with adversaries.

Gowa and Mansfield (1993) tested the prediction and found that alliances do increase trade between their members, and that the effect is stronger in bipolar than in multipolar systems — exactly where the security stakes of strengthening the wrong partner are clearest. The prediction matters for this chapter because it is, word for word, the hypothesis that the fragmentation literature now tests: that trade will reorganise itself along the lines of geopolitical alignment.

The most important refinement of the realist position came from Dale Copeland, and it rescues something from both sides. Copeland’s claim is that what matters is not the level of trade today but expectations of future trade (Copeland 1996, 2015). A dependent state that expects trade to continue has every reason to keep the peace, because the liberal gains are real and are expected to keep flowing. The same state, at the same level of dependence, becomes dangerous when it comes to expect that it will be cut off — because then dependence is a vulnerability about to be exploited, and acting before the window closes can look rational. Interdependence is pacifying or provocative depending on what leaders believe about the future.

From the archive · Washington and Tokyo, 1941

Copeland’s most cited illustration is the road to Pearl Harbor. Through the 1930s Japan depended on the United States for a large part of its oil and of other strategic materials, while it was waging war in China. As Japan advanced into Indochina, Washington tightened export controls step by step, and in the summer of 1941 the United States froze Japanese assets, which in practice cut Japan off from American oil; Britain and the Netherlands, which controlled the other accessible supplies, followed.

Japan’s leaders now faced a dependence that had turned, in their reading, from an asset into a countdown: stocks of oil would run down, the navy would lose the capacity to act, and the terms on offer from Washington — withdrawal from China — were ones they would not accept. In December 1941 they attacked the American fleet at Pearl Harbor and moved south against the oil-producing Dutch East Indies.

The episode does not show that trade causes war; Japan’s expansion was the cause of the embargo before the embargo became a cause of the attack. What it shows is the mechanism Copeland identifies. The same trading relationship that had given Japan a reason for restraint gave it, once expectations of continued supply collapsed, a reason for haste. For a firm the lesson transfers directly: the moment of maximum danger in a dependent relationship is not when trade is high, but when one side comes to believe it is about to end.

4.4 What the dyads say, and why they disagree

By the end of the 1990s the two camps had produced findings that pointed in opposite directions. Oneal and Russett (1997) found interdependence pacifying. Barbieri (1996), using dyads from 1870 to 1938 and measures that distinguished the salience of a relationship from its symmetry, found that extensive interdependence was associated with more militarised disputes, not fewer, and later extended the case against the liberal view at book length (Barbieri 2002). Much of the disagreement turned out to be about measurement — trade relative to whose GDP, counted from whose customs records, over which years, against which definition of a dispute — which is a warning that applies to every measure in this book.

Edward Mansfield’s review in the Oxford Handbook of International Political Economy is the best short guide to where the debate now stands (Mansfield 2021). Four of its points organise everything that follows.

The first is that the relationship runs both ways. Conflict reduces trade: states that expect to fight trade less, and states that are fighting trade hardly at all. A negative correlation between trade and conflict is therefore what we would observe whether trade causes peace, peace causes trade, or both are driven by something else — a shared alliance, a shared regime type, geographical distance. This is reverse causality in its classic form, and it is fatal to any naive reading of a dyadic correlation. It returns in the fragmentation literature in a new form: when trade between two blocs falls, we must ask whether the trade fell because the relationship deteriorated, or whether the deterioration is what the falling trade is measuring.

The second is that institutions matter. The finding of Mansfield and Pevehouse (2000) on preferential trading arrangements has proved more durable than the bare correlation between trade and peace, perhaps because institutions supply the information and the repeated interaction that the signalling and coalition mechanisms require.

The third is that context matters. The pacifying effect of trade differs with regime type, with the level of development, and with the distribution of power between the partners. A finding pooled across all dyads in all decades is an average over very different relationships, and an average can describe none of them well.

The fourth is the one that matters most for a business school: the question has changed. As great-power competition returned, research turned from asking whether trade pacifies to asking how states use trade as an instrument of conflict. That is the question of Chapters 8, 9 and 10 — cyber, economic diplomacy and sanctions — and it is the reason this chapter ends not with dyadic dispute data but with the geography of trade itself.

4.5 Bilateral ties, multilateral exits

One contribution reshaped the debate by asking which trade matters. Martin, Mayer, and Thoenig (2008) — economists rather than political scientists — separated two quantities that earlier work had often lumped together.

Bilateral versus multilateral openness — bilateral openness is how much two states trade with each other; multilateral openness is how much each trades with the rest of the world. The first raises what the pair loses from fighting each other. The second lowers it, because a state with many alternative partners can replace the trade a conflict destroys.

The logic is that of any outside option. If country A trades heavily with B and with almost no one else, a war with B is ruinous. If A trades heavily with B but also with fifty other countries, losing B is costly but survivable: the fifty others become substitutes. Their model predicts, and their empirical work supports, both effects at once: bilateral trade lowers the probability of a bilateral conflict, and multilateral openness raises it.

Effect on the risk of conflict between A and B
Bilateral trade between A and B lowers it: more to lose from fighting each other
Multilateral openness of A and B with everyone else raises it: substitutes make losing the partner cheaper

The implication is uncomfortable for the liberal view and was meant to be. Globalisation lowers the cost of bilateral conflict. The very process that multiplied trading relationships after 1990 made each individual relationship less essential, and therefore made breaking any one of them cheaper. A world of many alternative suppliers is a world in which a state can afford to fall out with one of them.

There is a corollary for the multinational that is easy to miss. Martin, Mayer and Thoenig describe the cost of switching for states, aggregated across an economy. A government that decides to reduce its dependence on a rival can point to a world of substitutes. The firm whose particular supplier sits in that rival, whose tooling is calibrated to that supplier’s components, whose certifications name that plant, does not get the aggregate substitution elasticity. It gets its own contracts, which do not become cheaper to break because the national economy has alternatives. Geopolitical decisions are taken at the level where switching is cheapest and paid for at the level where it is dearest.

4.6 From “does trade pacify?” to “how is trade used?”

The fourth of Mansfield’s points has a theoretical counterpart that has become central to the study of geoeconomics. Farrell and Newman (2019) observe that globalisation did not produce a flat network of equal partners. It produced hubs: the dollar clearing system, the messaging network banks use for cross-border payments, a handful of firms that control critical technologies, a small number of undersea cables and cloud providers. States with jurisdiction over a hub acquire two kinds of power over everyone who must pass through it. The panopticon effect is the ability to see — to gather information from the flows that cross the hub. The chokepoint effect is the ability to deny — to cut a target off from the network.

This is Hirschman’s influence effect, rebuilt for a networked economy. It also turns the liberal argument on its head. In the liberal story, dense economic ties reduce conflict by raising its cost. In the account of weaponised interdependence, dense ties are the instruments through which conflict is conducted. Interdependence does not stop states competing; it changes the terrain on which they compete, and the multinational, which builds and uses those networks, finds itself standing on that terrain.

The policy response has been to reorganise the networks themselves — to reduce exposure to a rival’s chokepoints and to build one’s own. That reorganisation has a name.

4.7 Fragmentation: three words, three policies

The vocabulary of the early 2020s distinguishes three policies that are often run together in public debate, and a firm that cannot tell them apart will misread its own exposure.

Friend-shoring — a term popularised in US policy debate in 2022, most prominently by the US Treasury Secretary — proposes to move supply chains towards trusted partners: to keep trading, but with friends. De-risking — the European Commission’s term, adopted in 2023 to describe its approach to China — proposes to reduce dependence on a rival in critical goods and technologies while preserving trade overall. De-coupling is the end point that both of the others say they are trying to avoid: separate economic blocs with little exchange between them.

Geoeconomic fragmentation — a policy-driven reversal of global economic integration, in which states redirect trade, investment, technology and payments according to geopolitical alignment rather than cost (Aiyar et al. 2023). It is distinguished from ordinary changes in trade patterns by its cause: it is driven by security and alignment, not by prices or technology.

The definition, from an IMF Staff Discussion Note that set out the research agenda, contains a claim that is hard to test: that the cause is geopolitical. A fall in trade between two countries may be the result of a tariff, a sanction, a shift in comparative advantage, a change in exchange rates, or a firm’s decision to build a factory closer to its customers. Fragmentation is a hypothesis about why trade patterns change. What the data can show directly is only whether they change along geopolitical lines — whether the lines that matter for trade start to coincide with the lines that matter for foreign policy.

That narrower question is the one Gopinath and her co-authors set out to answer.

4.8 How Gopinath and co-authors draw the lines

Gopinath et al. (2025), first circulated as an IMF working paper (Gopinath et al. 2024), ask whether the global economy has begun to split into blocs since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Their design has three steps, and each embeds a choice that a reader of the result should know about.

First, define the blocs. Countries are grouped into a US-leaning bloc, a China-leaning bloc and a non-aligned group on the basis of their foreign-policy alignment as revealed by voting in the UN General Assembly. The choice is deliberate. Alliances are too sparse to classify most countries; trade itself cannot be used, because trade is the outcome; and General Assembly votes are cast by nearly every state on hundreds of resolutions a year, recorded publicly and comparable across countries. Section 2 of the paper states exactly which measure, which reference period and which cut-offs the authors use. Any simpler classification — including the illustrative rule described later in this chapter — departs from theirs, and the size of the departure is part of what any comparison with their result has to report.

Second, compare trade within and between blocs before and after the invasion. The comparison is made in a gravity framework — the workhorse model of international trade, in which bilateral trade is explained by the economic size of the partners, the distance between them and a set of other frictions — so that the change attributed to bloc membership is the change left over once the usual determinants of bilateral trade have been accounted for. The paper also examines foreign direct investment, where the same question is asked of announced investment projects.

Third, look for connectors. Non-aligned countries whose trade with both blocs grew after 2022 are candidates for rerouted trade: goods that once moved directly between the blocs may now move through a third country, sometimes after some processing, sometimes after very little. Countries such as Mexico and Vietnam are the obvious candidates.

Independent evidence on the United States points the same way and sharpens what “connector” means. Freund et al. (2024), working with US imports at the ten-digit tariff line between 2017 and 2022, find that China’s share of US imports fell from 22 to 16 per cent and attribute the decline to the tariffs imposed from 2018. The imports were replaced by large developing countries with a revealed comparative advantage in the products concerned — and those countries were deeply integrated into China’s own supply chains and saw their imports from China grow faster, especially in strategic industries: to displace China as an exporter to the United States, a country had to embrace China’s supply chains. Within products the pattern looked like “China plus one” rather than broad diversification, and there was no consistent evidence of reshoring. China remained the largest supplier of goods to the United States in 2022. Alfaro and Chor (2023) reach a compatible conclusion from a longer view of US sourcing: direct imports from China have fallen while Vietnam and Mexico have gained share, and it is unclear whether the reallocation reduces dependence on supply chains linked to China at all. The bilateral line has moved; the network beneath it has moved much less.

The main finding of Gopinath et al. (2025) is that after the invasion trade between the two blocs fell relative to trade within them, by a margin the paper estimates with care and which no summary, including this one, should be trusted to round; foreign direct investment shows a similar pattern. The authors are careful in two directions. The fragmentation they measure is real and is concentrated in strategic sectors. It is also small compared with the Cold War, when trade between the Western and Soviet blocs was a small fraction of what it is now. Both halves of that conclusion matter to a firm. The first says the lines are hardening. The second says that the world is a long way from two separate economies, and that a strategy built on the assumption of complete de-coupling is a strategy built for a world that does not yet exist.

Bloc membership is fixed at a reference date, so countries that drift are classified by where they stood; the non-aligned group mixes countries that lean slightly and countries that genuinely balance. None of this undermines the paper; it defines what the paper licenses.

4.9 Turning alignment into a number

The whole design rests on a single variable that few readers outside political science have met, and it is worth understanding how it is made, because every choice in its construction travels into the result.

A General Assembly roll call records, for each resolution, whether each state voted yes, no or abstained. The simplest way to measure the alignment of two states is to count how often they vote the same way. Agreement scores of this kind have been used since the 1960s, and their limits were documented by Signorino and Ritter (1999), who showed that the common measures confound genuine similarity with the base rate of agreement on resolutions everyone supports. A deeper problem is comparability over time. The agenda changes from one year to the next. If the Assembly votes on more resolutions about the Middle East in one year and more about decolonisation in another, a change in a state’s agreement with the United States may reflect the agenda rather than the state.

Bailey, Strezhnev, and Voeten (2017) solve the problem with a spatial model. Each state is assumed to have a position — an ideal point — on an underlying dimension, and each resolution divides the dimension into the positions that favour it and those that oppose it. The model estimates both states’ positions and resolutions’ cut-points from the pattern of votes. Comparability over time comes from resolutions that are repeated in essentially identical form across years: because the same text is voted on again, it anchors the scale, and movement by a state relative to those anchors is movement by the state. Voeten (2000) had earlier shown that after the Cold War the main dimension of Assembly voting separates states by their stance towards the US-led liberal order — which is why the dimension can be read as alignment with, or against, the United States.

Ideal point — a state’s estimated position on a single underlying dimension of UN General Assembly voting, derived from all its roll-call votes and designed to be comparable over time. Higher values indicate positions closer to those of the United States. It measures revealed foreign-policy alignment in the Assembly; it does not measure trade, alliance membership, military co-operation or friendship.

The data come from the Dataverse record maintained by Erik Voeten and co-authors (Voeten 2026). Version 39, released in July 2026, estimates ideal points by calendar year, where older releases used Assembly sessions; the file still carries a session column, and the calendar year is the session number plus 1945. That change of meaning is not a pedantic detail: a figure built on the older file and a figure built on the newer one can disagree for reasons that have nothing to do with geopolitics. In 2021, the last full year before the invasion, the file covers 193 countries, with the United States at 2.41 and China at −0.41 on the scale.

An ideal point is a continuous variable. A bloc is a category. Getting from one to the other requires a rule, and the rule is a modelling choice. The simplest serviceable rule runs: a country is US-leaning if it is closer to the United States than to China by more than a chosen margin, China-leaning if the reverse holds, and non-aligned otherwise. The margin is a parameter, which means it can be moved — and when it moves, countries change bloc and the result changes with them. A result that survives a range of reasonable margins is evidence of fragmentation. A result that appears at one margin and vanishes at another is evidence of the margin.

The trade side of the comparison has its own decisions. The source is the IMF’s International Trade in Goods by partner country, the dataset formerly known as the Direction of Trade Statistics (International Monetary Fund 2026). It reports, for each reporting country and each partner, the value of goods exports and imports in US dollars. Two things about it shape any analysis built on it. The first is the mirror problem: A’s reported exports to B are not equal to B’s reported imports from A, because of timing, of valuation (exports are recorded free on board, imports including cost, insurance and freight), of re-exports and of confidentiality. An analysis must pick one side and say which, because the two sides can diverge most where trade is routed through third countries — precisely where the interesting movement is. The second is that the values are in current US dollars. When commodity prices rise, the dollar value of trade rises without a single extra barrel or tonne crossing a border, and 2022 was a year of very high energy and food prices. Trade “within” a bloc that includes large energy exporters can rise for reasons that have nothing to do with alignment.

The final step is the index. Each series — trade within the US-leaning bloc, within the China-leaning bloc, between the two, and involving the non-aligned — is divided by its own value in a base year and multiplied by a hundred. Indexing removes the enormous differences in level between the series and lets the eye compare their trajectories, which is what fragmentation is about. It also hands enormous weight to the base year. Index to 2021 and the post-COVID rebound of 2021 is built into the denominator; index to 2019 and it is not. Two honest analyses of the same data can therefore disagree about the size of the break simply because they chose different base years, and the base year belongs in the caption of any such figure, not in a footnote.

4.10 What fragmentation does to a multinational, channel by channel

This book follows five channels through which geopolitical risk reaches a firm. Fragmentation touches all of them, but not equally, and a firm that can say which channel matters most for its own business has already done most of the analysis.

Market access is the most direct. A firm whose markets sit in different blocs faces the prospect that the rules governing its sales — tariffs, export controls, procurement preferences, product standards — will diverge between them. The first practical step is unglamorous: classify the firm’s most important markets by alignment, and look not only at where they sit but at whether they are drifting.

Inputs are where friend-shoring and de-risking bite. Fragmentation in the data is concentrated in strategic sectors — semiconductors, critical minerals, advanced machinery, energy — and so is the policy pressure. A maker of consumer goods may see little beyond rising transport and compliance costs; a maker of semiconductor equipment may find its suppliers, customers and licences reorganised by law. A supplier in a connector country may be the cheapest route around a bloc boundary — and the next target of rules-of-origin enforcement, because governments that wish to make fragmentation stick have every reason to close the routes around it.

Finance follows the same lines. The between-bloc decline Gopinath and co-authors find in foreign direct investment reaches firms through investment screening, restrictions on outbound investment in sensitive technologies, and the cost of capital for projects that straddle the boundary. Payments, too, run through hubs of the kind Farrell and Newman (2019) describe, and access to them is a matter of jurisdiction.

Assets and people are exposed in the connector countries. A plant built to serve both blocs from a non-aligned location is a bet that the location will stay non-aligned and that both blocs will keep accepting its goods. Staff who move between blocs face visa regimes and export-control rules on the technical knowledge they carry.

Data and operations fragment as well: rules on data localisation, on cloud providers and on cross-border transfers increasingly follow the same alignment, and a firm whose systems are integrated across blocs may find that integration itself becomes a compliance problem.

Across all five channels the same distinction from Martin, Mayer and Thoenig applies. The aggregate economy has substitutes; the firm has contracts. When a government decides that de-risking is affordable because the country’s imports can be sourced elsewhere, it is making a claim about averages; the firm’s own substitutes may be few, slow and expensive. If the data show de-risking — selective reductions in strategic sectors with overall trade preserved — a blanket exit from the other bloc overreacts; if they show de-coupling, waiting overreacts the other way. The evidence so far is closer to the former, and the right response is sectoral.

A tempting but wrong conclusion is that non-aligned markets are safe because they are on neither side. They are where between-bloc trade is being rerouted, and they are therefore where enforcement, scrutiny and secondary sanctions will concentrate. Non-alignment is a position in a contest, not an exemption from it.

4.11 A short review of the literature

The commercial peace has a long pre-history in political philosophy and a short history as a testable proposition. Angell (1910) gave it its most influential popular form and its most notorious timing. The modern empirical literature begins with Polachek (1980), who modelled conflict as an activity priced by the gains from trade it endangers. Oneal and Russett (1997) and Russett and Oneal (2001) established the dyadic correlation between interdependence and peace, alongside democracy and international organisations; Barbieri (1996) and Barbieri (2002) challenged it with longer series and different measures, and much of the subsequent dispute concerned measurement as much as theory. Mansfield and Pevehouse (2000) showed that preferential trading arrangements dampen conflict, and Mansfield (2021) is the most recent and compact review of the whole field.

The realist tradition starts from Hirschman (1945), whose distinction between the supply and influence effects of trade, and whose concentration index, remain the foundation of the study of economic dependence as power. Waltz (1979) placed dependence within structural realism as a vulnerability states will seek to limit. Gowa and Mansfield (1993) and Gowa (1994) identified the security externalities of trade and showed that allies trade more with each other. Copeland (1996), developed at length in Copeland (2015), reconciled the two camps by making expectations of future trade the variable that decides whether interdependence restrains or provokes. Martin, Mayer, and Thoenig (2008) separated bilateral from multilateral openness and showed that globalisation can lower the cost of bilateral conflict.

The contemporary turn is from trade as a restraint to trade as an instrument. Farrell and Newman (2019) recast interdependence as a network with hubs that confer panopticon and chokepoint power. Aiyar et al. (2023) defined geoeconomic fragmentation and set out its channels; Gopinath et al. (2024), published as Gopinath et al. (2025), provided the first systematic evidence that trade and investment have begun to reorganise along lines of foreign-policy alignment; Freund et al. (2024) and Alfaro and Chor (2023) show, for US imports, that the reallocation away from China runs largely through countries tied into China’s own supply chains. The measurement of that alignment rests on a parallel literature: Signorino and Ritter (1999) on the pitfalls of agreement scores, Voeten (2000) on the post-Cold War structure of General Assembly voting, and Bailey, Strezhnev, and Voeten (2017) on dynamic ideal points comparable over time. Le Billon and Shykora (2020) extends the analysis from manufactured goods to commodities, showing how supply chains themselves carry conflict.

The through-line is a shift in what is being explained. The older literature asked whether economic ties prevent war. The newer one asks how the expectation of conflict reorganises economic ties — and therefore treats trade data not as a cause of peace but as a record of how states expect each other to behave.

4.12 What the evidence does not license

Suppose the lines separate after 2022 exactly as Gopinath et al. (2025) report. The temptation is to read the separation as a finding about geopolitics. It is a finding about a classification, a currency and a date, and each carries part of the result.

It is a correlation with a date: sanctions on Russia, the post-pandemic rebound and the surge in commodity prices all moved trade in 2022, and a gravity specification separates only some of them. Bloc membership is a modelling choice: move the margin and countries move between blocs, and a gap that moves with them is partly an artefact of where the lines were drawn. Values are not volumes, as the energy exporters’ 2022 figures show. And the result is aggregate: a firm’s products may sit in a sector where fragmentation is far stronger than the average, or in one where it is absent.

What the evidence does establish is narrower and still worth having: the broad pattern is visible in public data under transparent choices, a firm’s markets can be placed in blocs under a stated rule whose fragility can be shown, and the connectors can be identified — where, on the evidence of Freund et al. (2024), dependence on the rival has often been relabelled rather than removed.

Angell was right about the costs of war and wrong about what leaders would do with that knowledge; Copeland located the danger in expectations rather than in levels of trade. The fragmentation data are best read in the same spirit: not as proof that geopolitics has caused trade to split, but as a record of what states and firms now expect of one another. The evidence does not license the conclusion that alignment caused the separation, that a firm’s own sector follows the aggregate, that its non-aligned markets are safe, or that blocs drawn at one margin in one year will be the blocs that matter in five. It licenses something more modest and more useful: the knowledge that the expectation of a divided world is already reorganising the network the firm depends on, in places the averages do not show.
