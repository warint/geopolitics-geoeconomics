4.2 The lecture

The session opens with Quiz 1 (20 minutes) and four Risk Briefs (20 minutes); the lecture takes the remaining fifty.

4.2.1 1. Does trade keep the peace? The liberal answer

The commercial peace is one of the oldest ideas in international political economy. From Montesquieu (“peace is the natural effect of trade”) to Norman Angell’s The Great Illusion (1910), the argument is that trade raises the opportunity cost of war: states that gain from exchange lose those gains if they fight.

Modern versions add mechanisms:

  • Opportunity cost. Conflict disrupts trade; the more a dyad trades, the more it loses.
  • Signalling. Interdependent states can send costly signals — threatening economic ties — short of force, which reduces miscalculation.
  • Domestic coalitions. Trade creates exporters and importers with a stake in peaceful relations who lobby their governments.
  • Institutions. Trade agreements create forums and dispute-settlement procedures.

Statistical studies of dyads from the 1990s onwards (notably Russett & Oneal’s Triangulating Peace, 2001) found that dyads with more trade relative to GDP experience fewer militarised disputes.

4.2.2 2. The realist critique

Realists reply that interdependence can cause conflict:

  • Vulnerability. Dependence on a rival for essential goods is a security risk. States therefore limit it — or fight to secure supplies (Waltz, 1979).
  • Relative gains (Session 03). If trade makes the rival stronger faster, it is a threat.
  • Security externalities (Gowa, 1994). Trade raises the economic capacity of the partner; states therefore trade more with allies than with adversaries.
  • Expectations of future trade (Copeland, 2015). What matters is not today’s trade but whether leaders expect it to continue. When they expect to be cut off, interdependence becomes a reason to act before the window closes.

The history the realists point to: Britain and Germany were heavily interdependent in 1914.

4.2.3 3. What the evidence says: Mansfield (2021)

Mansfield’s review in the Oxford Handbook of International Political Economy sets out the debate and the main empirical findings. Four points to retain:

  1. The relationship runs both ways. Conflict reduces trade, so a negative correlation between trade and conflict does not by itself show that trade causes peace.
  2. Institutions matter. Preferential trading arrangements are associated with fewer disputes among members (Mansfield & Pevehouse, 2000).
  3. Context matters. The effect differs with regime type, the level of development, and the distribution of power between the partners.
  4. The question has changed. As geopolitical competition returned, research has turned from “does trade pacify?” to “how do states use trade as an instrument of conflict?” — the questions of Sessions 08, 10 and 11.

4.2.4 4. Bilateral versus multilateral openness

Martin, Mayer & Thoenig (2008), Make Trade Not War?, Review of Economic Studies, 75(3), 865–900 (doi:10.1111/j.1467-937X.2008.00492.x).

Their argument separates two effects:

Effect on the risk of a bilateral conflict
Bilateral trade between A and B lowers it: A and B have more to lose from fighting each other
Multilateral trade openness of A and B with the rest of the world raises it: if A can trade with everyone else, losing B costs less

The implication is uncomfortable for the liberal view: globalisation lowers the cost of bilateral conflict because it provides substitutes. A world of many alternative suppliers makes it cheaper to break with any one of them. Their empirical work finds support for both effects.

4.2.5 5. Fragmentation: the new question

Friend-shoring (a term popularised in US policy debate in 2022) proposes to move supply chains towards trusted partners. De-risking (the European Commission’s term, 2023) proposes to reduce dependence on rivals in critical goods without breaking trade overall. De-coupling is the end point: separate economic blocs.

Gopinath, Gourinchas, Presbitero & Topalova (2025) ask whether the data show this happening. Their design:

  1. Define blocs. Countries are grouped into a US-leaning bloc, a China-leaning bloc and a non-aligned group on the basis of their foreign-policy alignment as revealed by UN General Assembly voting. Read Section 2 of the paper for exactly which vote or measure, and which year, they use — ours will be a simplification, and you must say how it differs.
  2. Compare trade within and between blocs before and after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, controlling for the usual determinants of bilateral trade.
  3. Look for connectors — non-aligned countries whose trade with both blocs grew, through which some between-bloc trade may be rerouted.

Their main finding: after the invasion, trade between the two blocs fell relative to trade within them, by a margin of the order of ten percent or more (find the exact estimate in the paper and write it in your notes), and foreign direct investment shows a similar pattern. They are careful to say this is a fragmentation still small compared with the Cold War — but it is happening, and it is concentrated in strategic sectors.

4.2.6 6. What it means for the multinational

Classify your markets by alignment Which of your five most important countries (the team milestone) sit in which bloc — and which are drifting?
Connectors are opportunities and risks Mexico, Vietnam and others gain between-bloc trade — and attract scrutiny for transshipment and rules-of-origin enforcement
The cost of switching is falling for states, not for you Martin, Mayer & Thoenig: multilateral openness makes it cheaper for states to break with a partner. Your supply contracts do not get cheaper to break
De-risking is sectoral Fragmentation is concentrated in strategic goods; a firm in consumer goods and one in semiconductors face different worlds

4.2.7 7. What the figure does not license

  • A fall in between-bloc trade is a correlation with a date, not proof that geopolitics caused it: sanctions on Russia, the post-COVID rebound and price changes all move trade values in the same years.
  • Bloc membership is a modelling choice. Change the threshold or the base year and countries move between blocs — and the figure moves with them.
  • Values, not volumes. Trade in US dollars rises with commodity prices; 2022 was a year of very high energy and food prices.
  • It shows aggregate trade. Your firm’s products may be in a sector where fragmentation is far stronger — or absent.

4.2.8 8. The Tableau skill of the day

A dyadic table (exporter × partner × year) needs two copies of the country table: one for the exporter’s bloc, one for the partner’s. In Tableau’s data model, add the bloc table, relate it on exporter = country, then add it again and relate the second copy on partner = country. Blocs are sets defined by a condition on the ideal point, with parameters as thresholds. The trade series is indexed to a base year with a table calculation. Click-path: ../02-practice/README.md.

4.3 In the practice

The target. Gopinath, Gourinchas, Presbitero & Topalova (2025): trade between geopolitical blocs against trade within them, before and after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. You define the blocs yourself from UN General Assembly ideal points, then watch what happens to the finding when you move the line.

Work in your team. One extract per team; everyone builds their own workbook.

4.3.1 1 · Collect — 10 min

Ideal points and codebook from Dataverse; your IMF IMTS extract of bilateral goods exports, annual, 2015–latest. If not done: ../data/README.md.

Provenance log: one line per file. For the ideal points, log version 39 and the change to calendar-year estimation. For IMF, log the date of the extract and the exact indicator you chose.

4.3.2 2 · Prepare — 25 min

4.3.2.1 A · Classify countries into blocs (ideal points)

  1. Connect → Text file → IdealpointestimatesFP_2026FP.csv. The ISO 3-letter code, the year and the ideal point are iso3c, year and IdealPointFP (see the codebook). Rename iso3c → iso3 and IdealPointFP → IdealPoint (right-click → Rename): the steps below use those names.

  2. New sheet. Filter year = 2021 — the last full year before the invasion. (Gopinath et al. may use a different reference: check, and log the difference.)

  3. Read the ideal points of the US (USA) and China (CHN) off the data. Create two parameters, US ref and CHN ref (Float), and type those two values in.

  4. Create a third parameter Margin (Float, start at 0.5) and the calculated field:

    Bloc =
IF ABS([IdealPoint] - [CHN ref]) - ABS([IdealPoint] - [US ref]) > [Margin] THEN "US-leaning"
ELSEIF ABS([IdealPoint] - [US ref]) - ABS([IdealPoint] - [CHN ref]) > [Margin] THEN "China-leaning"
ELSE "Non-aligned"
END

  5. iso3 → Rows, Bloc → Rows, IdealPoint → Text. Sort by ideal point. Does the classification look right for countries you know? Adjust Margin until you can defend it — and write down the value and why.

  6. Export the classification as a derived file: Worksheet → Export → Crosstab to Excel (or View Data → Export All to CSV). Save it as data/derived/blocs_2021.csv — not in raw/. Add a provenance line: derived from the ideal points, Margin = …, base year 2021.

4.3.2.2 B · Relate trade to blocs — the dyad model

  1. New data source: Connect → Text file (or Excel) → your IMF extract. Identify the exporter, partner, year and value columns — in the IMF’s SDMX layout COUNTRY, COUNTERPART_COUNTRY, TIME_PERIOD, OBS_VALUE — and rename them exporter, partner, year, value. Keep only INDICATOR = XG_FOB_USD.
  2. Remove aggregate partners (“World”, “Advanced Economies”, regions — codes starting with G or TX, e.g. G001 World): filter them out, and log which.
  3. In the data source pane, drag blocs_2021.csv onto the canvas: Tableau creates a relationship. Click the noodle → relate exporter = iso3.
  4. Drag blocs_2021.csv onto the canvas a second time. Relate partner = iso3. Rename this copy Partner blocs and its Bloc field Partner bloc; rename the first copy’s field Exporter bloc.
  5. IMTS uses ISO3 codes, so the relationship works directly. If your download shows names instead, you need a concordance table — log it.

4.3.3 3 · Replicate — 25 min

4.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “Trade within and between blocs”

  1. The pair type:

    Pair type =
IF [Exporter bloc] = "Non-aligned" OR [Partner bloc] = "Non-aligned" THEN "Involves non-aligned"
ELSEIF [Exporter bloc] = [Partner bloc] THEN "Within bloc"
ELSE "Between US- and China-leaning"
END

  2. The index, 2021 = 100:

    Trade index = SUM([value]) / WINDOW_SUM(SUM(IF [year] = 2021 THEN [value] END)) * 100

  3. year → Columns as a discrete dimension (blue); Trade index → Rows; Pair type → Colour. Right-click Trade index → Edit Table Calculation → Compute Using → year, so each pair type is indexed to its own 2021.

  4. Reference line at 100. Annotate 2022.

  5. Title with the finding. Caption: both sources with versions, and “Blocs: ideal-point distance to US vs China, 2021, margin = …”.

4.3.3.2 Sheet 2 · “Who sits where?” — the classification on a map

iso3 (geographic role Country/Region, ISO 3166-1 alpha-3) on the map, coloured by Bloc. Mark your team firm’s five main countries with a group (Create → Group, “Our markets”).

4.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 20 min

  1. Compare. Put Sheet 1 beside the paper’s figure. Their data are quarterly and their estimate controls for other determinants of trade; ours is annual and raw. Does the direction match? Is the gap between “within” and “between” of a similar order? Write one difference and its likely cause.
  2. Break the bloc definition. Move Margin (0.25, 1.0), re-export, refresh. Which countries change bloc? Does the gap between the lines survive? This is the most important answer of the round.
  3. Break the side of the trade. If your extract has the mirror series (imports reported by the partner), rebuild the index with it. Same picture?
  4. Extend — connectors. Create a set of non-aligned countries (Create → Set, Top tab: top 10 by growth in exports to both blocs, 2021 → latest — or pick by hand from a table). Which are they? Is any of them among your firm’s markets?

4.3.7 What loses credit

  • Joining on country names (Türkiye, Côte d’Ivoire, Korea…)
  • Leaving “World” or regional aggregates among the partners
  • An index computed across pair types instead of within each one
  • A bloc threshold that is not reported on the slide
