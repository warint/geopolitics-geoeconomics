5  International institutions

Session 5 · Arenas of power, or constraints on it?
Pre-session slides · Lecture slides · Practice slides · Session folder · Data instructions

When a multinational relies on an international institution to manage its risk, is it relying on a rule — or on the states that buy and sell influence inside it?

Big work Kuziemko, I., & Werker, E. (2006). How much is a seat on the Security Council worth? Foreign aid and bribery at the United Nations. Journal of Political Economy, 114(5), 905–930. doi:10.1086/507155
Target figure US aid to elected Security Council members in event time — years −3 to +3 around the start of a two-year term
Data you collect UN Security Council elected members (United Nations); US foreign aid (Greenbook, ForeignAssistance.gov or its mirror) — or the authors’ replication file from AidData
Tableau skill LOD expressions (FIXED) · years relative to an event · event-time charts · reference bands

5.1.1 1. The reading — 60–75 min

Kuziemko, I., & Werker, E. (2006). How much is a seat on the Security Council worth? Foreign aid and bribery at the United Nations. Journal of Political Economy, 114(5), 905–930. doi:10.1086/507155

Read for the argument, not for coverage. Annotate four things:

What to look for
1 The research question, in one sentence
2 The identification idea: why does the rotation of elected seats let the authors separate the effect of the seat from everything else about a country?
3 The strongest single piece of evidence — and the evidence that the effect is larger when the Council matters more
4 One limitation you would raise as a referee — or as a firm relying on a UN resolution

The target figure is the paper’s evidence on aid in event time: aid in the years before, during and after a country’s term, year by year. Find where the paper shows it — as a figure, and in the year-by-year estimates behind it — and write down what is on each axis.

Background, optional: Keohane, R. O. (1984). After Hegemony: Cooperation and Discord in the World Political Economy. Princeton University Press — chapter 6, on why states build institutions. Mearsheimer, J. J. (1994/95). The false promise of international institutions. International Security, 19(3), 5–49 — the realist reply.

Decide as a team which route you take: details in ../data/README.md.

5.1.3 3. Self-check — 15 min

Answer on paper.

  1. In one sentence each: why do states build institutions, according to a liberal institutionalist and according to a realist?
  2. How many members does the Security Council have, how many are permanent, and how long is an elected member’s term?
  3. Why does the paper compare a country with itself in other years rather than with countries that never served?
  4. A Security Council resolution under Chapter VII imposes sanctions on a state where your firm has a distributor. Who is bound by it — and why should the firm care how the resolution was voted?

5.2 The lecture

5.2.1 1. Why would states under anarchy build institutions at all?

Session 03 left us with a system without a government above states. Session 04 showed trade reorganising itself along geopolitical lines. Yet the same system is dense with institutions — the UN, the WTO, the IMF, the World Bank, investment treaties, export-control regimes. Why?

Liberal institutionalism Realism
Key text Keohane (1984), After Hegemony Mearsheimer (1994/95), The False Promise of International Institutions
Why institutions exist They lower the transaction costs of cooperation, provide information, and lengthen the shadow of the future so that states can make credible commitments They reflect the distribution of power; the strong build them to serve their interests
Do they constrain states? Yes, at the margin — states comply because the institution makes reneging costly and cooperation valuable Rarely — when interests and rules collide, power wins
What it predicts for a firm Rules are a reasonably stable floor: dispute settlement, treaties, sanctions regimes behave predictably Rules are only as stable as the coalition behind them; read the coalition, not the treaty

Neither is a strawman. The useful question for a firm is not which theory is “true” but which institution, on which issue, behaves like which. That is an empirical question — and Kuziemko and Werker give one of its sharpest answers.

5.2.2 2. The Security Council in five facts

  1. Fifteen members: five permanent (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, the United States) and ten elected. Before 1966 there were six elected members.
  2. Elected members serve two-year terms, five elected each year by the General Assembly, by regional group, with a two-thirds majority; they cannot be immediately re-elected.
  3. Decisions on substantive matters need nine votes and no veto from a permanent member.
  4. Under Chapter VII the Council can impose measures — sanctions, arms embargoes, the use of force — that bind every UN member state.
  5. An elected member’s vote can be pivotal: the P5 need nine votes, so they must court elected members.

5.2.3 3. The price of a seat — Kuziemko and Werker (2006)

The question. Do powerful states pay for the votes of elected Security Council members?

The identification idea. Countries do not choose when they rotate onto the Council in any way that tracks their need for aid — rotation follows regional queues and norms. So the authors compare a country with itself in the years before, during and after its term. If aid rises during the term and falls back afterwards, the seat — not the country’s poverty, alliances or politics — is the likeliest explanation.

The findings, in the paper’s own summary:

  • US aid to a developing country rises substantially — by about 59% in the abstract’s headline estimate — when it rotates onto the Council, and aid through the UN rises too, more modestly.
  • The increase is concentrated in the term years and disappears after the term.
  • The effect is larger in years when the Council matters more — measured by how much the press covered it.

Why that last result matters. It is the difference between a statistical curiosity and a mechanism. If aid followed membership whatever the Council was doing, rotation might proxy for something else. That aid tracks the salience of Council business is what makes “vote buying” the most plausible reading.

The follow-up literature. Dreher, Sturm and Vreeland extended the question to the World Bank and the IMF, and Vreeland and Dreher (2014), The Political Economy of the United Nations Security Council: Money and Influence (Cambridge University Press), brings the evidence together. The common thread: temporary members of the Council receive more favourable treatment in several channels.

5.2.4 4. What it means for multinational firms

Institutions enter a firm’s risk in at least four places:

Where Institution What the firm relies on What Kuziemko and Werker warn
Sanctions UN Security Council (Chapter VII); then national and EU regimes (Session 10) A predictable list of who is sanctioned and why Resolutions are bargained; votes have prices; a P5 veto blocks UN sanctions — and pushes them into unilateral national regimes that diverge
Investment protection Bilateral investment treaties; ICSID arbitration A neutral forum if a host state expropriates Treaties are only as strong as the host state’s willingness to pay awards
Political risk insurance MIGA (World Bank Group), national export-credit agencies Cover against expropriation, war, transfer restrictions The insurer’s own shareholders are states — cover is a political decision too
Trade rules WTO dispute settlement Tariff bindings, non-discrimination The Appellate Body has been unable to function since 2019; “rules” now depend on the coalition

The lesson for the firm. An international institution is a risk mitigator when the coalition that sustains it is stable, and a risk source when it is not. A risk dashboard should therefore track not only the rules but the coalition: who sits on the Council, how they vote, what they are being offered — which is exactly what today’s practice round builds.

5.2.5 5. The Tableau skill of the day — LOD expressions

The practice needs two numbers for every country-year that do not exist in either file:

Years relative to a term. Join the term table (one row per term) to the aid table (one row per country-year) on the country code — a join in the physical layer, not a relationship, because a row-level calculation cannot mix fields from two related tables. Each country-year now appears once per term of that country, which is exactly what an event study needs:

[Event time]   =  [Year] - [Term start]

The country’s own pre-term baseline, a Level of Detail expression that fixes the computation at the country × term level whatever is in the view:

[Baseline aid] = { FIXED [Iso3], [Term start] :
                   AVG( IF [Event time] >= -3 AND [Event time] <= -1 THEN [Aid] END ) }

[Aid relative to baseline] = [Aid] / [Baseline aid]      // row level: one value per country-year-term

FIXED computes before the view’s dimension filters (context filters apply first) and ignores what is on Rows and Columns: every row of a term carries that term’s baseline. The ratio is then computed row by row, and averaging it at each event year gives the average across terms of a ratio computed within each term.

5.3 In the practice

The target. Kuziemko & Werker (2006): US aid to developing countries in the years around a Security Council term — flat before, higher during the two term years, back down after.

In your teams. One workbook per team; everyone builds at least one sheet.

5.3.1 1 · Collect — 15 min

You should arrive with route A or route B files in data/raw/ (../data/README.md). Finish what is missing, and fill the provenance log — one line per file.

file source url version / release accessed licence notes
us_foreign_aid_country.csv US State Dept (ForeignAssistance.gov, Country Summary) foreignassistance.gov/data or the mirror release / “frozen 2024-12-19” today free fiscal years; obligations; constant_amount; economic + military combined
unsc_elected_members.xlsx United Nations main.un.org/securitycouncil/… page date today free your transcription; name changes handled

Route A teams also need a term table. Open the replication file, filter on Security Council membership = 1, show country × year as a text table, export it (Worksheet → Export → Crosstab to Excel), and mark the first year of each two-year run as term_start. Ten minutes; it gives you the same term table as route B.

5.3.2 2 · Prepare — 20 min

  1. Connect to the aid file and to unsc_elected_members.xlsx. In the Country Summary, filter Transaction Type Name = Obligations (a data-source filter), exclude Fiscal Year = 1976tq, then make Fiscal Year a number and rename it Year; rename constant_amount to Aid.
  2. Drag the aid table to the canvas, then double-click it to open the physical layer, and drag the term table next to it: inner join on Country Code = Iso3 (or on country name only if you have checked every match — see the trap list in TABLEAU-TOOLKIT.md). Route A teams: use the replication file’s own country, year and aid fields. > Why a join, not a relationship? The next step is a row-level calculation that uses Year from > one table and Term start from the other. Relationships do not allow that.
  3. Check the row count before and after the join and write both in your log. The join should multiply rows: each country-year now appears once per term of that country.
  4. Keep only aid recipients (drop regional aggregates such as “World”, “Africa Region” — every Country Name ending in “Region”), and check that aid is in constant dollars (constant_amount, not current_amount). If it is not, say so in the log — the ratio to baseline below limits the damage, but does not remove it.

5.3.3 3 · Replicate — 25 min

5.3.3.1 Calculated fields

[Event time]               = [Year] - [Term start]

[Baseline aid]             = { FIXED [Iso3], [Term start] :
                               AVG( IF [Event time] >= -3 AND [Event time] <= -1
                                    THEN [Aid] END ) }

[Aid relative to baseline] = [Aid] / [Baseline aid]      // row level: one value per country-year-term

Route A teams: if aid is in logs in the file, use [Log aid] - [Baseline log aid] instead of a ratio.

5.3.3.2 Sheet 1 · “Aid in event time”

  1. Filter [Event time] to −3 … +3 (make it a dimension, discrete).
  2. [Event time] → Columns; [Aid relative to baseline] → Rows, then change its aggregation to AVG (right-click the pill → Measure → Average). Because the ratio is computed row by row — one row per country-year-term — the average at each event year is the average across terms.
  3. Mark type Line, then add Circle marks on a dual axis so each year is visible.
  4. Analytics → Reference Band from 0 to 1 on [Event time], shaded, labelled “On the Council”.
  5. Constant reference line at 1.0, labelled “Pre-term baseline”.
  6. Title it with the finding; caption with both sources, versions and access dates.

5.3.3.3 Sheet 2 · “How many terms?”

[Event time] → Columns, COUNTD([Iso3]) → Rows. The number of terms behind each point. Is the sample balanced across event years? If not, why?

5.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 20 min

  1. Differ. Put your Sheet 1 beside the paper’s event-time evidence. Same shape? Same size? Write one difference and its most likely cause (release of the aid data, years covered, the sample of countries, constant vs current dollars, levels vs logs).
  2. Break one decision. Replace the mean across terms by the median (a quick change of aggregation). Does the term-year bump survive? What does that tell you about whether a few large cases drive it?
  3. Break another. Restrict to terms starting after 1990. Does the pattern survive the end of the Cold War?
  4. Extend — salience. The paper finds the effect is larger when the Council matters more. Build a crude proxy: tag the years 1990–91 (Gulf War), 2002–03 (Iraq) as high salience with a calculated field, and colour Sheet 1 by it. What do you see — and why is two episodes far too few to conclude?

5.3.5 5 · The catch — 10 min

Original Ours The catch
The paper’s event-time evidence Your Sheet 1 What it does not license a multinational to conclude — for instance about how durable a UN sanctions resolution is, or what a host government on the Council will do

A member drawn at random presents in two minutes.
