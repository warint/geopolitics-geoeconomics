8.2 The lecture

The session opens with four Risk Briefs (20 min). The lecture follows.

8.2.1 1. Is cyberspace a domain of geopolitics?

Two caricatures dominate public debate. In the first, cyberspace is a revolutionary battlefield: a “cyber Pearl Harbor” can switch off a country’s grid, and small states or non-state actors can humble great powers. In the second, cyber operations are simply crime and espionage with better tools, of no strategic consequence.

Both are claims about evidence, and this session puts them to data. The question for a multinational is narrower and more practical: through which channels does inter-state rivalry in cyberspace reach my operations, and how should my decision architecture absorb it?

Three kinds of activity are easily confused:

Purpose Typical actor Example of the type
Espionage Steal information — state secrets or intellectual property intelligence services, contractors intrusion into a defence supplier’s network
Disruption and degradation Interrupt or damage systems military and intelligence units, proxies denial of service against a ministry; wiper malware
Crime Extract money criminal groups, sometimes tolerated or used by states ransomware against a hospital or a pipeline operator

The boundaries blur — states tolerate, hire or shelter criminal groups — and that blurring is itself a geopolitical fact.

8.2.2 2. Valeriano and Maness: cyber war versus cyber realities

Valeriano and Maness (2015) built a dataset of cyber incidents and campaigns between rival states — pairs of states with a history of enduring hostility — and asked what the record actually shows. Their argument, in four propositions:

  1. Restraint. States use cyber operations far less, and far less destructively, than the “cyber war” narrative predicts. The severity of most incidents is low: defacements, denial of service, espionage — rarely physical destruction.
  2. Regional rivals. Cyber conflict mostly follows existing rivalries — the United States and China, Russia and its neighbours, Israel and Iran, India and Pakistan, the two Koreas. Cyber does not create new conflicts; it is a tool in old ones.
  3. Why restraint? Fear of escalation and blowback, the risk of revealing and losing a capability once used, the possibility that a weapon is reverse-engineered and turned back, and the value of keeping networks intact to spy on them.
  4. Limited coercive effect. Cyber operations rarely change the target’s behaviour. They are better at signalling and spying than at compelling.

The Dyadic Cyber Incident and Campaign Data (DCID) extends that work; version 2.0 covers 2000–2020. It is today’s replication data.

What restraint does not mean for the firm. Low strategic severity is perfectly compatible with high commercial cost. An operation that barely registers on a scale of interstate harm can still halt a company’s production for weeks.

8.2.3 3. Farrell and Newman: weaponised interdependence

Farrell and Newman (2019) look at the networks that globalisation built — finance, communications, the internet’s infrastructure — and observe that they are not flat. They have hubs. States that have jurisdiction over a hub can use it:

Effect Mechanism Example of the type
Panopticon Use access to the hub to see what flows through it intelligence access to data transiting cables, data centres or cloud providers under a state’s jurisdiction
Chokepoint Use control of the hub to deny access to others cutting banks out of a financial-messaging network; denying firms access to software, chips or cloud services

For a multinational, the implication is uncomfortable: the firm itself can be the hub. Its cloud, its payment rails, its software updates are infrastructure that its home state can watch or weaponise — and that rival states therefore treat as a target. This is the bridge from cyber (today) to sanctions (Session 10) and space (Session 11).

8.2.4 4. The attribution problem

Every cyber dataset rests on attribution: someone has to say who did it. Attribution is

  • slow — months or years after the event;
  • probabilistic — technical indicators can be faked or reused;
  • political — governments choose when to attribute publicly, and to whom;
  • selective — what is attributed is what someone wanted known.

So event data on cyber operations is a record of public attributions, not a record of operations. That is the most important caveat of today’s practice round — and the heart of its “catch”.

8.2.5 5. When the firm is collateral: NotPetya and its lessons

In June 2017 the NotPetya malware, spread through a Ukrainian accounting-software update and widely attributed by Western governments to Russian military intelligence, spread far beyond its apparent target. Shipping, pharmaceutical, food and logistics multinationals reported very large losses — Maersk, Merck and Mondelez among them.

Three lessons for the firm:

  1. Proximity is technical, not political. Firms were hit because they ran a piece of software in Ukraine, not because anyone chose them.
  2. Insurance is not a backstop. Insurers invoked war exclusions to contest claims; the litigation that followed pushed the market to write explicit exclusions for state-backed cyber operations (Lloyd’s required them from 2023). A firm’s cyber cover may end exactly where geopolitics begins.
  3. The board owns it. Securities regulators increasingly require disclosure of material cyber incidents and of board oversight of cyber risk.

8.2.6 6. Digital sovereignty and the splintering of the network

States increasingly regulate where data sits and who may access it: data-localisation rules, restrictions on cross-border data transfers, security reviews of foreign cloud and telecoms equipment, bans on particular vendors. For a multinational this is geopolitical risk in its architecture:

  • Duplication costs — separate data centres or cloud regions per jurisdiction;
  • Conflicting obligations — one state’s law requires disclosure that another’s forbids;
  • Vendor risk — a supplier banned in one market may be mandatory in another.

8.2.7 7. Cyber risk in the decision architecture of the firm

Who Role in geopolitical cyber risk
Board Sets risk appetite; oversees disclosure; asks which state actors would want our data or our downtime?
CISO Turns threat intelligence into controls; owns incident response
Chief risk officer Integrates cyber with country, sanctions and supply-chain risk — the team-project view
Insurer Prices the risk — and draws the line at state-backed operations
Regulators Impose disclosure, localisation and vendor rules

The question to bring to the practice round: if the dataset shows where interstate cyber conflict concentrates, how should a firm with operations in those dyads change its architecture?

8.3 In the practice

The target. Valeriano & Maness’s descriptive picture of interstate cyber conflict: incidents by year and method, the distribution of severity, and the initiator × target matrix of rival dyads — rebuilt from DCID v2.0, then confronted with the wider EuRepoC record.

Work in your project teams. Everyone builds their own workbook; the team hands in one slide.

8.3.1 1 · Collect — 15 min

If you did the pre-session, both files are in data/raw/. If not:

  • DCID v2.0 — doi:10.7910/DVN/CQOMYV → file Master_DCID_2.0_Release_public_Sep2022.tab → Access File → Comma Separated Values (Original File Format). The codebook is not on Dataverse: Codebook 2.0 is on ryanmaness.com/cyber-conflict-dataset.
  • EuRepoC v1.3 — doi:10.5281/zenodo.14965395 → eurepoc_global_dataset_1_3.csv and eurepoc_codebook_1_2.pdf.

Provenance log — one line per file:

file source url version / release accessed licence notes
Master_DCID_2.0_…csv Maness, Valeriano et al. doi:10.7910/DVN/CQOMYV v2.0, Sep 2022 today CC0, cite original CSV behind the .tab; 433 rows
eurepoc_global_dataset_1_3.csv EuRepoC doi:10.5281/zenodo.14965395 v1.3 (1.3.2), Mar 2025 today CC BY-NC 4.0 3,414 rows

8.3.2 2 · Prepare — 15 min

  1. Connect → Text file → the DCID CSV. Look at the grid.

  2. Find the columns (checked in the September 2022 file — see ../data/README.md): initiator, StateA, StateB, Dyadpair, interactionstartdate, method, severity, targettype. There is no target column, and StateA/StateB are the dyad, not initiator and target. Write the exact names in your log.

  3. initiator is a COW code (16 values: 2 US, 365 Russia, 710 China, 630 Iran, 731 North Korea, 770 Pakistan, 666 Israel, 750 India, 732 South Korea…). Dyadpair joins the COW codes of StateA and StateB (2365 = 2 + 365; State B’s code is always the last three digits). Build names for both sides:

    [Initiator] = IF STR([initiator]) = RIGHT(STR([Dyadpair]), 3)
              THEN TRIM([StateB]) ELSE TRIM([StateA]) END
[Target]    = IF STR([initiator]) = RIGHT(STR([Dyadpair]), 3)
              THEN TRIM([StateA]) ELSE TRIM([StateB]) END

    Two incidents (313 and 335) name an initiator outside the dyad — check them by hand.

  4. method and targettype are codes. Alias them from the codebook: method 1 vandalism, 2 denial of service, 3 network intrusion, 4 network infiltration (4.1–4.4 are infiltration sub-types — group them, or round down with FLOOR([method])); targettype 1 private / non-state, 2 government non-military, 3 government military.

  5. interactionstartdate is month/day/year text (2/1/2008). If Tableau has not read it as a date, create Start Year = YEAR(DATEPARSE("M/d/yyyy", [interactionstartdate])).

  6. Record every recode in the log.

8.3.3 3 · Replicate — 25 min

8.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “Incidents per year, by method”

  1. Start Year → Columns (discrete is fine: one bar per year).
  2. COUNTD([Cyberincidentnum]) (or the table’s record count) → Rows.
  3. Method → Colour. Stacked bars.
  4. Title states the finding. Caption: Source: DCID v2.0 (Maness, Valeriano et al., 2022), accessed …

8.3.3.2 Sheet 2 · “How severe?”

  1. Severity → Columns (discrete), count → Rows. A bar chart of the distribution.
  2. Add a reference line at the median severity (Analytics → Median with Quartiles or a constant you computed).
  3. Annotate the highest-severity incidents: what were they?

8.3.3.3 Sheet 3 · “Who targets whom” — the dyad matrix

  1. [Initiator] → Rows, [Target] → Columns (the calculated fields from step 2.3).
  2. Count → Colour, then Show Me → highlight table. Add count to Label.
  3. Sort initiators by total count (right-click the pill → Sort → Field → Count → Descending).
  4. Which five cells hold most incidents? Do they match the book’s regional rivals?

8.3.3.4 Dashboard · link them

  1. New Dashboard → drag Sheet 3 on the left, Sheets 1 and 2 on the right.
  2. Dashboard → Actions → Add Action → Filter… — source sheet: Sheet 3; target sheets: 1 and 2; run on Select; clearing the selection shows all values.
  3. Click the US–China cell, then the Russia–Ukraine cell. Write down how their histories differ.

8.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 20 min

  1. Differ. Put your Sheets 1–2 beside the book’s descriptive figures. DCID v2.0 runs to 2020 (one incident starts in January 2021) and has been revised since 2015. Write one difference, and its likely cause.
  2. Break the population. Load EuRepoC as a second data source. Rebuild Sheet 1 on it — first with all incidents, then filtered to incidents attributed to state actors (field initiator_category; read the codebook to decide which values — State only, or also State affiliated actor). EuRepoC’s start_date is day.month.year text: DATEPARSE("dd.MM.yyyy", [start_date]). How many times larger is EuRepoC? What does the difference tell you about what DCID leaves out?
  3. Restraint, tested. In DCID, what share of incidents sits in the lowest severity levels? (Quick Table Calculation → Percent of Total on Sheet 2.) Does the evidence support restraint as of 2020? What would you need to know about 2022 onward?
  4. Extend — the firm. Using targettype = 1 (private / non-state), isolate incidents that hit non-government targets. Which dyads hit firms most? Name a sector you would worry about.

8.3.7 What loses credit

  • Comparing DCID with all of EuRepoC without saying so
  • Empty cells read as “no cyber conflict” rather than “no rivalry in the data”
  • Codes (1, 2, 3) left on the chart instead of aliased labels
  • A catch about the book rather than about what the firm may conclude
