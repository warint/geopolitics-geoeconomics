8  Cyber Geopolitics: Restraint, Rivals and Weaponised Networks

8.1 The war that was supposed to happen

For most of the past three decades, the public conversation about cyberspace and international politics has been conducted in the future tense. A “cyber Pearl Harbor” would switch off a country’s power grid; a handful of skilled operators would humble a great power; the next war would be won or lost before a shot was fired. The scenarios were vivid, and they were not absurd: modern economies do run on networked systems, and those systems can be broken. What the scenarios lacked was a record. They were claims about what could happen, offered in the absence of systematic evidence about what had.

The opposite caricature is just as easy to state. On this view cyber operations are simply crime and espionage conducted with better tools — a nuisance for security departments, of no strategic consequence, and no business of a book on geopolitics. It, too, is a claim about evidence, and it, too, is usually made without any.

This chapter takes the question to the data. It does so through the work of Brandon Valeriano and Ryan Maness, who built a dataset of cyber incidents between rival states and asked what the record actually shows (Valeriano and Maness 2015), and through the argument of Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman that the networks globalisation built are not flat, and that states with jurisdiction over their hubs can turn them into instruments of coercion (Farrell and Newman 2019). The two works answer different questions. The first asks how states use offensive cyber operations against each other. The second asks how states use the architecture of the global economy — including its digital architecture — to watch and to deny. A multinational needs both answers, because it sits inside the architecture and in the path of the operations.

The question for the firm is narrower and more practical than the question for the strategist. It is not whether cyberspace is a new domain of war. It is through which channels inter-state rivalry in cyberspace reaches a firm’s operations, and how its decision architecture should absorb it. The answer, this chapter argues, is that the firm is rarely the intended target of strategic cyber operations, frequently their collateral, and increasingly a piece of the infrastructure over which states compete.

8.2 Three activities that look alike

Three kinds of activity are routinely run together under the heading “cyber attack”, and the first task is to separate them.

Espionage steals information: state secrets, negotiating positions, intellectual property, the personal data of officials. Its typical actors are intelligence services and their contractors, and its typical intrusion — into a ministry, a defence supplier or a law firm advising on a merger — is designed never to be noticed. Disruption and degradation interrupt or damage systems: a denial-of-service attack that floods a ministry’s website, malware that wipes the disks of a company’s computers, an operation that manipulates an industrial control system. Its actors are military and intelligence units and the proxies they sponsor. Crime extracts money: ransomware that encrypts a hospital’s records, fraud against a bank’s payment systems. Its actors are criminal groups.

Cyber operation — the use of computer code or network access by an organised actor to obtain information from, disrupt, degrade or manipulate a target’s information systems. Espionage, disruption and crime are all cyber operations; they differ in purpose and actor, not in technique. The same intrusion may serve any of the three.

The boundaries blur, and the blurring is itself a geopolitical fact. States tolerate criminal groups that operate from their territory against foreign targets, hire them, shelter them, and occasionally use their tools. An intrusion that begins as espionage leaves access that can later be used for disruption. A ransomware campaign run by a criminal group may serve a state’s purposes by sowing disorder in a rival’s economy, without the state having ordered it. When a ransomware attack by a criminal group believed to operate from Russia forced the shutdown of the Colonial Pipeline in the United States in May 2021 (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency 2023), it was crime in its motive and geopolitics in its consequences: the question of what the Russian state knew, tolerated or could have prevented became a matter between governments.

For a multinational the distinction matters because the three activities call for different responses. Espionage is a threat to the firm’s intellectual property and its negotiating position, and the defence is largely technical and organisational. Disruption is a threat to its operations, and the defence includes resilience — the ability to keep working when systems fail. Crime is a threat to its cash and its reputation, and it is the one where law enforcement, insurers and the firm’s own willingness to pay become part of the calculation. A risk register that lists “cyber” as a single line has not yet done the first piece of analysis.

From the archive · Estonia, 2007

In April 2007 the Estonian government moved a Soviet-era war memorial, the Bronze Soldier, from the centre of Tallinn to a military cemetery. The decision provoked protests by members of the country’s Russian-speaking minority and an angry response from Moscow. Over the following weeks a wave of distributed denial-of-service attacks hit Estonian government websites, banks, newspapers and political parties. Estonia, among the most digitised societies in Europe, found its online services intermittently unreachable.

Estonian officials pointed to Russia. The Russian government denied involvement, and the public record never established a chain of command; much of the traffic came from botnets and from individuals mobilised on Russian-language forums. The episode thus contained, in compressed form, nearly every feature that later analysts would identify in state cyber conflict: an existing political rivalry, an operation of low technical sophistication and limited physical harm, high political salience, and attribution that remained contested.

Its institutional legacy was substantial. NATO’s Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence was established in Tallinn the following year, and under its auspices a group of international lawyers produced the Tallinn Manual, an attempt to state how existing international law applies to cyber operations (Schmitt 2013). The episode was widely described at the time as the first cyber war. With hindsight it looks more like the first clear case of what Valeriano and Maness would call restraint: a rival state’s displeasure expressed through disruption that stopped well short of destruction.

8.3 The restraint thesis

Valeriano and Maness built their dataset around a simple design choice that shapes everything they found. They did not try to catalogue every cyber operation in the world. They took pairs of states with a history of enduring hostility — rivals, in the sense the international-conflict literature has used for decades — and recorded the cyber incidents between them. The unit is the incident, nested in campaigns; the population is rival dyads; the evidence is public. The dataset has since been extended as the Dyadic Cyber Incident and Campaign Data (DCID), whose version 2.0 covers incidents beginning between 2000 and 2020 (Maness et al. 2022).

Cyber incident (in DCID) — an individual cyber operation launched by one state against another within a rival dyad, with a start date, a method, a target type, an objective and a severity score. Incidents can be grouped into campaigns, sustained series of operations with a common purpose. The unit is the incident between rivals, not the operation in general.

The choice of rivals as the population is a theoretical commitment, not a convenience. The study of international conflict has long found that a small number of pairs of states account for a large share of militarised disputes, and that these enduring rivalries have their own dynamics: hostility persists, crises recur, and each side interprets the other’s moves in the light of a long history. If cyber operations are a new instrument of old conflicts, rivalries are where they should appear; if they are something genuinely new — an instrument that lets any state hurt any other cheaply — they should appear outside rivalries too. Restricting the data to rivals makes the first hypothesis testable and the second invisible — a limitation to which this chapter returns when the rival-dyad record is set beside a wider one.

From that record they drew four propositions.

The first is restraint. States use cyber operations far less often, and far less destructively, than the “cyber war” narrative predicts. Most incidents in the data are of low severity: defacements of websites, denial-of-service attacks, espionage. Operations that destroy physical property or take lives are rare. The image of cyberspace as a domain of constant, escalating attack does not survive contact with a systematic count.

Cyber restraint — the empirical pattern, and the theoretical argument, that states with offensive cyber capabilities use them sparingly and at low severity against their rivals, holding back the most destructive options. Restraint is a claim about strategic severity, not about frequency of nuisance or about commercial cost.

The second is that cyber conflict follows regional rivalries. The dyads that account for most of the incidents are those that were already hostile before anyone had a network: the United States and China, Russia and its neighbours and the West, Israel and Iran, India and Pakistan, the two Koreas. Cyber operations do not create new conflicts; they are a tool in old ones. That finding matters for anyone who expected the low cost of entry to cyber operations to flatten the international hierarchy. The states that are most active in cyberspace are, by and large, the states that were already contending.

The third proposition explains the first: why states hold back. The fourth is that cyber operations have limited coercive effect. They rarely change the target’s behaviour. They are better at signalling displeasure and at gathering intelligence than at compelling a rival to do something it would otherwise not do — a conclusion that the same authors developed at greater length in later work on cyber strategy (Valeriano, Jensen, and Maness 2018).

The restraint thesis did not arrive in an empty field. Thomas Rid had argued, provocatively, that cyber war in the sense of an act of force that is violent, instrumental and political had never occurred, and that what was called cyber war was better understood as sabotage, espionage and subversion — old activities in a new medium (Rid 2012, 2013). Erik Gartzke made a related argument from the logic of force: an attack that cannot be followed up by conventional power changes nothing durable, so cyber operations are likely to be adjuncts to force rather than substitutes for it (Gartzke 2013). Lucas Kello took the opposite position, arguing that the cyber revolution genuinely strained the concepts of international relations because it blurred war and peace into a condition of permanent, sub-threshold “unpeace” (Kello 2013, 2017). Valeriano and Maness’s contribution was to bring data to an argument that had been conducted largely through concepts and anecdotes, and the data came down closer to Rid and Gartzke than to the prophets of catastrophe.

8.4 Why states hold back

The reasons for restraint are worth taking one at a time, because each has an analogue in the firm’s own calculation.

Escalation. A destructive cyber operation against a rival invites a response, and the response need not stay in cyberspace. A state that switches off a rival’s grid has made a move whose consequences it cannot control. In a relationship where both sides have conventional and sometimes nuclear forces, the incentive to stay below the threshold that would provoke them is strong.

Revelation. A cyber capability depends on a vulnerability the target has not fixed. Using it reveals it, and once revealed it can be patched — in the target’s systems and everyone else’s. The most sophisticated capabilities are therefore wasting assets, worth more held in reserve than spent.

Blowback. Malicious code, once released, can be captured, analysed and reused. A weapon that can be reverse-engineered and turned back on its maker, or on the maker’s allies and firms, is a weapon to be used with care.

Access. The value of being inside a rival’s networks is often greater than the value of damaging them. A state that can read a rival’s communications has every reason not to do anything that would alert the rival to its presence. Espionage, in this sense, restrains disruption.

These incentives are not the whole story. Ben Buchanan has argued that intrusions made for defensive purposes — to understand a rival’s capabilities, or to pre-position against a future threat — look identical, from the target’s side, to preparation for attack, and that this creates a security dilemma in which each side’s prudence alarms the other (Buchanan 2017). Restraint in the use of destructive operations is compatible with constant, mutual intrusion, and the intrusions themselves are a source of instability.

The war in Ukraine has tested the restraint thesis harder than anything in the dataset, which ends in 2020. Russian cyber operations against Ukraine have been extensive since 2014, and they intensified around the full-scale invasion of February 2022. The best systematic study of the earlier period found that cyber operations had little discernible effect on events on the battlefield (Kostyuk and Zhukov 2019), which is consistent with the fourth proposition: cyber operations are weak instruments of compulsion even in war. Whether the restraint thesis holds as of today is an open question. A pattern measured to 2020 is evidence about 2000–2020, and the war has supplied episodes that sit awkwardly within it.

One of them began in the first hours of the invasion. On 24 February 2022 an operation against the KA-SAT satellite network operated by the American company Viasat disabled large numbers of the modems its customers used in Ukraine and elsewhere in Europe. The apparent target was Ukrainian military and government communications. Among the collateral casualties was the remote monitoring and control of wind farms in central Europe, whose operators used the same service. In May 2022 the European Union, the United Kingdom and the United States attributed the operation to Russia (UK Government 2022). The episode is consistent with restraint in the narrow sense — it was an adjunct to a conventional invasion, not a substitute for one — and it is a warning about everything the restraint thesis does not cover. A commercial satellite network was a target because the Ukrainian state relied on it, and the firms that suffered had no connection to the war except that they were customers of the same hub.

The most famous exception proves the rule in an instructive way. The operation later known as Stuxnet, discovered in 2010, sabotaged centrifuges at Iran’s uranium-enrichment plant at Natanz by manipulating the industrial control systems that ran them. It was widely attributed to the United States and Israel, neither of which has formally acknowledged it. It is the clearest case in the public record of code causing physical destruction to a rival’s strategic asset, and it fits the restraint thesis better than the catastrophe narrative: it was narrowly targeted, designed to look like malfunction rather than attack, and aimed at delaying a programme rather than at provoking a war. It also illustrates blowback. The code escaped the plant’s isolated network, spread to computers around the world, and was analysed in detail by security researchers, so that its techniques became available to everyone. For firms, the lesson was that industrial control systems — the machinery of plants, pipelines and utilities — were now a target class, and that the code designed to attack one of them could end up on their own networks.

Low strategic severity is not low commercial cost. An operation that barely registers on a scale of harm between states — a wiper that destroys no infrastructure and kills no one — can halt a company’s production for weeks. The restraint thesis is about what states do to each other. The firm’s question is what happens to the firms that happen to be in the way.

8.5 Weaponised interdependence

Valeriano and Maness study offensive operations. Farrell and Newman study something quieter and, for a multinational, probably more consequential: the way in which the networks of the global economy can be used by the states that sit at their centre (Farrell and Newman 2019).

Their starting observation is that the networks globalisation built — for finance, for communications, for the internet’s physical and logical infrastructure — are not flat meshes in which every node is equal. They are highly asymmetric, with a few hubs through which most traffic passes. Network economics explains why: hubs are efficient, and efficiency attracts more traffic, which makes the hub more central still. The internationalisation of the 1990s was conducted on the assumption that this centralisation was a technical fact with no political content. Farrell and Newman’s argument is that the assumption was wrong. A state that has jurisdiction over a hub can use it.

Panopticon effect — the use of jurisdiction over a network hub to see what flows through it: to gather information about the activities of other states, firms and individuals whose traffic passes through the hub.

Chokepoint effect — the use of jurisdiction over a network hub to deny others access to it: to cut a bank out of a payment system, a firm out of a software supply, a country out of a market for critical components.

The two effects need each other. The panopticon tells a state where to apply the chokepoint, and the chokepoint gives the panopticon its teeth. Farrell and Newman’s cases make both concrete. The disclosures by Edward Snowden in 2013 showed the extent to which American intelligence agencies had used the concentration of global internet traffic and data storage in American firms and on American territory to collect information at scale — the panopticon. The disconnection of sanctioned Iranian banks from the SWIFT financial-messaging network in 2012, after the European Union adopted the necessary legal measures, showed how a hub located in one jurisdiction could be used to deny a whole country’s banking system its normal connection to the world — the chokepoint.

The implication for a multinational is uncomfortable in two ways. The first is that the firm’s exposure to geopolitical risk depends on where the hubs it uses are located and whose law applies to them. A firm whose payments clear through one jurisdiction, whose data are stored with providers subject to another, and whose software updates arrive from a third has three sets of exposure to the coercive choices of three states, most of which have nothing to do with the firm.

The second is more uncomfortable still: the firm itself can be the hub. A cloud provider, a payment network, a telecommunications operator, a maker of widely used software — each is infrastructure that its home state can watch through or weaponise, and that rival states therefore have reason to treat as a target, to exclude, or to replace with a domestic alternative. A firm in that position is not a bystander to geopolitical competition. It is part of the terrain. This is the bridge to sanctions in Chapter 10, where the chokepoint becomes a legal instrument, and to space in Chapter 11, where the hubs are in orbit.

8.6 Attribution: evidence about evidence

Every dataset of state cyber operations rests on an act of attribution. Somebody has to say who did it. And attribution in cyberspace has four properties that make it unlike the identification of the attacker in almost any other kind of conflict event.

Attribution — the identification of the actor responsible for a cyber operation, at three levels: the machines used, the individuals or groups who operated them, and the state, if any, that directed or sponsored them. Public attribution is the decision to announce the third.

It is slow. Forensic analysis takes weeks or months; public attribution by governments often comes a year or more after the event. It is probabilistic. Technical indicators — the code, the infrastructure, the working hours of the operators, the language settings left in a file — can be faked, borrowed or reused, and a careful operator plants false ones. It is political. Governments decide when to attribute publicly, to whom, and with how much of their evidence, and they decide in the light of their relations with the accused state and their wish to protect their own sources. And it is selective. What is attributed publicly is what someone wanted known.

Rid and Buchanan put the point sharply: attribution is what states make of it (Rid and Buchanan 2015). It is not a technical fact waiting to be discovered but a judgement, assembled from technical, operational and strategic evidence, and communicated for a purpose. Lindsay offered a partial corrective: for the most consequential operations the set of capable actors is small, and the attribution problem is therefore less severe than it is for routine intrusions — which is one reason deterrence of major attacks remains possible (Lindsay 2015). Both views lead to the same conclusion about data.

An event dataset of cyber operations is a record of public attributions, not a record of operations. That sentence is the most important caveat attached to every count in this chapter. It has predictable consequences for what the data show. States that attribute publicly — mostly Western democracies, with active security services and a free press — appear more often as victims than states that do not. Operations by allies against each other are rarely attributed in public and are therefore nearly invisible. Operations that succeeded in remaining undetected are, by definition, absent. A dyad with no incidents in the data may be a dyad with no operations, or a dyad in which neither side chose to say anything.

8.7 When the firm is collateral

The restraint thesis concerns the intentions of states. The cost to firms is driven by something else: the technical reach of what states release.

From the archive · NotPetya, June 2017

On 27 June 2017, the eve of a Ukrainian public holiday, malware spread through the update mechanism of a Ukrainian accounting program widely used by businesses operating in the country. It looked like ransomware, displaying a demand for payment, but its encryption could not be reversed: it was a wiper dressed as a ransom note. Within hours it had spread beyond Ukraine through the internal networks of any company that ran the software at a Ukrainian site. In February 2018 the governments of the United States and the United Kingdom, among others, publicly attributed the operation to the Russian military.

The apparent target was Ukraine. The victims included some of the largest firms in the world. The shipping company Maersk lost the use of systems across its global operations. The pharmaceutical company Merck and the food company Mondelez reported very large losses, as did logistics and manufacturing firms in many countries. None of them had a quarrel with anyone; they had a subsidiary, an office or a supplier in Ukraine, running a program everybody used (Greenberg 2019).

The aftermath carried a second lesson. When the companies claimed under their property insurance, insurers invoked the war exclusions that such policies had carried for generations, arguing that an operation by a state’s military was an act of war. The litigation lasted years and did not go uniformly the insurers’ way; in Merck’s case, New Jersey courts held that the traditional exclusion, written with armed conflict in mind, did not cover the attack. The market’s response was to write explicit exclusions for state-backed cyber operations into its contracts, which the Lloyd’s market required from 2023. The effect is that a firm’s cyber cover may now end exactly where geopolitics begins.

Three lessons follow for the firm. The first is that proximity in cyberspace is technical, not political. Firms were hit by NotPetya because they ran a piece of software in a country at war, not because anyone chose them. A firm’s exposure to a state’s cyber operations depends on its network architecture — what it connects to, what it updates from, what its subsidiaries can reach — as much as on its political profile.

The second is that insurance is not a backstop. A risk transfer that excludes the scenario the board is most worried about does not transfer that risk. The question to ask of a cyber policy is not whether it covers “cyber attacks” but what it says about operations attributed to states — and who decides the attribution.

The third is that the board owns it. Securities regulators increasingly require listed firms to disclose material cyber incidents and to describe how their boards oversee cyber risk. In the United States, rules adopted in 2023 require listed companies to report material incidents promptly and to describe their risk management and governance of cybersecurity in their annual reports (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission 2023). The geopolitical part of cyber risk — the part that arises from the rivalry of states — is thereby made a matter of corporate governance, whatever the firm’s own view of it.

8.8 Digital sovereignty and the splintering network

Weaponised interdependence has a defensive mirror. States that fear the panopticon and the chokepoint of others respond by asserting control over the networks within their own jurisdiction: where data must be stored, who may access them, which foreign equipment may be installed, which vendors may operate. The vocabulary varies — digital sovereignty, data localisation, trusted vendors, technological self-reliance — and the effect is the same. The single global network on which the internationalisation of the 1990s assumed firms could operate is splintering into jurisdictions with different rules.

Three instruments matter most for a multinational. Data-localisation and transfer rules require that certain data be stored within a country or restrict their movement abroad; the invalidation by the Court of Justice of the European Union in 2020 of the framework that governed transfers of personal data from the European Union to the United States showed how quickly a legal basis for a firm’s data architecture can disappear. Security reviews and vendor bans exclude foreign suppliers from critical networks; the restrictions imposed on Huawei’s telecommunications equipment by the United States and a number of its allies from 2019 onward forced operators to redesign and in some cases to strip out networks they had already built. Access laws give governments the power to compel firms under their jurisdiction to hand over data, including data stored abroad; the United States’ CLOUD Act of 2018 is the best-known example, and it sits awkwardly beside European data-protection law.

For the firm, these instruments generate three kinds of cost. Duplication: separate data centres or cloud regions per jurisdiction, separate supply chains for network equipment, separate compliance regimes. Conflicting obligations: one state’s law requires a disclosure that another’s forbids, and the firm must choose which to break. Vendor risk: a supplier banned in one market may be mandatory, or at least dominant, in another, so that a single global architecture becomes impossible. None of these costs arises from a cyber operation. All of them arise from the geopolitics of cyberspace.

8.9 Where cyber risk sits in the firm

The five channels through which geopolitical risk reaches a multinational help locate cyber risk precisely. It runs most obviously through data and operations: the operation that halts production, the localisation rule that forces a redesign. It runs through assets and people, when intellectual property is stolen or staff in a jurisdiction are compelled to provide access. It runs through finance, when insurers exclude state-backed operations and when payment hubs become chokepoints. It runs through inputs, when a vendor is banned or a software supply chain is compromised — NotPetya travelled through a software update. And it runs through market access, when a firm is excluded from a market as a security risk, or when its home state’s use of the firm as a hub makes it unwelcome abroad.

Inside the firm, the risk is distributed across several actors, and the distribution is part of the problem. The board sets risk appetite and oversees disclosure; its distinctive question is which state actors would want our data or our downtime, and why? The chief information security officer turns threat intelligence into controls and owns incident response, but typically reports through a technology function with limited geopolitical analysis. The chief risk officer is the natural integrator of cyber risk with country, sanctions and supply-chain risk — the view this book takes. The insurer prices the risk and, increasingly, draws the line at state-backed operations. And regulators impose disclosure, localisation and vendor rules from outside. A firm in which each of these actors holds a piece of the picture and nobody holds the whole has a decision architecture designed for crime and espionage, not for geopolitics.

What should change for a firm with a plant in a country that sits in one of the densest cells of the rivalry matrix? Not, in the first instance, its technology. The first change is to the map: an inventory of which systems at that site connect to which systems elsewhere, which software is updated from which vendors, and which data cross which borders — because it is along those connections that an operation aimed at a government travels into a firm. The second is to segmentation: a subsidiary in a contested jurisdiction whose network can reach the group’s core systems is a bridge for anyone who compromises it, and the cheapest geopolitical control a firm can buy is often a wall between the two. The third is to contracts: what the insurance policy says about operations attributed to states, what the cloud provider’s terms say about government access in each jurisdiction, and what suppliers have agreed about notifying the firm when they are compromised. The fourth is to governance: who in the firm asks, before an investment rather than after an incident, which states would want the firm’s data or its downtime.

8.10 What the record shows, and how it was made

The descriptive picture at the heart of Valeriano and Maness’s book — incidents by year, by method and by severity, and the matrix of who targets whom — can be rebuilt from the public release of DCID, and the codebook repays reading because it is where the concepts of this chapter become columns.

The unit is one incident between an initiating state and a target state within a rival dyad. The dyad’s two states are recorded in the order of their Correlates of War codes, so that the first state of the pair is not necessarily the initiator; the initiator is recorded separately, and the target is whichever state of the dyad did not initiate. The method is coded on a scale that runs from vandalism through denial of service and network intrusion to network infiltration, with sub-codes for kinds of malicious software. Severity is coded on a scale the codebook defines from 0 to 10, of which the released file uses only the values 0 to 6 — a discrepancy that, if the codebook’s scale applies, is itself consistent with restraint: in two decades of rivalry, nothing in the record reaches the upper part of the scale. The type of target distinguishes private and non-state targets from governmental ones, civilian and military, and the objective distinguishes disruption, short- and long-term espionage and degradation. The September 2022 release contains 433 incidents with start dates from September 2000 to January 2021.

Two properties of the data follow directly from the arguments above. The first is that the population is rival dyads. A cell of the initiator × target matrix that is empty does not mean “no cyber conflict”; it means “no rivalry in the data”, because pairs of states that are not coded as rivals never enter. The second is that the incidents are public attributions. The matrix is therefore a picture of who has been publicly accused of operating against whom — itself a geopolitical artefact, shaped by which governments attribute and when.

A second dataset, the European Repository of Cyber Incidents, makes both properties visible (Zettl-Schabath et al. 2025). It records incidents with state and non-state initiators, including criminal, hacktivist and unattributed operations, and in its version 1.3 it contains 3,414 incidents with start dates from 2000 to 2024 — several times the size of DCID. Restricting it to incidents attributed to state or state-affiliated actors leaves 1,014; restricting it to states alone leaves 329. The gap between those numbers, and between all of them and DCID’s 433, is a measure of how much the answer to “how much cyber conflict is there?” depends on the definition of the population and of attribution. The two datasets do not contradict each other. They answer different questions: how rival states use cyber operations against each other, and how much publicly reported cyber activity of any origin there has been. A firm that reads the first as if it answered the second will underestimate its exposure several times over; one that reads the second as if it measured state strategy will see a war where there is mostly crime.

8.11 A short review of the literature

The debate over whether cyberspace is a domain of war was framed in the early 2010s. Rid (2012) and Rid (2013) argued that cyber war had not taken place and would not, reclassifying cyber operations as sabotage, espionage and subversion; Gartzke (2013) argued from the logic of force that cyber operations would complement rather than replace conventional power; Kello (2013) and Kello (2017) argued, against both, that the cyber revolution unsettled the conceptual foundations of international relations. The legal question of how existing international law applies to cyber operations is set out in Schmitt (2013).

Systematic evidence arrived with Valeriano and Maness (2015), whose dataset of incidents between rival states supported a thesis of restraint and regional rivalry. The dataset has been extended through successive releases, of which Maness et al. (2022) is the current public version; Maness and Valeriano (2016) examine whether cyber incidents shift the broader conflict and cooperation between rivals, and Valeriano, Jensen, and Maness (2018) develop the argument that cyber operations are weak instruments of coercion. Kostyuk and Zhukov (2019) tested cyber operations in the war in eastern Ukraine against battlefield events and found little effect. Buchanan (2017) sets out the cybersecurity dilemma created by intrusions that are defensive in purpose and threatening in appearance.

On attribution, Rid and Buchanan (2015) treat it as a political judgement assembled from technical and strategic evidence, and Lindsay (2015) argues that its difficulty is overstated for consequential operations. On the economic architecture of coercion, Farrell and Newman (2019) is the foundational statement of weaponised interdependence, with its panopticon and chokepoint effects. Wider datasets that include non-state actors, such as Zettl-Schabath et al. (2025), now allow the rival-dyad picture to be set against the full record of attributed incidents, and the firm’s experience of state operations is documented most vividly in Greenberg (2019).

8.12 What the evidence does not license

The record of cyber conflict between rivals is informative. It shows that incidents concentrate in a few dyads, that most are of low severity, and that the methods have shifted over two decades. It is tempting to read the matrix of initiators and targets as a map of where a multinational’s cyber risk lies. It is not, and the reasons are the arguments of this chapter.

The matrix does not license a firm to conclude that it is safe in a country whose cells are empty. An empty cell means no publicly attributed incident between rivals; it says nothing about operations that were never attributed, about operations between states that are not coded as rivals, or about criminal groups, which account for much of the cyber harm firms actually suffer. Nor does a dense cell tell a firm that it is a target. The incidents in that cell were aimed mostly at governments, and the firm’s exposure to them depends on whether it is technically in the path — whether it runs software, holds data or provides infrastructure that an operation against the government might traverse. NotPetya would have appeared in the data as one incident in one dyad; its cost fell on firms in dozens of countries. The attack on the satellite network in 2022 would appear, if at all, as an incident between Russia and Ukraine; its casualties included wind farms in Germany.

Nor does the evidence for restraint license complacency. It is a pattern in state behaviour to 2020, measured on a scale of harm between states. It does not bound the commercial cost of a low-severity operation, and it does not tell a firm what has happened since a large-scale war began in Europe. Restraint describes what states have chosen not to do to each other. It is silent about what they have done to the networks that happen to connect them, and about the coercion that weaponised interdependence allows without a line of malicious code being written.

What the evidence does support is a reordering of the firm’s questions. The first is not “are we a target?” — for most firms the honest answer is “probably not, deliberately” — but “what are we connected to?”: which of the firm’s networks, suppliers, software vendors and hubs lie in the path of the rivalries the record shows to be dense. The second is “whose jurisdiction are we in?”: which states can see through, or cut off, the services on which the firm depends. The third is “who pays?”: what the firm has agreed with its insurer about the operations that a government will one day attribute to a state. A firm that can answer those three questions has turned cyber risk from a line in the information-technology budget into what the evidence says it is — a geopolitical exposure, carried mostly by firms that nobody meant to hit.
