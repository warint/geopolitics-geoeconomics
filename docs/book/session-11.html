11.2 The lecture

The session opens with four Risk Briefs (20 min). The lecture follows.

11.2.1 1. Infrastructure in orbit

Few multinationals own a satellite. Almost all of them depend on one. Three services carry most of that dependence:

Service What the firm uses it for Who provides it
Positioning, navigation and timing (PNT) fleet and container tracking, aviation and shipping, precision agriculture — and above all time: telecom networks, power grids and trading venues synchronise their clocks to GNSS signals four global systems, each run by a state: GPS (United States), Galileo (European Union), BeiDou (China), GLONASS (Russia)
Connectivity ships, aircraft, mines, rigs and remote sites; back-up links for networks on the ground geostationary operators, and since 2019 constellations of thousands of satellites in low Earth orbit
Earth observation crop and commodity forecasts, insurance claims, supply-chain and site monitoring, due diligence state agencies and a growing number of commercial imaging firms

Sessions 08 and 10 showed how networks become instruments of power. Today’s network is physically above the firm, and its hubs are a handful of states and, increasingly, a handful of companies. The scale of the change is recent: ESA’s 2026 report notes that the exponential growth in the number of new payloads continued in 2025, driven by a higher launch frequency, rideshare launches and the deployment of large constellations by commercial operators (ESA Space Debris Office, 2026).

11.2.2 2. The orbital commons: law and physics

The law. The Outer Space Treaty (1967) is the constitution of the domain, and it was written for two superpowers, not for a market:

Article Rule What it means for a firm
II outer space “is not subject to national appropriation” by claim of sovereignty, use or occupation no one can own an orbit — but whoever occupies it first, in practice, keeps it
IV no nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit; the Moon and celestial bodies for peaceful purposes only conventional weapons in orbit and anti-satellite tests are not prohibited by the Treaty
VI states bear international responsibility for national activities, “whether … carried on by governmental agencies or by non-governmental entities”, which require “authorization and continuing supervision” every commercial satellite has a state behind it — its licensing state
VII the launching state is liable for damage caused by its objects debris has an owner, in law, long after it has stopped working
IX “due regard” to the interests of others; consultations before potentially harmful interference the closest the Treaty comes to traffic rules — without an enforcer

Two further layers matter to business. The International Telecommunication Union coordinates radio frequencies and orbital positions through its Radio Regulations: an operator’s state files a satellite network with the ITU and coordinates it with earlier filings, so priority goes, broadly, to the first to file — an incentive to file early and large, sometimes for “paper satellites” that may never fly. And national licensing — of launches, spectrum, remote sensing, exports — is where the Treaty’s “authorization and continuing supervision” becomes a permit a firm must hold.

The physics. Kessler and Cour-Palais (1978) showed that once the density of objects in an orbital shell passes a threshold, collisions produce fragments that cause further collisions: a debris belt that grows on its own, even if no one launches again. A commons in the strict sense — no one can be excluded, and every use depletes it for others — with physics in place of an economy. Debris in low orbit re-enters as the atmosphere drags it down; the higher the orbit, the longer it stays — years at a few hundred kilometres, decades to centuries above that.

For the firm: an orbit is a shared infrastructure whose quality is set by the least careful actor in it. A destructive test by one state raises the collision risk — and the insurance price — of every operator in that shell.

11.2.3 3. Astropolitics: three readings of the new space race

Reading Implication
Dolman (2002), Astropolitik classical geopolitics carried into space: in the spirit of Mackinder, whoever controls low Earth orbit controls access to near-Earth space, and through it the Earth; a realist case for the United States to take and hold that control space is a territory to dominate — Session 03’s anarchy, with orbits as chokepoints
Pekkanen (2019), “Governing the new space race” the new space race is driven by three intertwined trends — democratisation (more states and non-state actors), commercialisation (firms striking out on their own, not only supplying governments), militarisation — while about 95% of space technologies are dual-use, and treaty-making is in decline space is a market governed by national laws, contracts and a few old principles — and the firms in it are part of the security problem
Bowen (2020), War in Space spacepower matters for its effects on Earth; Earth orbit is a “cosmic coastline” from which states support, and threaten, what happens on land, at sea and in the air space is infrastructure for terrestrial power — and therefore a target when terrestrial power is contested

The three readings do not exclude one another. Read together they explain why the same constellation can be a consumer broadband business, a military communications network and a foreign-policy instrument, all at once — Pekkanen’s dual use, with the strategic stakes of Dolman and Bowen.

11.2.4 4. Counterspace: the threats

The Secure World Foundation’s annual open-source assessment (Samson & Brett, 2026) tracks what states can do to deceive, disrupt, deny, degrade or destroy space systems, across five categories, in thirteen countries:

Category What it is Reversible? Who it hits first
Direct-ascent a missile launched from Earth to destroy a satellite no — and it creates debris the target, then every operator in that shell
Co-orbital a satellite that approaches, inspects, interferes with or attacks another depends the target
Electronic warfare jamming or spoofing of signals — uplinks, downlinks, GNSS yes users on the ground: ships, aircraft, networks
Directed energy lasers or microwaves to dazzle or damage sensors partly imaging satellites
Cyber attacks on satellites, ground stations or user terminals yes, unless hardware is destroyed customers of the operator

Its central finding for this course: many states have researched and tested destructive capabilities, but only non-destructive capabilities are actively used against satellites in current military operations. Four states have destroyed a satellite with a missile from the ground: the United States (1985, and the low-altitude intercept of a failing satellite in 2008), China (2007), India (2019) and Russia (2021). In April 2022 the United States declared it would not conduct destructive direct-ascent tests, and the UN General Assembly called on all states to make the same commitment in December 2022.

What firms have actually suffered is the reversible kind. On 24 February 2022, the day of the invasion of Ukraine, a cyberattack on the ground segment of Viasat’s KA-SAT network disabled thousands of user modems in Ukraine and across Europe — including the remote monitoring of thousands of wind turbines in Germany; the EU, the UK and the US attributed it to Russia. GNSS jamming and spoofing around conflict zones has become routine; in 2024 Finnair suspended flights to Tartu, in Estonia, for several weeks because interference made satellite-based approaches unusable.

The lesson for an analyst. Destructive tests make headlines and debris. Jamming, spoofing and cyberattacks on ground segments make losses — and they fall on the customers, not on the satellite.

11.2.5 5. The evidence: the debris record

ESA’s Space Debris Office has published its Space Environment Report every year since 2017. The 2026 edition (Issue 10.0, data to the end of 2025) is today’s big work. Its method is worth reading before its figures: it counts catalogued objects — from the US public catalogue, the Russian Vimpel catalogue and the UK’s historical RAE tables — and asserted objects known to exist by design, cross-correlated in ESA’s DISCOS database; objects too small or too erratic to be tracked are outside its scope.

Its headline numbers, at the end of 2025 (Table 3.1):

  • 44,964 objects in Earth orbit, of which 16,946 payloads; 25,060 in low Earth orbit;
  • “ever since the start of the space age there has been more space debris in orbit than operational satellites”;
  • fragmentations other than collisions — explosions, mostly of old rocket stages and batteries — are the dominant source of debris: on average 9.8 non-deliberate fragmentations a year over the last two decades; 2024 added over 3,000 catalogued fragments, mostly from propulsion-related break-ups.

The target figure — Figure 2.1, the number of objects in geocentric orbit by object class since 1957 — shows the cumulative result. Three events stand out, all recorded in the report:

Date Event Altitude What it did
11 January 2007 China destroys its Fengyun-1C weather satellite with a ground-launched missile about 850 km the largest single source of catalogued debris in the public catalogue; thousands of fragments, most still in orbit
10 February 2009 the active Iridium 33 and the defunct Cosmos 2251 collide about 780 km the first accidental collision between two intact satellites — Kessler, observed
15 November 2021 Russia destroys Cosmos 1408 with a ground-launched missile about 470–490 km some 1,800 catalogued fragments; low enough that almost all re-entered within three years — ESA attributes the 2022 spike in re-entering debris to the test

What the figure does not measure. It counts objects, not risk: a 10-cm fragment and a 5-tonne rocket stage each count once, and objects too small to catalogue — the majority of the fragments able to disable a satellite — do not appear at all. And it is drawn from catalogues that themselves improve: when sensors get better, the number of objects rises without anything new happening in orbit. In the practice round, you will find a third limit — the one hidden in how the public catalogue dates debris.

11.2.6 6. One operator: concentration, and weaponised interdependence in orbit

The new space race has a feature the Cold War one did not: most of the active satellites in orbit belong to one company. SpaceX’s Starlink, first launched in May 2019, grew in six years into the majority of the world’s operating satellites; you will measure how large a majority in the practice round, and you will find that concentration of who launches payloads has returned to Cold War levels — with a firm, not a superpower, at the top.

Starlink in Ukraine shows what that means. Within days of the 2022 invasion, Starlink terminals became a backbone of Ukraine’s military and civil communications. The company’s own decisions about where the service would work — including, by its owner’s account, a refusal to enable it for an attack near Crimea in 2022 — became questions of war policy, and governments moved to contract for the service directly.

Read it with Farrell and Newman (2019) from Session 08. A constellation is a hub: its operator sees who connects, where (a panopticon), and can switch a user, a region or a country off (a chokepoint). The two conditions for weaponisation are met when the network is concentrated and when a state can direct the firm that runs the hub — and Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty guarantees that every operator has a licensing state behind it. A firm that depends on one operator depends on that operator’s state as well.

States are responding as you would expect from Session 03: by building their own. The EU’s IRIS² secure-connectivity programme (Regulation (EU) 2023/588), China’s Qianfan and Guowang constellations, and national launch programmes are all, in part, answers to dependence on one American firm. Export controls draw the same line from the other side: spacecraft and their components sit on the US Munitions List (Category XV of the ITAR) or, for most commercial satellites since 2014, under the Export Administration Regulations (the “9x515” entries) — so a non-US manufacturer with a US component needs US permission to sell to some customers.

11.2.7 7. What can the firm do?

Through which channel does space risk reach the P&L? The course’s five channels apply:

Channel How space risk arrives Example
Market access export controls and licensing on space technology; spectrum and landing rights a satellite or terminal that cannot be sold or switched on in a market
Inputs a service from a single operator or a single state’s GNSS connectivity for ships or mines from one constellation
Finance and payments trading and payment systems time-stamped from GNSS; insurance capacity a timing outage; a premium that doubles after a fragmentation event
Assets and people ships and aircraft navigating in jammed or spoofed areas rerouted flights; vessels misreporting their position
Data and operations cyberattacks on ground segments and terminals; loss of imagery feeds the KA-SAT modems; a crop forecast without images

What a dashboard should track — each with a threshold that triggers a named decision:

  1. Dependence — the share of a critical service (connectivity, imagery, timing) supplied by one operator or one state’s system; e.g. above 50% from one operator → contract a second one.
  2. Interference — official warnings of GNSS interference (aviation authorities publish them) in the regions where the firm’s ships, aircraft or sites operate.
  3. Environment — destructive tests and fragmentation events in the orbital shells of the firm’s providers; ESA’s yearly environment indicators.
  4. Rules — changes in export-control lists, licensing conditions and ITU filings that affect the firm’s suppliers or markets.

Strategies, each with a cost and a limit:

Strategy Example Cost Limit
Multi-orbit, multi-operator geostationary + low-orbit links from two providers price, integration the second operator may share the first one’s state
Multi-constellation receivers GPS + Galileo (+ others) in the same receiver small one jammer can still blind them all locally
Terrestrial back-up holdover clocks and fibre-delivered time; cellular and fibre links capital not available at sea or in remote sites
Insurance in-orbit and third-party liability cover premium a thin market; capacity shrinks after large losses
Compliance by design export-control classification of every space component bought or sold staff, delays rules change with the balance of power

11.2.8 8. The Tableau skill of the day

  • A stock from flows. Pivot LAUNCH_DATE and DECAY_DATE into one Event date column; a calculated field Change = +1 for a launch, −1 for a decay; SUM([Change]) by year with a Running Total table calculation is the number of objects in orbit.
  • HHI with LOD expressions. Share of each owner in a year’s payloads: {FIXED [Launch year], [OWNER] : COUNT([NORAD_CAT_ID])} / {FIXED [Launch year] : COUNT([NORAD_CAT_ID])}; the sum of squared shares, built so that it adds up correctly over rows, is the HHI.
  • Dashboard action across data sources: Dashboard → Actions → Add Action → Filter, mapping Year in one source to Launch year in the other.

11.3.2 2 · Prepare — 15 min

You build two data sources from the same file: one reshaped into events (for the stock of objects in orbit), one left as it is (for concentration).

11.3.2.1 Data source A · “SATCAT events”

  1. Connect → Text file → satcat.csv. Check the types: LAUNCH_DATE and DECAY_DATE must be dates (click the type icon above the column if they arrive as strings), NORAD_CAT_ID a number.

  2. Data source filter (top right of the data source page → Filters → Add): ORBIT_CENTER = EA. Objects around the Sun or the Moon never decay and would stay “in orbit” for ever.

  3. Pivot the two dates. Select LAUNCH_DATE and DECAY_DATE in the grid → right-click → Pivot. Rename Pivot Field Names → Event and Pivot Field Values → Event date. Each object now has two rows: its launch and its decay (empty if it is still in space).

  4. Calculated fields:

    Change  =  IF [Event] = "LAUNCH_DATE" THEN 1 ELSE -1 END
Year    =  YEAR([Event date])

  5. Check the grain. Note the row count before and after the pivot, then exclude the null Event date rows (decays that have not happened). What is one row now? Write it in the log.

11.3.2.2 Data source B · “SATCAT objects”

  1. Data → New Data Source → the same satcat.csv, not pivoted. Data source filter OBJECT_TYPE = PAY — a data source filter, not a sheet filter (step 3 explains why).

  2. Calculated fields:

    Launch year  =  YEAR([LAUNCH_DATE])
Power        =  CASE [OWNER] WHEN "US" THEN "United States"
                             WHEN "PRC" THEN "China"
                             WHEN "CIS" THEN "USSR / Russia"
                             ELSE "Rest of the world" END
Active       =  [OPS_STATUS_CODE] = "+" OR [OPS_STATUS_CODE] = "P" OR [OPS_STATUS_CODE] = "B"
                OR [OPS_STATUS_CODE] = "S" OR [OPS_STATUS_CODE] = "X"
Operator     =  IF STARTSWITH([OBJECT_NAME], "STARLINK") THEN "Starlink (SpaceX)"
                ELSEIF STARTSWITH([OBJECT_NAME], "ONEWEB") THEN "OneWeb"
                ELSEIF STARTSWITH([OBJECT_NAME], "KUIPER") THEN "Kuiper (Amazon)"
                ELSEIF STARTSWITH([OBJECT_NAME], "QIANFAN") THEN "Qianfan"
                ELSEIF STARTSWITH([OBJECT_NAME], "HULIANWANG") THEN "Guowang"
                ELSE "Other: " + [OWNER] END

    CIS is CelesTrak’s code for the USSR and Russia; OWNER is a registry-style code, not the operator — log both decisions.

11.3.3 3 · Replicate — 35 min

11.3.3.1 Sheet 1 · “Objects in Earth orbit, by type” — the target figure (data source A)

  1. Year → Columns: right-click → Convert to Dimension, keep it continuous (green).
  2. SUM([Change]) → Rows. You see net additions per year — a jagged line.
  3. Right-click the pill → Quick Table Calculation → Running Total. Now you see the stock.
  4. OBJECT_TYPE → Colour. Edit Table Calculation → Compute using → Specific Dimensions → tick Year only, so the running total restarts for each type. Mark type Area (stacked).
  5. Filter Year ≤ 2025 to match ESA’s end point. Never filter out early years with a normal filter: it runs before the table calculation and the running total would restart from zero. (To hide early years, use a table-calculation filter such as LOOKUP(MIN([Year]), 0).)
  6. Annotate 2007, 2009 and 2021 (right-click → Annotate → Point). Look at the debris band. Where are the steps?
  7. Title stating the finding; caption with source, snapshot date and “objects dated by launch date”.

11.3.3.2 Sheet 2 · “Payloads launched per year, by power” (data source B)

  1. Launch year → Columns (continuous dimension), CNT([NORAD_CAT_ID]) → Rows, Power → Colour, mark type Bar. Where does the Cold War end, and where does the new race begin?

11.3.3.3 Sheet 3 · “How concentrated is the new space race?” — the HHI (data source B)

  1. Create:

    Owner share  =  {FIXED [Launch year], [OWNER] : COUNT([NORAD_CAT_ID])}
              / {FIXED [Launch year] : COUNT([NORAD_CAT_ID])}
HHI piece    =  10000 * [Owner share] / {FIXED [Launch year] : COUNT([NORAD_CAT_ID])}

    Why the second line: each owner’s squared share must be counted once, but the owner appears on as many rows as it has payloads. Spreading share × 10,000 over the year’s rows means that summing an owner’s rows gives exactly share² × 10,000.

  2. Launch year → Columns, SUM([HHI piece]) → Rows, mark type Line. That sum is the HHI. Check: 1957 must give 10,000 (two Soviet payloads). Reference lines at 2,500 and 1,800, labelled.

  3. Why the data source filter in step 6: a FIXED expression is computed before ordinary filters. Had you filtered OBJECT_TYPE on the sheet, the shares would still include debris and rocket bodies. (Adding the filter to context also works.)

11.3.3.4 Sheet 4 · “Who launched that year?” (data source B)

11.3.4 4 · Compare and break — 15 min

  1. Differ. Put Sheet 1 beside ESA’s Figure 2.1 and Table 3.1 (end of 2025: 44,964 objects, of which 16,946 payloads). Give your totals by type and one reason for each gap — which catalogues ESA adds, what it calls “unidentified”, what the public catalogue leaves out.

  2. Break: when does debris appear? Your debris band does not step up in 2007, 2009 or 2021. Re-date the four big events’ debris to the date the event happened. In data source A:

    Appearance year  =  IF [Event] = "LAUNCH_DATE" AND CONTAINS([OBJECT_NAME], "FENGYUN 1C DEB") THEN 2007
                    ELSEIF [Event] = "LAUNCH_DATE" AND (CONTAINS([OBJECT_NAME], "COSMOS 2251 DEB")
                         OR CONTAINS([OBJECT_NAME], "IRIDIUM 33 DEB")) THEN 2009
                    ELSEIF [Event] = "LAUNCH_DATE" AND CONTAINS([OBJECT_NAME], "COSMOS 1408 DEB") THEN 2021
                    ELSE [Year] END

    Duplicate Sheet 1 and replace Year by Appearance year. How big is each step now? What about the thousands of other fragments whose event you did not re-date?

  3. Break: flag or firm? On Sheet 5, replace Operator by OWNER, and compute the HHI of active satellites both ways. Which number describes your firm’s dependence better?

  4. Extend. For your team’s firm, name the satellite services it relies on (GNSS timing, connectivity at sea or in remote sites, imagery). Find their operators in the file — NAVSTAR (GPS), GSAT0… (Galileo, coded ESA), BEIDOU, [GLONASS-M] — and add the one that matters to Sheet 5. Optional, with GCAT: orbital launch attempts per year (LaunchCode starting O or D) by launch-provider state.

The model catch, if you need one to beat: a count of objects is not a count of risk — the fragments able to disable a satellite are mostly too small to catalogue, and the public catalogue dates debris by its parent’s launch, so the ASAT tests vanish from a chart built naively on it; and concentration by flag hides dependence on one firm. Beat it — say something specific to your firm.

11.3.8 What loses credit

  • A running total that restarts because early years were filtered out
  • Objects around the Sun or the Moon counted as “in orbit”
  • An HHI that is not 10,000 in 1957 — shares computed after a sheet filter, or squared on every row
  • OWNER presented as the operator, or blank status counted as inactive
  • A dashboard action that silently filters to nothing
  • A catch about ESA rather than about your firm
