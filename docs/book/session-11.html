11  Space Geopolitics: The Orbital Commons and the New Space Race

11.1 Infrastructure overhead

Few multinationals own a satellite. Almost all of them depend on one, and most do not know which. A container line tracks its ships by satellite and talks to their crews by satellite when they are out of range of land. A mining company runs its remote sites over satellite links. An agribusiness forecasts harvests from imagery, an insurer settles flood claims from it, and a buyer of commodities checks the stockpiles of its suppliers with it. Above all, almost every firm depends on satellites for time. Telecommunications networks, electricity grids, data centres and trading venues synchronise their clocks to the signals of global navigation satellite systems, because those signals carry time from atomic clocks in orbit to any receiver on the ground, cheaply and everywhere.

Three services carry most of this dependence. Positioning, navigation and timing comes from four global systems, each run by a state: GPS by the United States, Galileo by the European Union, BeiDou by China and GLONASS by Russia. Connectivity comes from operators in geostationary orbit, 36,000 kilometres up, and since 2019 from constellations of thousands of satellites in low Earth orbit, a few hundred kilometres up. Earth observation comes from state agencies and from a growing number of commercial imaging firms.

Chapters 8 and 10 showed how networks become instruments of power: a network’s hubs are under someone’s jurisdiction, and whoever has jurisdiction can watch the traffic or cut it off. The network of this chapter is physically above the firm. Its hubs are a handful of states and, increasingly, a handful of companies, and its environment is degrading in a way no terrestrial network’s does — through debris that stays where it was created for years or centuries. The scale of the change is recent. The European Space Agency’s 2026 environment report notes that the exponential growth in the number of new payloads continued in 2025, driven by a higher launch frequency, rideshare launches and the deployment of large constellations by commercial operators (ESA Space Debris Office 2026).

The question of this chapter is who holds the risk when orbit is crowded, contested and dominated by one operator — and what the firm can do about it. The answer it develops is that the firm holds more of it than it thinks, that most of it arrives through the reversible and unspectacular threats rather than the destructive ones, and that dependence on a satellite service is, by the construction of space law, dependence on a state.

11.2 A domain written for two superpowers

The law of outer space was written at speed, during the Cold War, by and for two states that were then the only ones able to get there. Sputnik’s launch in October 1957 settled one question before anyone had negotiated it: the Soviet satellite passed over every country on Earth, and no state protested that its airspace had been violated. The principle that satellites may overfly territory — which the United States, planning reconnaissance satellites of its own, had every interest in establishing — became the foundation of the domain.

The rest of the foundation was laid in the Outer Space Treaty, opened for signature in January 1967 and in force by October of that year (United Nations 1967). It remains the constitution of the domain, and the articles that matter to a firm can be read in two pages. Article II declares that outer space “is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means”: no one can own an orbit. Article IV forbids placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit, and reserves the Moon and other celestial bodies for peaceful purposes — but it says nothing to prohibit conventional weapons in orbit, or the testing of weapons that destroy satellites from the ground. Article VI makes states internationally responsible for national activities in space “whether such activities are carried on by governmental agencies or by non-governmental entities”, and requires those activities to be authorised and continually supervised by “the appropriate State”. Article VII makes the launching state liable for damage caused by its objects. Article IX requires states to act with “due regard” to the interests of others and to consult before activities that could cause harmful interference — the closest the Treaty comes to traffic rules, without anyone to enforce them.

Orbital commons — Earth orbit treated as a resource that no one may appropriate (Outer Space Treaty, Article II) and from whose use no one can easily be excluded, but whose use by one actor degrades it for others — through congestion of orbits and spectrum and through debris. The commons is legal in origin and physical in its consequences.

Article IV’s silence on conventional weapons was not an accident; neither superpower wanted to give up the option. Its prohibition on nuclear weapons in orbit, by contrast, had been learned the hard way.

From the archive · Starfish Prime, 1962

On 9 July 1962 the United States detonated a thermonuclear warhead of about 1.4 megatons some 400 kilometres above Johnston Island in the Pacific. The test, Starfish Prime, was intended to study the effects of a nuclear explosion at high altitude. Its most lasting effect was one its planners had underestimated: electrons from the explosion were trapped in the Earth’s magnetic field, creating an artificial radiation belt that persisted for years.

The satellites then in low orbit were few, and several were damaged. Telstar 1, the first commercial communications satellite — built by AT&T’s Bell Laboratories and launched the day after the test — suffered radiation damage to its electronics and failed within months; Britain’s first satellite, Ariel 1, was also damaged. A single test by one state had degraded an orbital environment shared by everyone, and the first commercial satellite was among the casualties.

The Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963 prohibited nuclear tests in outer space, and the Outer Space Treaty four years later prohibited nuclear weapons in orbit. Neither prohibited the next form of the same problem: a conventional weapon that destroys a satellite and fills its orbit with fragments.

Article II has also become the ground of a newer contest, over resources rather than weapons. The Treaty forbids national appropriation of outer space and celestial bodies; it does not say whether a firm may own what it extracts from them. The United States answered the question for its own citizens in the Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act of 2015, which recognises their property rights in resources obtained from asteroids and other bodies, and Luxembourg followed with a law of its own in 2017. In 2020 the United States and seven partners signed the Artemis Accords, a non-binding set of principles for the civil exploration of the Moon that treat resource extraction as consistent with the Treaty; China and Russia announced a joint lunar research station the following year and gathered partners of their own. The pattern resembles the contest over technical standards described in Chapter 6: where the treaty process has stalled, rules are written by coalitions, and a firm’s access to a future market depends on which coalition its home state has joined.

11.3 Liability, registration and the right to transmit

The Outer Space Treaty is the constitution; three further layers turn it into rules a firm encounters.

The first is liability and registration. The Liability Convention of 1972 elaborated Article VII: a launching state is absolutely liable for damage its space objects cause on the surface of the Earth or to aircraft in flight, and liable for fault for damage elsewhere in space. The Registration Convention of 1975 requires launching states to register their objects with the United Nations. The two together mean that every object in orbit has, in law, a state that answers for it — long after it has stopped working.

From the archive · Cosmos 954, 1978

On 24 January 1978 a Soviet ocean-surveillance satellite, Cosmos 954, re-entered the atmosphere over the Northwest Territories of Canada. It was powered by a small nuclear reactor, and its re-entry scattered radioactive debris over a wide stretch of the Canadian north. Canada and the United States mounted a search and clean-up operation, Operation Morning Light, that lasted for months.

Canada presented a claim to the Soviet Union under the Liability Convention and general principles of international law, for the cost of locating, recovering and cleaning up the debris. The claim was settled in 1981, by protocol, for three million Canadian dollars — about half the amount claimed. It remains the only claim ever brought under the Convention.

The case shows both the strength and the limits of the regime. Liability attaches to a state, and a state paid. But it took three years, the settlement was negotiated rather than adjudicated, and the claimant was a state. A firm whose satellite is destroyed by a fragment from another state’s anti-satellite test has no standing under the Convention: it must persuade its own state to claim, against a launching state that may deny responsibility, for damage whose cause it may be unable to prove.

The second layer is spectrum. A satellite is useless without radio frequencies to command it and to carry its service, and frequencies and orbital positions are coordinated by the International Telecommunication Union through its Radio Regulations. An operator’s state files a satellite network with the ITU and must coordinate it with networks filed earlier, so priority goes, broadly, to the first to file. The incentive is to file early and large — sometimes for “paper satellites” that may never fly — and the result is that orbital slots and frequencies are a scarce asset allocated by a procedure most firms never see.

The third layer is national licensing. Article VI’s “authorization and continuing supervision” becomes, in practice, a set of permits: to launch, to use spectrum, to operate a remote-sensing system, to export a satellite or its components, to land a service in a national market. This is where a firm meets space law directly, and where the balance of power enters most visibly — through the export-control lists discussed below and through decisions about which foreign constellations may sell service in which countries. It is also where the rules on debris acquire teeth. The international guidelines are voluntary, but a licence condition is not: in 2022 the US Federal Communications Commission adopted a rule requiring operators of satellites in low orbit that it licenses, or that serve the US market, to dispose of them within five years of the end of their missions, replacing a guideline of twenty-five years. A firm’s satellite provider is therefore bound not by one law of space but by the licensing conditions of every state whose market it serves.

11.4 The physics of a commons

Law makes orbit a commons in one sense: no one may own it. Physics makes it a commons in the stricter sense economists use. A common-pool resource is one from which exclusion is difficult and whose use by one actor subtracts from what is left for others. Garrett Hardin’s parable of the herdsmen on a common pasture, each adding animals because he captures the whole gain while bearing only a share of the cost, is the classic statement of why such resources tend to be overused (Hardin 1968). Elinor Ostrom showed that real communities often escape the tragedy through rules they design and enforce themselves — but under conditions of clear boundaries, monitoring and graduated sanctions that Earth orbit does not meet (Ostrom 1990). Brian Weeden and Tiffany Chow applied her framework to space and found most of its design principles missing (Weeden and Chow 2012).

What turns congestion into a tragedy in orbit is a mechanism identified by Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais in 1978 (Kessler and Cour-Palais 1978). Objects in the same orbital shell occasionally collide; each collision produces fragments; the fragments raise the density of objects in the shell and therefore the rate of further collisions. Below a critical density, the atmosphere drags enough debris down to keep the population in check. Above it, collisions generate fragments faster than drag removes them, and the debris population grows on its own.

Kessler syndrome — the self-sustaining growth of the debris population in an orbital shell once the density of objects passes a threshold at which collisions create fragments faster than atmospheric drag removes them. Past the threshold, the population grows even if nobody launches again.

Altitude matters enormously, because the drag that cleans orbits weakens with height. Debris a few hundred kilometres up re-enters within years; debris above about 800 kilometres can remain for decades or centuries. A fragmentation in a high, busy shell is therefore a long-lived degradation of an asset shared by every operator in it. Two decades ago, simulations by NASA’s debris specialists already suggested that the low-orbit debris population would keep growing through collisions even if all launches stopped (Liou and Johnson 2006).

Economists have since modelled the incentives. Nodir Adilov, Peter Alexander and Brendan Cunningham show that, because each operator ignores the collision risk its satellites and debris impose on others, open access leads to more launches and more debris than would be efficient (Adilov, Alexander, and Cunningham 2015). Akhil Rao, Matthew Burgess and Daniel Kaffine estimate that an internationally harmonised fee on each satellite in orbit — making operators pay for the risk they impose — would greatly increase the long-run value of the space industry (Rao, Burgess, and Kaffine 2020). No such fee exists. Mitigation guidelines adopted through the UN and by space agencies ask operators to limit debris and to remove satellites from busy orbits at the end of their lives; compliance is voluntary and uneven.

For the firm: an orbit is a shared infrastructure whose quality is set by its least careful user. A destructive test by one state, or a rocket stage that explodes years after launch, raises the collision risk — and the insurance price — for every operator in that shell, and therefore for every customer of those operators.

11.5 Three readings of the new space race

Who will govern this commons, and in whose interest? International relations offers three readings of what has come to be called the new space race, and they do not exclude one another.

The first is classical geopolitics carried upwards. Everett Dolman’s Astropolitik takes Mackinder’s logic — whoever controls the pivot controls the world — and applies it to orbit: whoever controls low Earth orbit controls access to the rest of space, and through it can dominate the Earth below (Dolman 2002). On this realist reading the United States should seize and hold that control while it can, and space is a territory to dominate, its orbits and transfer routes the chokepoints of a new strategic geography. It is the anarchy of Chapter 3, with orbits in place of straits.

The second reading is institutional and commercial. Saadia Pekkanen describes the new space race as driven by three intertwined trends (Pekkanen 2019). Democratisation: many more states, and many non-state actors, now operate in space. Commercialisation: firms no longer only supply governments but strike out on their own, with their own capital and customers. Militarisation: states increasingly treat space as a domain of warfare. Binding them together is the fact that about 95 percent of space technologies, on her account, are dual-use. With treaty-making in decline, the domain is governed less by new international law than by national legislation, contracts, and a few old principles. On this reading space is a market, and the firms in it are part of the security problem.

The third reading puts the Earth back at the centre. Bleddyn Bowen argues that spacepower matters for its effects on Earth: orbit is a “cosmic coastline” from which states support — and threaten — what happens on land, at sea and in the air (Bowen 2020). Satellites are valuable because they make terrestrial forces see, communicate and navigate; that is also why they are targets when terrestrial power is contested.

Read together, the three explain why the same constellation can be a consumer broadband business, a military communications network and an instrument of foreign policy all at once. Pekkanen supplies the dual use; Dolman and Bowen supply the strategic stakes. For a multinational the practical conclusion is the same under all three: a commercial satellite service is never only commercial.

11.6 Counterspace: what states can do to satellites

If satellites are targets, the question is what can be done to them, by whom, and with what consequences for the firms that use them. The Secure World Foundation’s annual open-source assessment tracks the capabilities of thirteen countries to deceive, disrupt, deny, degrade or destroy space systems, in five categories (Samson and Brett 2026).

Counterspace capabilities — the means of deceiving, disrupting, denying, degrading or destroying space systems: direct-ascent missiles, co-orbital systems, electronic warfare, directed energy and cyber operations. They differ in whether their effects are reversible and in who bears them first — the satellite’s owner, every operator in the orbit, or the users on the ground.

Direct-ascent weapons are missiles launched from the Earth to destroy a satellite. Their effects are irreversible and, at most altitudes, create debris that threatens every operator in the shell. Co-orbital systems are satellites that approach another to inspect it, interfere with it or attack it. Electronic warfare jams or spoofs signals — the uplink to a satellite, its downlink to users, or navigation signals on the ground — and its effects end when the jamming stops. Directed-energy weapons use lasers or microwaves to dazzle or damage sensors. Cyber operations attack satellites, ground stations or user terminals, and can disable a service without touching a satellite.

The assessment’s central finding is the one that matters most for business: many states have researched and tested destructive capabilities, but only non-destructive ones are actively used against satellites in current military operations. Four states have destroyed one of their own satellites with a missile launched from the ground: the United States in 1985 — and, in 2008, by intercepting a failing satellite at low altitude — China in 2007, India in 2019 and Russia in 2021. In April 2022 the United States declared that it would not conduct destructive direct-ascent tests, and in December 2022 the UN General Assembly called on all states to make the same commitment.

What firms have actually suffered is the reversible kind. On 24 February 2022, the day Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine began, a cyberattack on the ground segment of Viasat’s KA-SAT network disabled thousands of user modems in Ukraine and across Europe, including those used for the remote monitoring of thousands of wind turbines in Germany. The European Union, the United Kingdom and the United States attributed the attack to Russia. The satellite was untouched; the service failed for its customers, many of them nowhere near the war. Since then, the jamming and spoofing of navigation signals around conflict zones has become routine. In 2024 Finnair suspended its flights to Tartu, in Estonia, for several weeks because interference made the airport’s satellite-based approach procedures unusable.

The lesson for an analyst. Destructive tests make headlines and debris. Jamming, spoofing and cyberattacks on ground segments make losses — and the losses fall on the customers, not on the satellite. A firm’s counterspace exposure is measured on the ground, in the regions where its ships, aircraft and sites depend on signals from orbit.

11.7 The debris record, and how it is counted

The most complete public account of the orbital environment comes from ESA’s Space Debris Office, which has published an annual Space Environment Report since 2017. The 2026 edition, with data to the end of 2025, is the source of the figures that follow (ESA Space Debris Office 2026), and its method deserves reading before its numbers.

ESA counts two kinds of objects. Catalogued objects are those tracked and published in catalogues: the US public catalogue, the Russian Vimpel catalogue and, for the early decades, the United Kingdom’s historical RAE tables. Asserted objects are known to exist by design — a satellite released from a dispenser, a component separated as planned — even if not yet catalogued. The two are cross-correlated in ESA’s DISCOS database and classified into object classes. Objects too small or too erratic to be tracked are outside the report’s scope.

On that basis, the report counts 44,964 objects in Earth orbit at the end of 2025, of which 16,946 are payloads, and 25,060 objects in low Earth orbit (ESA Space Debris Office 2026). It observes that, ever since the start of the space age, there has been more debris in orbit than operational satellites. And its most important finding for anyone who thinks the debris problem is the weapons tests is this: fragmentations other than collisions — explosions, mostly of old rocket stages and batteries — are the dominant source of debris. Non-deliberate fragmentations have averaged 9.8 a year over the last two decades, and 2024 alone added more than 3,000 catalogued fragments, mostly from propulsion-related break-ups.

The report’s count of objects in geocentric orbit by object class since 1957 shows the cumulative result of seven decades of launches, explosions, collisions and re-entries, and three events stand out on it. On 11 January 2007 China destroyed its Fengyun-1C weather satellite with a ground-launched missile, at about 850 kilometres; the test remains the largest single source of catalogued debris in the public catalogue, and most of its thousands of fragments are still in orbit. On 10 February 2009 the active Iridium 33 and the defunct Cosmos 2251 collided at about 780 kilometres — the first accidental collision between two intact satellites, Kessler’s mechanism observed. And on 15 November 2021 Russia destroyed Cosmos 1408 at roughly 470 to 490 kilometres, producing some 1,800 catalogued fragments; at that altitude almost all re-entered within three years, and ESA attributes the 2022 spike in re-entering debris to the test.

The contrast between the first and third events is a lesson in altitude. Both were deliberate destructions of a satellite by its own state; one polluted a high orbit for decades, the other a low orbit for a few years. A count of objects treats them alike.

What the count does not measure. It counts objects, not risk: a ten-centimetre fragment and a five-tonne rocket stage each count once, and the objects too small to catalogue — the majority of the fragments able to disable a satellite — do not appear at all. It is drawn from catalogues that themselves improve: when sensors get better, the count rises with nothing new having happened in orbit. And it shows the environment, not the exposure of any operator or customer, which depends on which shells their satellites use.

11.8 From a catalogue to a stock

ESA’s series can be approximated from public data, and the approximation shows how a concept — the number of objects in orbit — becomes a number. The public catalogue, maintained from US Space Force data and redistributed by CelesTrak, does not record a stock. It records objects: one row for each catalogued object over its whole life, with a launch date and, if it has re-entered, a decay date. There is no “year” in the file. An object is in orbit in a given year if it was launched by then and had not decayed by then, so the stock in each year is the running sum of launches minus the running sum of decays — a stock rebuilt from two flows. Much of what a risk analyst needs to know is a stock of this kind, which no one publishes and which has to be rebuilt from the events that change it.

The construction exposes the catalogue’s decisions. Debris carries the launch date of its parent object, not the date of the event that created it: the fragments of China’s 2007 test are dated to the launch of Fengyun-1C in 1999, so a debris series built on launch dates moves in the wrong year, and the published record and the reconstruction disagree precisely at the events that matter. “Not decayed” is not the same as “in Earth orbit”, because some objects orbit the Sun, the Moon or a Lagrange point. The owner field records a country, organisation or company code, not the operator: every Starlink satellite is simply American, and the former Soviet Union and Russia share one code. Operational status describes today only, so the file cannot say how many satellites were active in any past year. And the public catalogue is not the environment: it omits classified objects and everything too small to track, which is one reason ESA’s totals, drawn from more catalogues and asserted objects, are higher.

11.9 One operator

The new space race has a feature the Cold War one did not. In the first years of the space age, concentration was extreme because only two states could launch anything; it fell for decades as Europe, Japan, China, India and many others acquired the capability, and as commercial operators multiplied. It has risen again — not because states have dropped out, but because one company has launched more satellites than everyone else together. SpaceX’s Starlink, first launched in May 2019, grew in six years into the majority of the world’s operating satellites. Measured on the public catalogue’s owner codes, the concentration of payloads launched each year has returned to levels last seen in the Cold War, with a firm rather than a superpower at the top.

Concentration needs a measure, and the standard one is the index that antitrust authorities use. Its origins are in geopolitics: Albert Hirschman devised an index of the concentration of a country’s trade in 1945, to show how Nazi Germany had used the structure of trade to make its smaller partners dependent on it (Hirschman 1945). Orris Herfindahl reinvented it a few years later for industrial concentration, and it carries both their names.

Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) — the sum of the squared market shares of all participants, with shares in percent, so that it runs from near 0 (many small participants) to 10,000 (one participant with everything). It weights large shares heavily: one participant with 75 percent contributes 5,625 on its own. US merger guidelines treat markets above 2,500 (U.S. Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission 2010) or, in the 2023 revision, above 1,800 (U.S. Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission 2023) as highly concentrated.

The index, built from the catalogue, inherits the catalogue’s owner-code problem twice over. Concentration by state hides the fact that one company dominates the American share; concentration by operator must be reconstructed from object names. Each choice yields a different index, and each answers a different question — about the balance of power among states, or about the dependence of customers on firms.

Concentration matters to a firm because of what it implies about dependence, and Starlink in Ukraine shows what that dependence means. Within days of the 2022 invasion, Starlink terminals became a backbone of Ukraine’s military and civil communications. The company’s decisions about where its service would work — including, by its owner’s account, a refusal to enable it for an attack near Crimea in 2022 — became questions of war policy, and governments moved to contract for the service directly.

Farrell and Newman’s framework, introduced in Chapter 8, applies without modification (Farrell and Newman 2019). A constellation is a hub. Its operator sees who connects and from where — a panopticon — and can switch off a user, a region or a country — a chokepoint. Weaponisation requires two conditions: that the network be concentrated, and that a state can direct the firm that runs the hub. Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty guarantees the second, because every operator has a licensing state that is internationally responsible for its activities and must supervise them. A firm that depends on one operator depends on that operator’s state as well.

States are responding as the balance-of-power reasoning of Chapter 3 would predict: by building their own. The European Union’s IRIS² secure-connectivity programme, established by Regulation (EU) 2023/588, China’s Qianfan and Guowang constellations, and the many national launch programmes are all, in part, answers to dependence on one American firm. Export controls draw the same line from the other side. Spacecraft and their components sit either on the US Munitions List, under Category XV of the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, or — for most commercial satellites since 2014 — under the Export Administration Regulations, in the “9x515” entries; a non-US manufacturer whose satellite contains a controlled US component needs US permission to sell it to some customers. The balance of power reaches into the bill of materials.

Navigation is the older case of the same pattern, and the one on which almost every firm already depends. GPS was built by the US military. Its opening to the world followed a tragedy: after the Soviet Union shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007, which had strayed into Soviet airspace, in September 1983, the United States announced that GPS would be made available for civilian use once it was operational. For years the civilian signal was deliberately degraded — “Selective Availability” — until the degradation was switched off in May 2000. The service on which the world’s networks and logistics came to rely was, and remains, a gift that one state chose to give and could in principle withdraw or degrade, regionally or globally. Europe’s decision to build Galileo, and China’s to build BeiDou, were in large part refusals to accept that dependence. For a firm, the existence of four global systems is a genuine diversification — but only if its receivers use more than one, and only against the failure or withdrawal of a system, not against a jammer in the next port.

11.10 Through which channels, and what the firm can do

Space risk reaches the profit and loss account through the same five channels as every other risk in this book, and naming the channel is the first step towards a decision.

Through market access, export controls and licensing decide whether a satellite, a terminal or a service can be sold or switched on in a given market, and spectrum and landing rights decide whether a constellation may serve a country at all. Through inputs, a firm that buys connectivity for its ships or mines from one constellation, or that times its networks from one state’s navigation system, has a single point of failure with a sovereign behind it. Through finance and payments, trading venues and payment systems time-stamp transactions from navigation signals, and the insurance capacity for space assets — a thin market — shrinks after large losses. Through assets and people, ships and aircraft operating in jammed or spoofed regions are rerouted, delayed, or misreport their positions, with consequences for safety and for sanctions compliance alike. Through data and operations, a cyberattack on a ground segment cuts the firm’s links, as the KA-SAT modems showed, and the loss of an imagery feed leaves a crop forecast or a supply-chain monitor blind.

Four kinds of evidence bear on that exposure, and each can be tied in advance to a decision. Dependence: the share of a critical service — connectivity, imagery, timing — supplied by one operator or one state’s system; above a share the firm has fixed beforehand, the decision is to contract a second. Interference: the official warnings of navigation-signal interference that aviation authorities publish for the regions where the firm’s ships, aircraft or sites operate. Environment: destructive tests and fragmentation events in the orbital shells its providers use, and the yearly indicators in ESA’s report. Rules: changes in export-control lists, licensing conditions and ITU filings that affect its suppliers or markets.

The strategies available each have a cost and a limit. A firm can buy multi-orbit, multi-operator connectivity — geostationary and low-orbit links from two providers — at the price of integration, but the second operator may share the first one’s licensing state. It can use multi-constellation receivers that combine GPS, Galileo and others, which is cheap and protects against the failure of one system, but a local jammer can still blind them all. It can invest in terrestrial back-ups — holdover clocks and time delivered over fibre and cellular networks — which requires capital and is unavailable at sea or at remote sites. It can buy insurance, in a thin market whose capacity shrinks after large losses. And it can practise compliance by design, classifying every space-related component it buys or sells under the relevant export-control regimes, at the cost of staff and delay, knowing that the rules change with the balance of power.

A single case ties these together. A shipping subsidiary’s only broadband at sea is one low-orbit constellation; the operator’s government has been asked by an ally to switch the service off over a strait the firm’s ships use. The firm’s options this quarter turn on the distinction between dependence on a service and dependence on a state. A second operator under the same licensing state diversifies the first and not the second; a second operator under another state diversifies both, at the price of a second set of export controls and a second government’s discretion.

11.11 A short review of the literature

The legal foundation is the Outer Space Treaty (United Nations 1967), whose Articles II, VI and VII make orbit unappropriable, place every private operator under a responsible state, and attach liability to the launching state. The governance problem it leaves open is that of a commons. Hardin (1968) states why open-access resources are overused and Ostrom (1990) the conditions under which communities escape the tragedy; Weeden and Chow (2012) apply Ostrom’s design principles to orbit and find most of them absent. The physical mechanism is Kessler and Cour-Palais (1978), with Liou and Johnson (2006) showing by simulation that the low-orbit debris population would grow even without new launches. The economics of the externality is set out by Adilov, Alexander, and Cunningham (2015), and Rao, Burgess, and Kaffine (2020) estimate the gains from orbital-use fees.

The strategic literature divides along the lines of this chapter’s three readings. Dolman (2002) carries classical geopolitics into orbit; Bowen (2020) argues that spacepower matters for its terrestrial effects; Pekkanen (2019) describes the democratisation, commercialisation and militarisation of a domain in which most technology is dual-use and treaty-making has stalled. The empirical record of threats is the Secure World Foundation’s annual assessment (Samson and Brett 2026), and that of the environment ESA’s annual report (ESA Space Debris Office 2026).

For the firm, the bridge is Farrell and Newman (2019): a concentrated constellation under a state’s jurisdiction meets both conditions for weaponised interdependence. The measure of concentration is Hirschman (1945)’s index, conceived to analyse how trade structures create political dependence, and its antitrust thresholds are those of the US merger guidelines (U.S. Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission 2010, 2023).

11.12 What the evidence does not license

The public record of the orbital environment is unusually good. ESA’s count of objects by class, the catalogue from which it can be approximated, and the concentration of who launches payloads from Sputnik to today together show the steady accumulation of debris, the steps that the 2007 test, the 2009 collision and the 2021 test cut into the record, the explosion of payloads since the large constellations began, and a concentration index that falls for decades and then climbs back. They also show the decisions underneath: the dating of debris, the filtering of objects that do not orbit the Earth, the reading of owner codes, the definition of an active satellite.

Three conclusions do not follow. A rising count of objects does not tell a firm that its services are at greater risk, because risk depends on the size of objects, the shells its providers use and the manoeuvring capability of their satellites — none of which the count contains. The absence of a spike does not mean the absence of a threat: the counterspace effects firms have actually suffered — jamming, spoofing, cyberattacks on ground segments — leave no trace in a catalogue of objects. And concentration by owner code is not the firm’s dependence. A firm may buy its connectivity from a small operator in an unconcentrated market and still depend on one state’s navigation system for its time, or buy from the dominant operator through a reseller and not know it.

What the evidence does support is a conclusion about where the risk sits. Space was governed for half a century by a bargain between two states that could each destroy the other’s satellites and chose, mostly, not to. That bargain has not been replaced; it has been overlaid by a market in which one firm carries much of the world’s new capacity, by coalitions writing rules the treaty process cannot, and by an environment that the least careful user degrades for everyone. In each of those layers the risk falls less on the owner of the satellite than on the customer of the service, and the customer is usually the last to know. A multinational’s exposure to space therefore begins not with the sky but with a list it rarely keeps: the satellite services it buys, the operators and states behind each, and the regions where its ships, aircraft and sites depend on signals that someone else can switch off.
