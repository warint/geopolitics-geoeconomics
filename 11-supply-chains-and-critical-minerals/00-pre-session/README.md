# Session 11 — Pre-session preparation

> **When a handful of countries mine and refine what your products cannot be made without, who
> really holds the risk?**

Slides: [`INTE60019A-S11-Pre-Session.qmd`](INTE60019A-S11-Pre-Session.qmd)

Four things to do before class. Budget **about two hours** — and more if you transcribe slowly.
**The transcription in step 3 is not optional**: the practice round starts from it.

| | Before class | Used in class for |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Read the OECD paper | the target figures |
| **2** | Read Le Billon & Shykora | the lecture discussion and the catch |
| **3** | Collect the three datasets; transcribe four USGS tables | Practice round 11 |
| **4** | Self-check | the lecture |

---

## 1. The big work — 45 min

**Kowalski, P., & Legendre, C. (2023). Raw materials critical for the green transition:
Production, international trade and export restrictions. *OECD Trade Policy Papers*, No. 269. OECD
Publishing.** [doi:10.1787/c6bb598b-en](https://doi.org/10.1787/c6bb598b-en) · free

Read for the **argument**, not for coverage. Annotate four things:

| | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **1** | How the paper **measures** concentration of production and trade |
| **2** | How the **export-restrictions inventory** is built: which measures, which commodities, which countries |
| **3** | The **strongest single piece of evidence** that restrictions have spread since 2009 |
| **4** | One **limitation** a procurement director would raise |

Find the figures that show the **growth of export restrictions over time** and the **concentration
of production by country**: they are today's targets.

---

## 2. The companion reading — 30 min

**Le Billon, P., & Shykora, L. (2020). Conflicts, commodities and the environmental geopolitics of
supply chains.** In S. O'Lear (Ed.), *A research agenda for environmental geopolitics* (pp. 59–73).
Edward Elgar. [doi:10.4337/9781788971249.00012](https://doi.org/10.4337/9781788971249.00012)

Annotate: (a) what makes a commodity a *conflict* commodity; (b) how supply chains carry conflict
and environmental harm from extraction sites to consumers; (c) one governance response they discuss
and why it falls short.

Revisit **Farrell & Newman (2019)** from Session 08 — the definitions of *chokepoint* and
*panopticon* effects only.

---

## 3. The data — 40 min

Details for all three: [`../data/README.md`](../data/README.md).

1. **OECD export-restrictions inventory** — export the full table as CSV from the OECD Data Explorer.
2. **USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries** — download the latest summaries for **cobalt, natural
   graphite, lithium, rare earths**, and **transcribe** each *World Mine Production* table into one
   file, `usgs_world_production.csv`, following the five rules in the data README. Split the four
   minerals across the team; one person checks every row against the PDF.
3. **GSCPI** — download `gscpi_interactive_data.csv` and identify the **latest vintage** column.

---

## 4. Self-check — 15 min

Answer on paper. If you cannot, that is what the lecture is for.

1. In Farrell and Newman's terms, what is the difference between a **chokepoint** and a
   **panopticon** effect? Give one example of each from minerals or finance.
2. A mineral has three producers with shares of 70%, 20% and 10%. Compute its **HHI**. Is it
   "highly concentrated"?
3. Why might a country restrict the export of an ore it produces, *apart* from geopolitical
   coercion? Give two reasons.
4. Name a product your team's firm sells that would stop without one of the four minerals.
