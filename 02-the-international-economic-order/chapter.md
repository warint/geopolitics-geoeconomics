## The rules a firm does not notice

A multinational firm lives inside a set of rules it rarely thinks about. When a Canadian
manufacturer ships components to a plant in Mexico, assembles them, and sells the result in
Germany, it assumes a great deal: that the tariff it pays in Germany is the same as its competitors
pay; that a dispute over that tariff will be settled by procedure rather than by retaliation; that
the dollars it earns can be converted and moved; that the patent on the component will be respected
in the country where it is assembled. None of these assumptions is a law of nature. Each was
written, by particular states, at particular moments, in their own interest, and each is held in
place by something — power, shared interest, or institutions with momentum of their own.

This chapter is about that set of rules: the international economic order. Its question is simple to
state. Who wrote the rules multinationals play by, and what happens to the rules when the writers'
share of the world shrinks? The answer has three parts. The first is historical: how the post-1945
order was built, in layers, by the United States and its allies. The second is economic, and comes
from Richard Baldwin's *The Great Convergence*, read against the World Bank's own national-accounts
data: how a fall in the cost of moving ideas after about 1990 moved industry and income from the
countries that wrote the rules to a handful that did not. The third is political: why that
convergence produced a backlash, and what the backlash means for a firm whose supply chain was built
on the old assumptions.

## What an order is

The word "order" is used loosely, so it is worth fixing.

::: {.definition}
[**International order**]{.term} — a set of rules, institutions and shared expectations governing
relations between states, sustained by some distribution of power among them.
:::

::: {.definition}
[**International economic order**]{.term} — the part of the international order that governs
trade, money and investment: tariff commitments and their exceptions, exchange-rate and
capital-account arrangements, the treatment of foreign investors and their property, and the
procedures by which disputes about all of these are settled.
:::

Three questions describe any economic order, and they are worth keeping in view for the rest of the
book. *Who wrote the rules?* — which states designed the institutions, and in whose interest.
*What do the rules say?* — how open they are to trade, to capital and to investment, and how
disputes are resolved. *What holds them in place?* — whether it is the power of a dominant state, a
common interest among many, or the inertia of institutions that are costly to abandon. The first
question is about history, the second about law, and the third about politics. A firm's exposure to
geopolitical risk is largely a matter of the third: rules that are held in place by one state's
power are as durable as that power and that state's willingness to use it.

The third question has a classic answer in political economy, and a classic rejoinder.
@kindleberger1973, writing about the Great Depression, argued that the interwar collapse of trade
and finance happened because no state was both able and willing to stabilise the system — Britain
was able no longer, and the United States was not yet willing. An open world economy, on this view,
needs a stabiliser: a state that keeps its market open in a slump, lends counter-cyclically, and
acts as lender of last resort. @krasner1976 generalised the point into what became known as
hegemonic stability theory: openness is most likely when a single state is economically dominant,
because the dominant state gains most from openness and has the means to enforce it. The rejoinder
came from @keohane1984, whose *After Hegemony* argued that institutions, once established, can
sustain cooperation even as the hegemon that created them declines, because they reduce the costs of
making and monitoring agreements and states have reasons to keep them. Whether the post-1945 order
outlives the relative decline of its authors is the empirical test of that debate, and it is being
run now.

::: {.definition}
[**Hegemonic stability**]{.term} — the proposition that an open international economy is most
likely to be created and maintained when a single dominant state has both the capacity and the
interest to supply its rules and absorb the costs of enforcing them.
:::

## The post-1945 order, in four layers

The order that multinationals take for granted was assembled in layers over half a century. It is
easier to understand as a stack than as a single design.

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · Bretton Woods, 1944]{.archive-label}

In July 1944, while the war in Europe and the Pacific was still being fought, delegates of
forty-four allied nations met at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. The two
dominant figures were John Maynard Keynes for the United Kingdom and Harry Dexter White for the
United States Treasury, and the conference largely ratified a compromise their governments had
negotiated in advance. The British wanted a system that would protect a debtor country's freedom to
pursue full employment; the Americans, who would be the world's creditor, wanted a system in which
their money would not be used to finance others' deficits indefinitely.

The conference created the International Monetary Fund, to oversee a system of fixed but adjustable
exchange rates and to lend to countries facing temporary balance-of-payments difficulties, and the
International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the core of what became the World Bank.
Currencies were pegged to the dollar; the dollar was convertible into gold at a fixed price for
foreign official holders. Capital controls were not merely tolerated but expected: the founders had
watched short-term capital flows destabilise the interwar economy and did not intend to repeat
the experience [@eichengreen2019].

What is striking, read from the twenty-first century, is how much of the design reflected the
balance of power in the room. The United States held most of the world's monetary gold and was the
only major economy undamaged by the war. The institution's voting shares, its headquarters in
Washington, and the central role of the dollar all followed. The rules were multilateral in form and
American in their foundations — which is the observation that every later debate about the order
starts from.
:::

The second layer was trade. The planned International Trade Organization, which was to have stood
beside the Fund and the Bank, was never established: its charter was negotiated but not ratified by
the United States. What survived was a provisional agreement, the **General Agreement on Tariffs
and Trade** of 1947, which turned out to be one of the most durable provisional arrangements in
history. The GATT rested on two principles. **Most-favoured-nation** treatment required each member
to extend to all members any tariff concession it gave to one; **national treatment** required that
imported goods, once across the border, be treated no worse than domestic ones. On that basis,
successive negotiating rounds cut tariffs on manufactured goods among the industrial countries.

The record of those rounds, which @tbl-ch02-gatt-rounds reproduces from the WTO's own history of
the GATT years, is worth reading row by row, because it shows the order growing in two directions at
once. It grew in *membership*: twenty-three countries negotiated in Geneva in 1947, sixty-two in the
Kennedy Round of the 1960s, one hundred and twenty-three in the Uruguay Round. And it grew in
*scope*: the first five rounds dealt with tariffs alone; the Kennedy Round added anti-dumping; the
Tokyo Round reached non-tariff measures and a set of plurilateral "codes" that only some members
signed; the Uruguay Round took in services, intellectual property, agriculture, textiles and dispute
settlement, and created a new organisation to administer the result.

Two features of the table matter for what follows. The first is that the countries that joined
late joined an agenda already set. A developing economy that acceded in the 1980s or 1990s did not
negotiate the most-favoured-nation principle, the treatment of anti-dumping duties, or the
structure of tariff bindings; it accepted them as the price of entry, and bargained over its own
schedule. The second is that the last round moved the order behind the border, into the domestic
regulation of services, patents and investment, where political resistance is strongest. Even before then, the WTO's own history records,
the rule-writers had turned in the recessions of the 1970s and early 1980s to bilateral
market-sharing arrangements and agricultural subsidies the GATT could not discipline
[@wto_gattyears].

The third layer is not an institution but a bargain, and the political scientist John Ruggie gave it
its name.

::: {.definition}
[**Embedded liberalism**]{.term} — the post-war compromise in which states committed to
multilateral openness in trade while retaining the domestic policy autonomy — macroeconomic
management, social insurance, capital controls — needed to make that openness politically
sustainable at home [@ruggie1982].
:::

Ruggie's point was that the post-war order was not the liberalism of the nineteenth century
restored. It was liberalism *embedded* in a domestic social settlement: governments would open their
markets, but they would also cushion their citizens from the dislocations openness caused, and they
would keep the instruments to do so. Free trade was managed; capital was controlled; full employment
was a government responsibility. The bargain held for roughly a quarter of a century, and its
unravelling is the background to most of what follows.

The fourth layer is the neoliberal turn and the creation of the World Trade Organization. The
monetary pillar of Bretton Woods broke first.

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · The end of gold convertibility, 1971]{.archive-label}

On 15 August 1971, President Nixon announced that the United States would suspend the
convertibility of the dollar into gold for foreign official holders, and imposed a temporary
surcharge on imports. The announcement was made without consulting the other members of the IMF. A
realignment of exchange rates negotiated at the Smithsonian Institution in December 1971 tried to
restore a system of pegs; by 1973 the major currencies were floating against one another, and they
have floated since [@eichengreen2019].

The episode is usually told as the end of Bretton Woods, and in its monetary design it was. But it
is more instructive as a demonstration of the third question — what holds the rules in place. The
system had depended on the United States' willingness to subordinate its own policy to the gold
parity. When that willingness ended, the rule ended, unilaterally, on a Sunday evening. The
institutions survived: the IMF found new roles, and the dollar remained the centre of the system,
now without the anchor. The order changed form without collapsing, which is the pattern to look for
whenever someone announces the end of an order.
:::

Through the 1980s and 1990s, capital controls in the industrial countries were dismantled, and many
developing countries followed. In trade, the Uruguay Round of negotiations, launched in 1986, ended
with the creation of the **World Trade Organization** in 1995. The WTO differed from the GATT in two
ways that matter for firms. Its rules reached far beyond tariffs on goods — into services, into
intellectual property through the TRIPS agreement, into investment measures through TRIMs — so
that the order began to govern not only what crossed borders but what happened behind them. And it
had a binding dispute-settlement mechanism, with an Appellate Body, so that rulings could no longer
be blocked by the losing party. China joined in 2001, after fifteen years of negotiation, on terms
that bound it to many of these rules.

How should the result be characterised? @ikenberry2011 argues that the American-led order was
**easy to join and hard to overturn**: open, rule-based, and institutionalised enough that rising
states would gain more by joining it than by challenging it. On this reading, the order is not
merely an expression of American power but a set of institutions that bind the hegemon too, and
that therefore give others a reason to accept it. Chapter 3 sets the realist reply beside this
view: that institutions reflect the distribution of power and will be revised when it shifts.

## Firms as authors as well as subjects

It would be a mistake to read the order as something states wrote and firms merely obeyed. At
several points, firms were among its authors, and the layers that matter most to multinationals
today are the ones in which their influence was greatest.

The clearest case is intellectual property. Before the Uruguay Round, patents, copyrights and
trademarks were governed by conventions administered outside the trade regime, with weak
enforcement and wide latitude for national policy; many developing countries did not grant patents
on pharmaceuticals at all. The TRIPS agreement changed that, by making minimum standards of
intellectual-property protection a condition of WTO membership, enforceable through the
dispute-settlement mechanism. @sell2003 traces how this happened: a small coalition of
chief executives from American pharmaceutical, software and entertainment firms organised in the
1980s, defined the problem as one of trade rather than of intellectual property, persuaded the
United States government to adopt their framing, and worked with counterparts in Europe and Japan
to build a joint position. Twelve corporations, in her account, effectively drafted the core of a
public international law. The episode is the strongest evidence that the post-1945 order was not
only American in its foundations but, in some of its later layers, corporate.

A second case is the protection of foreign investment, which the GATT and the WTO largely left
aside. Here the instrument was the bilateral investment treaty. The first was signed between West
Germany and Pakistan in 1959; the Convention establishing the International Centre for Settlement
of Investment Disputes, under World Bank auspices, followed in 1965; and in the 1990s the number of
such treaties multiplied. Their distinctive feature is that they allow a foreign investor to bring a
claim directly against a host state before an international tribunal, without needing its own
government to take up the case. For a multinational, this is the layer of the order that most
directly answers Kobrin's political risk (Chapter 1): it is the legal response to expropriation and
discriminatory treatment. It is also the layer that has attracted the most political contestation
in the 2010s, from governments that came to see investor-state arbitration as a constraint on
regulation — Rodrik's trilemma, applied to a single clause.

The point for the firm is twofold. First, the rules a firm relies on are not all of a piece:
some are multilateral (tariff bindings), some are bilateral (investment treaties), some are
private or semi-private (standards, the subject of Chapter 6). They will fail in different
ways. Second, if firms helped write some of the rules, they have a voice in how they are rewritten:
the influence a firm exercises over the rules is one of the few geopolitical exposures it can
partly manage from the inside.

## Three costs, three unbundlings

Baldwin's book offers a different way into the same history, one that starts not from institutions
but from costs. Globalisation, he argues, has been shaped by three successive constraints: the cost
of moving **goods**, the cost of moving **ideas**, and the cost of moving **people** [@baldwin2016].
Each time one of these costs fell sharply, it "unbundled" something that geography had previously
held together.

::: {.definition}
[**Unbundling**]{.term} — in Baldwin's account, the separation, made possible by a fall in a
particular cost of distance, of activities that had previously had to take place in the same
location: first production from consumption, then the stages of production from one another.
:::

### The first unbundling: goods move, ideas stay

From the nineteenth century, steam ships and railways made it cheap to move goods. Production no
longer had to happen near consumption: a factory in Manchester could clothe customers in India. But
the cost of moving ideas remained high, because coordinating complex production still required
people to be close to one another. So industry clustered — in Britain, western Europe, North
America, later Japan — where know-how could circulate cheaply within a local area. The result was
the great divergence: the industrialising North pulled away from the rest of the world in income,
because the knowledge that made factories productive stayed where it was invented. Trade grew, but
it was trade in finished goods between nations, and the comparative advantage that governed it was
a national property.

### The second unbundling: ideas move, inside the firm

From about 1990, information and communication technology made it cheap to move ideas — to
coordinate complex activities at a distance. The factory itself could now be unbundled. Stages of
production that had to sit together in one place could be dispersed across countries, each stage
located where its costs were lowest, and coordinated from headquarters by telecommunications and
computers. A firm could combine its own technology, management and marketing with low-wage labour
in a nearby developing country — for the United States, Mexico; for Germany, Poland and central
Europe; for Japan, the rest of East Asia.

The crucial move in Baldwin's argument is what travelled with the stages of production. When a
Japanese firm moved assembly to Thailand, it did not move only the assembly. It moved the know-how
to perform it: the process engineering, the quality systems, the managerial practice. Know-how that
had been confined within national borders now flowed across them — but inside the firm's own
production network, under its control. This is why the second unbundling produced convergence
rather than divergence. A handful of developing economies, those that joined the supply chains of
northern firms, industrialised at a speed previously unknown, because they did not have to invent
the know-how; they had it brought to them.

::: {.definition}
[**Global value chain**]{.term} — the international fragmentation of a production process into
stages located in different countries and coordinated, usually by a lead firm, so that what
crosses borders is increasingly intermediate goods, services and know-how rather than finished
products.
:::

Baldwin's empirical picture of the shift emphasises its regional character. Supply-chain trade,
@baldwin2015 show, is organised around a few hubs — "Factory North America", "Factory Europe",
"Factory Asia" — each pairing high-technology headquarter economies with lower-wage neighbours. The
second unbundling was less a globalisation than a set of regionalisations, linked by the
headquarters economies.

### The third unbundling: people, virtually

The third constraint is the cost of moving people, and specifically of face-to-face interaction.
Baldwin's prospective third unbundling would come from telepresence and telerobotics: technologies
that let a worker in one country perform services in another without travelling. It is a forecast
rather than a finding, and the least developed part of the argument.

### The multinational as the vehicle

For the multinational, the key point of the book is a single sentence's worth of argument: in the
second unbundling, *the multinational firm is the vehicle of convergence*. The convergence of
incomes between the old industrial economies and the new ones did not happen through anonymous
markets in finished goods. It happened through supply chains that multinational firms built, owned
or controlled, along which they moved technology and organisational capability.

That is why geopolitics now reaches firms so directly. If know-how, jobs and capabilities travel
along supply chains, then supply chains are where states will intervene when they decide that the
movement of know-how threatens their security. The export controls of the 2020s on advanced
semiconductors and the equipment to make them are attempts to stop exactly the flow the second
unbundling made cheap. The firm's supply chain has become a geopolitical object, not because firms
chose to make it one, but because it carries what states care about.

## The convergence in the data

Baldwin's evidence for the convergence is a picture: the share of the G7 — Canada, France, Germany,
Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom and the United States — in world income, which rose for over a
century and then fell sharply from around 1990. The share lost by the G7 was gained mostly by a
small group of developing economies, which he calls the "Industrialising Six": China, Korea, India,
Poland, Indonesia and Thailand.

The picture can be checked against the World Bank's World Development Indicators, and the check is
instructive, because reproducing it requires three decisions, each of which shapes what the figure
says.

The first is that **the G7 is not in the data**. The World Bank publishes the world total and many
aggregates — income groups, regions, the euro area — but no G7. The analyst constructs it by summing
the seven members, and the G7 that results is a political grouping turned into a statistic by
someone's choice. Done with GDP in current US dollars, the result confirms Baldwin's shape and dates
its turning point precisely. In the release of October 2026, the G7 produced about 66 per cent of
world output in 1970. The share dipped in the 1970s, recovered through the 1980s, and peaked at
about 67.5 per cent in 1993. It then fell almost without interruption: below 60 per cent by 2005,
below 50 by 2010, and about 44 per cent in 2024 and 2025. In a single generation the economies that
wrote the rules went from two-thirds of world output to well under half. @fig-ch02-g7-share draws
the series.

Three things are easy to miss. The first is how flat the G7 line is for two decades
before the fall. The 1970s dip — from 66 per cent in 1972 to about 60 per cent in 1981 — is the oil
shock and the dollar's depreciation after the end of convertibility, not convergence: oil exporters'
incomes rose in dollar terms, and the dollar value of everything outside the United States moved
with the currency. The recovery to the 1993 peak is in large part the yen and the European
currencies appreciating against the dollar after the Plaza Accord of 1985. None of this is the
great convergence; it is exchange-rate noise around a stable distribution. The second is the
timing of the fall. Baldwin dates the second unbundling to about 1990, but the G7 share at market
rates did not fall decisively until the early 2000s — it was still 65 per cent in 2001 and below 60
only in 2005. The lag is the time it took supply-chain industrialisation to show up in national
output measured in dollars, and it coincides with China's accession to the WTO. The third is that
the fall has stopped. Since 2013 the G7 share has moved between about 44 and 47 per cent, and the
Industrialising Six peaked at 26 per cent in 2021. At market rates the convergence has, for now, paused.

The Industrialising Six account for most of the difference. Their combined share was about 6 per
cent in 1970 and a little over 5 per cent in 1990; it reached about 16 per cent in 2010 and about a
quarter of world output by the early 2020s. Within the six, China dominates. Its GDP at market
exchange rates was about 6 per cent of the United States' in 1990 and 41 per cent in 2010; it
reached a high of about 77 per cent in 2021 and has since fallen back, to about 64 per cent in 2024,
partly because the dollar strengthened. The convergence is real, and it is not a straight line.

### Whose decline?

A G7 total hides who is in it. @fig-ch02-g7-members takes the aggregate apart, and the result
disagrees with the way the story is usually told. The usual account is about American decline: the
hegemon that wrote the rules is losing the weight to enforce them. At market exchange rates, the
data say something else. The United States produced about 36 per cent of world output in 1970, a
figure inflated by the dollar's overvaluation under the fixed parity, but about 26 per cent in 1990
and about 26 per cent in 2025. Over the period of the great convergence, the American share is
essentially unchanged. The share the G7 lost was lost by the other six. Japan's fall is the most
dramatic in the panel: from 7 per cent of world output in 1970 to a peak of about 18 per cent in
1994, at the height of the yen, and down to under 4 per cent in 2025, the combined effect of three
decades of slow growth, deflation and a weaker currency. The four large European economies together
produced about 23 per cent of world output in 1990 and about 13 per cent in 2025; Germany's share
roughly halved, France's halved, and Italy's more than halved. In 1990 the G7 outside the United States
produced about 40 per cent of world output; in 2025, about 18 per cent.

The decomposition matters for the argument of this chapter in two ways. For hegemonic stability
theory, the relevant quantity is the hegemon's share, not the club's; on that measure, at market
rates, the stabiliser of the post-1945 order has not shrunk since the end of the Cold War, while its
allies have. The order's problem is less that its leading author is weaker than that the coalition
which shared the costs of running it — the Europeans and Japan, who financed the institutions,
matched American tariff cuts and carried a large part of the votes in the Fund and the Bank — now
carries much less weight. That changes the politics of burden-sharing, which is a recurring theme of
the 2020s. For a multinational, the decomposition means that "the G7 market" is not a single
market whose relative size is declining; it is one market, the American, that has held its weight,
and several that have not. A European firm whose home market has halved as a share of world demand
faces a different problem from an American one.

The second decision is the **unit of account**. Market exchange rates are the natural measure if the
question is purchasing power on world markets — what an economy's output can buy abroad, how large
it looks to an exporter selling in dollars. But the share then moves with the dollar. When the
dollar strengthens, every other economy shrinks in dollar terms and the US share rises, without any
change in what anyone produces. Part of any year-to-year movement in the series is the exchange
rate, and the Chinese reversal after 2021 is partly that.

::: {.definition}
[**Purchasing-power parity (PPP)**]{.term} — a conversion of national output into a common unit
using the relative prices of a common basket of goods and services rather than market exchange
rates, so that an economy's output is valued at what it would cost in a reference country.
:::

The alternative is GDP at purchasing-power parity. Because many goods and services — especially
non-traded services — are cheaper in lower-income economies, converting their output at market
exchange rates understates its real volume. The difference is large. The World Bank's PPP series
begins only in 1990, which is to say at the start of the convergence, and in that year the G7's
share at PPP was already about 46 per cent — twenty points below its share at market rates. By 2025
it was about 28 per cent. On this measure China's economy passed that of the United States in 2014,
and by 2025 was about a third larger; at market rates it has never passed it.

Which measure is right depends on the decision. If a board is deciding where the purchasing power
for its *exported* product sits — how many dollars or euros a market can pay — market exchange rates
are the relevant measure. If it is deciding where real production capacity and local markets for
locally made goods are, PPP is closer. A firm that shows its board only one of the two has made a
strategic choice without stating it.

since, to a Chinese economy about 134 per cent of the American in 2025; at market rates China rose to
77 per cent of US output in 2021 and has fallen back to 63. Two respectable measures tell one story
of a power transition already completed and another of a transition that stalled. The realist
argument of Chapter 3, about whether a rising state's power is approaching that of the established
one, depends on which is used.

The table also shows why the two measures should not be averaged or blended. They answer different
questions, and the gap between them is itself information: it is a rough index of how cheap
non-traded goods and services are in an economy relative to the reference. As economies converge in
income, their price levels tend to rise towards those of richer economies and the gap narrows — so
part of what the market-rate series records as convergence over a long period is the closing of the
price gap, not only growth in volume. The PPP numbers are also revised whenever the International
Comparison Program publishes a new round of price surveys, so an analyst who quotes "China passed the United States in 2014" is quoting a particular vintage of
price data, and should say so [@worldbank_wdi].

The third decision is about **who is in the denominator**. The world total is a World Bank aggregate
that includes economies for which data are sparse or estimated, particularly in the early decades,
and not every member of the Industrialising Six is present throughout. Poland has no GDP figure in
the series before 1990: for the first twenty years of the period, the Six are in fact five. A
stacked chart of shares — G7, I6, rest of the world — therefore shows the "rest of the world"
absorbing whatever is missing from the Six, which is an artefact of the data, not a fact about the
world.

## From convergence to backlash

The convergence was, by most measures of global welfare, a remarkable success. It was also
politically destabilising in the countries whose share was shrinking, and the most useful framework
for understanding why comes from Dani Rodrik.

::: {.definition}
[**Rodrik's trilemma**]{.term} — the proposition that a country can have at most two of three
things: deep economic integration with the world economy, national sovereignty in policy-making, and
democratic politics [@rodrik2011].
:::

The logic is that deep integration requires harmonised rules — on products, on investment, on
capital flows — and those rules constrain what national governments can do. If the nation-state
keeps its sovereignty and pursues deep integration, democratic choice must be constrained:
governments adopt whatever policies markets reward, a condition Rodrik describes with Thomas
Friedman's phrase, the "golden straitjacket". If integration and democracy are both kept, the rules
must be made democratically above the state — a global federalism no one has built. If sovereignty
and democracy are kept, integration must be shallow: the Bretton Woods compromise, with managed
openness and room for national policy.

The trilemma is a reading of the history in the previous sections. Embedded liberalism was the
third option: shallow integration that left room for democracy and sovereignty. The neoliberal turn
and the WTO pushed towards deep integration — Rodrik calls it hyperglobalisation — and, for a
while, the countries concerned accepted the straitjacket. The backlash of the 2010s and 2020s —
Brexit, the tariff wars that began in 2018, the return of industrial policy, export controls,
investment screening, "de-risking" — can be read as a sequence of choices of sovereignty and
democracy over deep integration.

The economics behind the politics became clearer with evidence on the distribution of the gains.
@autor2013 showed that US local labour markets more exposed to rising Chinese import competition
after 2000 suffered larger falls in manufacturing employment and wages, and higher take-up of public
transfers, with effects that persisted. What the "China shock" literature showed was that the losers
were geographically concentrated, that adjustment was slow, and that the compensation the
embedded-liberal bargain had promised did not reach them. A bargain that is not honoured stops being
a bargain.

### Slowbalisation

The phenomenon that the press calls *slowbalisation* is visible in a single series, world trade as
a percentage of world GDP, drawn in the left panel of @fig-ch02-trade-fdi. It was about 26 per cent
in 1970 and 38 per cent in 1990, and peaked at about 60 per cent in 2008; it fell to 52 per cent in
2009 and has oscillated since between the low fifties and 62 per cent, reached in 2022, a year of
high commodity prices. The last year in the release jumps to well above any earlier value, but it
rests on reports from 138 economies, against 183 for 2022, and should be read as provisional. Whether
the flattening is a retreat from globalisation or a change in its form is contested. A retreat would
show up as firms reshoring and supply chains shortening; a change of form, as trade in goods
stagnating while poorly measured trade in services and data continues to grow. The series alone
cannot settle the question, because its denominator, GDP, has shifted towards services, which are
traded less, in exactly the period in question.

and were near 1 per cent in 2023–2025. Source: World Bank, World Development Indicators

The right panel tells a starker story, and it is the one closer to the multinational's own
decisions. Net inflows of foreign direct investment — the money firms commit to owning and
controlling operations abroad — were under 1 per cent of world GDP until the late 1980s, rose to
4.6 per cent in 2000 and 5.3 per cent in 2007, waves of cross-border mergers riding the second
unbundling, and have since fallen back. They averaged 3.3 per cent of world GDP in 2000–2007 and 1.6
per cent from 2018, with a low of 0.9 per cent in 2023: the level of the early 1990s. The series is
noisy, because it records financial flows rather than factories — the 2018 trough reflects American
multinationals repatriating foreign earnings after the 2017 US tax reform [@unctad2019], and much FDI
passes through holding companies in low-tax jurisdictions [@damgaard2024] — but the direction is not in doubt. Trade has
plateaued; the investment that builds supply chains has retreated. If slowbalisation is a change of
form, it is one in which firms keep trading through the networks they built while becoming much
more reluctant to build new ones abroad.

### The dispute mechanism

One institutional fact belongs in every firm's risk register. The WTO's Appellate Body has been
unable to hear appeals since December 2019, when the terms of its members expired and the United
States blocked the appointment of replacements. A panel ruling can now be appealed "into the void",
where no body exists to hear it, and so never become binding. For a firm, a discriminatory measure
by a trading partner is now more likely to be resolved by bargaining or retaliation between
governments than by a legal ruling — by power rather than by procedure.

## Interdependence as an instrument

The first two parts of this chapter describe an order designed to make interdependence safe. The
third must add that interdependence was never only an economic fact. Albert Hirschman, writing in
1945 about Nazi Germany's trade with south-eastern Europe, showed that trade relations could be
structured deliberately to create dependence: by trading with small partners for whom the trade was
a large share of their total, a great power could make the threat of interrupting it a source of
political influence [@hirschman1945]. The gains from trade, in his account, were also the measure
of the power the trade conferred — the cost a partner would bear if it stopped.

@farrell2019 brought the argument into the age of networks. The infrastructure of globalisation —
financial messaging, dollar clearing, internet backbones, cloud platforms — is not flat; it has
hubs, and the hubs sit disproportionately in a few jurisdictions. States that control the hubs can
use them for surveillance, observing what flows through, and for exclusion, cutting adversaries off.
Interdependence, on this reading, has been "weaponised": the very networks that the economic order
built become instruments of coercion. Chapters 8, 10 and 11 develop the argument for cyber,
sanctions and space. Its relevance here is that the order's openness and its vulnerability are the
same thing seen from two sides.

For a multinational, the practical form of weaponised interdependence is jurisdictional reach. A
firm that clears payments in dollars, stores data with a provider headquartered in one country, or
uses components containing technology controlled by another, has placed part of its operations under
that country's law, wherever the firm itself is incorporated and wherever the transaction takes
place. The order built after 1945 made it natural to route business through American banks,
platforms and technology; the same routing now carries American regulatory authority with it, and
China and the European Union have begun to build their own instruments of reach. A firm's exposure
is therefore a matter not only of where it sells and produces, but of which networks it runs through
and whose law travels along them.

## What it means for the multinational

The history and the economics lead to four implications for a firm's strategy.

**The market is no longer where the rules are written.** For most of the post-war period, the
largest markets and the rule-makers were the same countries. The convergence separated them. A
European firm now earns a growing share of its revenue in markets whose governments did not design
the WTO, did not participate in Bretton Woods, and do not feel bound by the spirit — as distinct
from the letter — of rules they joined late. The firm's legal environment and its commercial
environment have come apart.

**The supply chain is a geopolitical object.** If the second unbundling moved know-how along supply
chains, then supply chains are where governments now intervene to stop it: export controls,
outbound investment screening, restrictions on technology transfer, requirements to locate certain
stages of production in trusted jurisdictions. The question for the firm is no longer only where a
stage is cheapest, but where it is permitted, and by whom.

**Multilateral protection is weaker.** With the Appellate Body unable to hear appeals, the
guarantee that a trading partner's discriminatory measure could be challenged and removed through
procedure has weakened. Firms that relied implicitly on that guarantee — exporters into markets
with a history of trade remedies, for example — face disputes settled more often by negotiation and
retaliation. The firm's government's bargaining power has become part of the firm's risk profile.

**Measurement choices are strategy choices.** At market exchange rates the G7 still produces
nearly half of world output; at PPP, little more than a quarter; and within the G7 the American
share has held while Japan's and Europe's have shrunk. A risk analyst's first duty is to show both
measures, and the members, and say which question each answers.

Mapped onto the five channels of this book, the order's transformation runs mainly through **market
access** — the tariffs, trade remedies and bloc-based diversion of Chapter 4 — and through
**inputs**, where the know-how and components that the second unbundling dispersed are now subject
to control. But it reaches **finance and payments** too, because the dollar-centred monetary system
that survived 1971 is the platform on which financial sanctions operate.

## What the figure does not license

Baldwin's figure is a powerful image, and its power is a reason for care. It does not license four
conclusions that boards and commentators routinely draw from it.

A falling G7 share is **relative**. The G7 economies kept growing in absolute terms through the
whole period; the denominator grew faster. A falling share does not mean the G7 market shrank, and a
firm selling into the G7 did not lose customers because the line went down.

At current US dollars, the share **moves with the dollar**: a reading in any single year can say
as much about currency markets as about convergence.

Convergence is **concentrated**. Most of the gain went to a handful of economies, and above all to
China. "The South" did not converge; specific economies that joined specific supply chains did.

It shows **income**, not **rule-making power**. Whether a shrinking share of world GDP means a
shrinking ability to set the rules is a question about the relation between economic size and
political influence — the subject of Chapter 3. Hegemonic stability theory predicts that it does;
institutionalists predict that rules can outlast the share. The chart cannot settle the dispute,
because it does not measure the thing in dispute.

## A short review of the literature

::: {.lit}
The political economy of the post-war order begins with the hegemonic-stability debate.
@kindleberger1973 attributed the interwar collapse to the absence of a stabilising power;
@krasner1976 generalised the argument to the structure of trade; @keohane1984 replied that
institutions can sustain cooperation after hegemony. @ruggie1982 named the domestic bargain —
embedded liberalism — on which post-war openness rested, and @ikenberry2011 reinterpreted the
American-led order as liberal, institutional and self-binding, and therefore "easy to join and hard
to overturn". The monetary history, from Bretton Woods through the end of gold convertibility, is
told by @eichengreen2019.

The economics of the new globalisation is organised by @baldwin2016 around falling costs of moving
goods, ideas and people, with the multinational's supply chain as the conduit for the second
unbundling; @baldwin2015 map the regional structure of supply-chain trade. The distributional
consequences in the rich countries are documented most influentially by @autor2013, and their
political consequences are read through @rodrik2011's trilemma of integration, sovereignty and
democracy.

A third strand treats economic interdependence as a source of power rather than only of gains.
@hirschman1945 showed how asymmetric trade could be designed to create political dependence;
@farrell2019 extended the argument to the networked infrastructure of finance and communications,
introducing "weaponised interdependence" — the strand that Chapters 8, 10 and 11 follow.

The through-line is that every account of the order answers the same question — what holds the
rules in place — and that the answers differ chiefly in how much weight they give to the
distribution of power. The great convergence is the event that puts that weight to the test.
:::

## Whether the rules follow the income

The international economic order was written by a small number of states, at a moment when they held
most of the world's gold, most of its industry and, by the time the series begins, two-thirds of its
output. The rules they wrote were multilateral in form, and in several layers they were written with
the help of the firms that would live under them. Those rules made the second unbundling possible:
they made it cheap and safe for a multinational to move know-how across borders inside its own
production network, and the movement of know-how is what produced the convergence. The order, in
this sense, financed its own relative decline — which is not a failure of design, since an open
order was always going to spread industry to the places it reached.

What the data cannot say is whether the rules will follow the income. The G7's share of world
output has fallen from about two-thirds to well under half at market prices, and to little more
than a quarter at purchasing-power parity. The institutions the G7 built are still standing: the
Fund and the Bank still lend, the WTO's schedules still bind tariffs, the dollar still clears most
of the world's trade. But some of what made them work has already gone. The Appellate Body cannot
sit. The embedded-liberal bargain no longer holds in the countries that wrote it. And the networks
through which goods, money and data move have become instruments of the states that host their hubs.

Two readings of that evidence compete. On the hegemonic-stability reading, rules that rested on the
power of their authors will be revised as that power shrinks, and the revision is already under way
— in tariffs, in export controls, in the rival institutions that rising states have begun to build.
On the institutionalist reading, rules that are costly to abandon and useful to everyone will
outlast the share of those who wrote them, and what looks like decline is a change of form, as in
1971. The evidence of the last decade supports neither reading cleanly. The order has changed form
without collapsing; it has also lost two of the things that made it an order rather than a set of
habits.

For a multinational, the practical consequence is that the rules can no longer be treated as a
constant in the business plan. A firm that built its supply chain on the assumption of
non-discriminatory tariffs, procedural dispute settlement and neutral financial networks built it
on rules that were held in place by a particular distribution of power. A falling G7 share does not
tell the firm that its G7 customers are poorer, that emerging markets in general are richer, or
that the rules it relies on have already changed. It tells it that the economies that wrote the
rules now produce a much smaller share of the world's income than when they wrote them, and that
the question of what holds the rules in place — which the next chapter answers from the realist
side — has become a question about the firm's own operations.
