## 1. What economic diplomacy was, and what it has become

**Economic diplomacy** is the use of the instruments of foreign policy for economic ends — and of
economic instruments for foreign-policy ends. In the post-1990 order (Session 02) it was mostly the
first: negotiate trade agreements, open markets, promote exports, attract investment. Diplomats
served firms.

Three shifts have redefined it:

| Shift | What changed | Consequence for the firm |
|---|---|---|
| **Securitisation** | Trade, investment and technology are now treated as matters of national security | Firms face screening, export controls and "trusted vendor" rules from their *own* side |
| **From promotion to coercion** | The same networks used to promote exports are used to deny access (Session 08's chokepoints; Session 10's sanctions) | Home-state support can become home-state constraint overnight |
| **Digitalisation** | Diplomacy is conducted in public, on social media, aimed at domestic and foreign publics at once | Firms are drawn into public diplomatic quarrels — boycotts, hashtags, official denunciations |

## 2. Putnam: two-level games

Putnam (1988) models international negotiation as two simultaneous games:

- **Level I** — negotiators bargain with each other to reach a tentative agreement;
- **Level II** — each negotiator's domestic constituencies decide whether to **ratify** it.

The **win-set** is the set of Level I agreements that would be ratified at Level II. Three lessons:

1. **Larger win-sets make agreement more likely** — but leave a negotiator open to being pushed.
2. **A small win-set is a bargaining asset**: "my parliament will never accept that" is credible
   when it is true.
3. Negotiators play **both boards at once**: they try to shape the other side's Level II and to
   manage their own.

**For the firm.** Multinationals are Level II actors in *several* countries at once. An export
industry that lobbies for a trade deal enlarges the win-set at home; a firm exposed abroad may be a
hostage at the other side's Level II.

## 3. Bjola and Manor: the two-level game goes digital

Bjola and Manor (2018) revisit Putnam through the negotiations over the Iran nuclear agreement
(2015). Their argument: **digital diplomacy** — ministries and leaders communicating directly with
publics on social media — gives negotiators a new tool to act on Level II, **their own and the other
side's**. Governments used digital channels to build domestic support for ratification and to
reach the counterpart's public, reshaping win-sets during the negotiation itself.

Two implications for multinationals:

- **Speed.** Domestic opinion now moves within the negotiation, not after it — so the regulatory
  outcome a firm plans for can change faster.
- **Exposure.** A firm's brand can be pulled into a digital campaign by either government.

## 4. Rose: embassies as export promotion

Rose (2007) asks whether the foreign service earns its keep. He combines the number of embassies
and consulates that a set of exporting countries keep abroad with their bilateral exports, in a
gravity framework that controls for the size of and distance between partners. His finding: exports
are **higher where a country keeps more posts**, and the association survives the standard gravity
controls. He reports that an additional **consulate** is associated with a further rise in exports of
several percent — read the size off his tables, and note which kind of post matters most.

**Three objections**, which the practice round tests:

| Objection | The worry | How to probe it |
|---|---|---|
| **Reverse causality** | Countries open posts *where they already trade* | Do posts predict *future* trade growth, or only follow past trade? |
| **Omitted variables** | Large, rich, close partners get more posts *and* more trade | Divide trade by partner GDP; colour by distance or region |
| **Measurement** | A post is not a budget; an honorary consulate is not an embassy | Separate embassies from consulates |

Rose takes reverse causality seriously and argues that the evidence is consistent with posts having
an effect, but a cross-section cannot settle it. That is the catch of the day.

## 5. The firm and the state: bargaining, screening, and diplomacy by firms

**Triangular diplomacy.** Stopford and Strange (1991) argued that diplomacy is no longer only
state–state: it is also **state–firm** (governments bargain with multinationals over investment)
and **firm–firm** (alliances). Vernon's **obsolescing bargain** (1971) adds the dynamic: once a firm's
capital is sunk, its bargaining power falls.

**Investment screening** is economic diplomacy aimed *at* firms: the US Committee on Foreign
Investment (CFIUS), the EU framework regulation on FDI screening (Regulation 2019/452), and the
national-security review under the *Investment Canada Act*. Screening has widened from defence to
data, critical minerals, infrastructure and technology.

**Commercial diplomacy — the toolbox**:

| Instrument | Example of the type |
|---|---|
| Embassies and consulates | trade commissioners, market intelligence, introductions |
| Trade missions and state visits | ministers travelling with business delegations |
| Export credit and insurance | export credit agencies covering political risk |
| Investment promotion agencies | incentives, aftercare |
| Treaties | investment treaties with dispute settlement |

**When to lean on the home state** — and when that is itself a risk: in a dispute, home-state backing
helps; in a confrontation between states, being seen as an instrument of one may make the firm a
target of the other.
