## From serving firms to screening them

For most of the period covered by Chapter 2 — the decades in which the liberal economic order was
built out and then globalised — economic diplomacy had a settled meaning and a settled direction.
Diplomats negotiated trade agreements, opened markets, promoted exports and courted investors. Trade
commissioners in embassies found distributors for small exporters; ministers led delegations of
chief executives on state visits; export credit agencies insured contracts that private insurers
would not touch. The state served the firm, and the firm's success abroad was counted as the state's
success.

That meaning has not disappeared, but it has been joined by others, and the direction of travel has
partly reversed. The same governments that promote their firms abroad now screen foreign firms at
home, restrict what their own firms may sell and to whom, and draw companies into public quarrels
conducted on social media. A multinational today is still the beneficiary of economic diplomacy. It
is also, increasingly, its object and its instrument — and sometimes a diplomat in its own right.

::: {.definition}
[**Economic diplomacy**]{.term} — the use of the instruments of foreign policy for economic ends,
and of economic instruments for foreign-policy ends. The first covers trade negotiation, export and
investment promotion; the second covers sanctions, export controls, aid and investment screening
used to pursue security or political goals.
:::

::: {.definition}
[**Commercial diplomacy**]{.term} — the part of economic diplomacy that serves particular firms and
sectors: trade commissioners, market intelligence, introductions, advocacy in disputes, trade
missions, export credit and investment promotion. Its unit of account is the firm, not the economy.
:::

Three shifts have redefined the field. The first is **securitisation**: trade, investment and
technology are now treated as matters of national security, so that a firm faces screening, export
controls and "trusted vendor" rules not only from rival states but from its own side. The second is
the move **from promotion to coercion**: the networks that were built to promote exports — financial
messaging, payment systems, technology supply chains — are the same networks that states now use to
deny access, as the chokepoints of Chapter 8 showed and the sanctions of Chapter 10 will show in
detail. Home-state support can become home-state constraint overnight. The third is
**digitalisation**: diplomacy is increasingly conducted in public, on social media, addressed to
domestic and foreign audiences at once, and firms are pulled into it — through boycotts, hashtags
and official denunciations — without having chosen to be.

This chapter takes the redefinition in three steps. It begins with the theory that best captures how
international bargaining is shaped by domestic politics, Robert Putnam's two-level game, and with
the argument of Corneliu Bjola and Ilan Manor that digital diplomacy has changed how that game is
played. It then turns to the empirical question at the heart of the evidence: whether the most
traditional instrument of commercial diplomacy, the embassy, actually promotes exports, as Andrew
Rose found. Finally it turns to the relationship between states and firms directly — bargaining,
screening and the choice a firm faces about when to lean on its home state.

## Two boards at once

Putnam's article begins from a frustration with theories of international relations that treated the
state as a unitary actor, and with theories of domestic politics that ignored the international
environment [@putnam1988]. His alternative is to model an international negotiation as two games
played simultaneously.

At **Level I**, negotiators representing their governments bargain with each other to reach a
tentative agreement. At **Level II**, each negotiator's domestic constituencies — legislatures,
parties, interest groups, bureaucracies, public opinion — decide whether to ratify it. Ratification
need not be formal; any domestic process that can kill an agreement counts. An agreement survives
only if it is acceptable at both levels, on both sides.

::: {.definition}
[**Win-set**]{.term} — for a given Level II constituency, the set of possible Level I agreements
that it would ratify. An agreement is possible only where the two sides' win-sets overlap. The size
of each win-set depends on the distribution of power and preferences at home, on the domestic
institutions that govern ratification, and on the strategies of the negotiators themselves.
:::

Three lessons follow, and each has a direct application to firms.

The first is that **larger win-sets make agreement more likely**. If many possible deals would be
ratified at home, the negotiators have more room to find one that the other side can also accept.
But a large win-set also leaves a negotiator open to being pushed: the other side knows that
concessions can be extracted without endangering ratification.

The second, and the most counter-intuitive, is that **a small win-set can be a bargaining asset**. A
negotiator who can say, credibly, "my parliament will never accept that" has a reason to refuse that
the other side cannot argue away. Thomas Schelling had made a version of this point about the power
of binding oneself; Putnam located the binding in domestic politics. The credibility matters. A
constraint that is invented for bargaining purposes is a bluff, and bluffs can be called.

The third is that **negotiators play both boards at once**. A skilled negotiator does not simply
report the constraints of Level II; he or she works to shape them — building support at home for a
deal that is coming, and reaching across to the other side's Level II to make its ratification
easier. Putnam called the latter "reverberation": a move at Level I that changes the balance of
opinion in the other country's domestic politics.

Putnam's framework also explains two features of international agreements that otherwise look
irrational. The first is **involuntary defection**: an agreement that a negotiator sincerely
accepted fails at Level II because the domestic coalition behind it collapses. The second is the
role of **side-payments and issue linkage**: a negotiator who cannot win ratification on the merits
of a deal can enlarge the win-set by compensating the losers at home, or by linking the deal to a
concession on another issue that brings a new constituency into the coalition. Both have a direct
bearing on firms. The firms and sectors that stand to lose from an agreement are the ones a
government must compensate or overrule; the firms that stand to gain are the ones it mobilises. And
the domestic institutions that govern ratification — a legislature's voting rules, the role of
sub-national governments, the timing of elections — determine how large the win-set can be, which is
why a firm's analysis of a negotiation that affects it should begin with the other side's
ratification process rather than with the negotiators' public statements.

### Where the multinational sits

Draw the two-level game on a board — two negotiators facing each other at Level I, each standing on
a circle of domestic constituencies at Level II — and ask where a multinational belongs. The answer
is that it belongs in *several* circles at once. At home, an export-oriented firm or industry is one
of the constituencies whose support enlarges the win-set for a trade agreement; firms lobby, testify
and fund campaigns, and governments count their support before signing. Abroad, the same firm's
subsidiaries, plants and employees are part of the *other* country's Level II, where they may be
assets (a large employer can argue for an agreement locally) or hostages (a visible foreign firm is
a convenient target for retaliation if negotiations go badly).

The double position changes what a firm should expect from negotiations that concern it. A firm
whose home government is negotiating with a host government on its behalf is not a spectator. It is
part of the domestic politics that determine what its own negotiator can accept, and part of the
foreign domestic politics that determine what the other side can accept. The question it must ask is
which of its positions — lobbyist at home, employer abroad, symbol of its home country — the
negotiators will use, and whether it can control that use.

The renegotiation of the North American Free Trade Agreement between 2017 and 2018 shows the game at
work, with firms on every board. The United States imposed tariffs on Canadian and Mexican steel and
aluminium in June 2018, on national-security grounds, while the negotiation was under way — a Level
I move aimed squarely at the other sides' Level II, where steel and aluminium producers and their
workers would press their governments to settle. Canada's system of supply management in dairy was a
constraint at its own Level II, defended by a well-organised domestic constituency, and the eventual
agreement opened only a limited part of the dairy market. Automobile manufacturers, whose supply
chains crossed all three borders many times, were Level II actors in all three countries at once,
and the new rules of origin for vehicles were written with their production networks in view. For
each firm involved, the question was not only what the agreement would say but which domestic
coalition in which country would decide whether there was an agreement at all.

## The two-level game goes digital

Putnam wrote in 1988, about a world in which Level II was reached mainly through legislatures,
interest groups and the press. Bjola and Manor revisit his model three decades later through the
negotiations over Iran's nuclear programme, which culminated in the Joint Comprehensive Plan of
Action of July 2015 [@bjola2018]. Their argument is that **digital diplomacy** — ministries,
embassies and leaders communicating directly with publics on social media — gives negotiators a new
instrument for acting on Level II, both their own and the other side's.

The mechanics are straightforward. Before social media, a negotiator who wanted to build domestic
support for a deal worked through intermediaries: parliamentary allies, friendly editors, interest
groups. A negotiator who wanted to reach the other side's public had few channels at all. Digital
platforms remove the intermediaries. Foreign ministers and their staff can address their own public
directly, framing the negotiation as it happens, and can address the other side's public in its own
language, bypassing its government. In the Iran negotiations, Bjola and Manor show, governments used
these channels to shape how the negotiation was perceived at home and abroad, attempting to widen
the win-sets on which ratification depended while the bargaining was still going on.

Two implications matter for multinationals. The first is **speed**. Domestic opinion now moves
within the negotiation rather than after it, so that the outcome a firm plans around can change
faster, and for reasons visible to anyone who follows the right accounts. The second is
**exposure**. A firm's brand can be pulled into a digital campaign by either government — praised as
a model of cooperation, denounced as a symbol of the other side, or made the subject of a boycott
that a ministry amplifies. Neither role is chosen by the firm.

The Iran agreement also illustrates the fragility of win-sets over time, which is the deeper lesson
for planning. The agreement was concluded and ratified in the sense Putnam meant; it then lost its
domestic support in one of the countries that had made it. The United States withdrew in 2018 and
reimposed sanctions, including sanctions aimed at foreign firms that continued to do business with
Iran. European companies that had re-entered the Iranian market after 2015 found that the domestic
politics of a third country — the United States — determined whether they could stay. The two-level
game, for a multinational, is played on more boards than two.

## Do embassies sell?

The most traditional instrument of commercial diplomacy is the diplomatic post itself: the embassy,
high commission or consulate, with its commercial section, its trade commissioners and its network
of contacts. Governments spend substantial sums maintaining these networks and justify part of the
cost by their contribution to trade. Rose asked whether the justification holds [@rose2007].

His design combined, in a single recent cross-section, data on the embassies and consulates that 22
large exporting countries maintain in some 200 destinations with their bilateral exports, using the
framework that economists have used to explain bilateral trade since Jan Tinbergen first applied it
in the 1960s [@tinbergen1962].

::: {.definition}
[**Gravity model**]{.term} — the empirical relationship in which bilateral trade between two
countries rises with their economic size and falls with the distance between them, by analogy with
Newton's law of gravitation. Modern versions add measures of trade costs — common language, shared
borders, colonial ties, trade agreements — and controls for each country's overall openness to trade
with all partners.
:::

The model's empirical success is remarkable — few relationships in economics fit cross-country data
as well — and its theoretical foundations were put on a firm footing by Anderson and van Wincoop,
who showed that what matters is not only the trade cost between two partners but each partner's
trade costs with everyone else, which they called multilateral resistance [@anderson2003]. Head and
Mayer's survey is the practical guide to estimating it [@head2014]. For Rose's purposes the gravity
model is a way of asking a precise question: *holding constant the size of the partners and the
distance between them, and the other standard determinants of trade, do countries export more to
places where they keep more diplomatic posts?*

His answer was yes. Exports are higher where a country keeps more posts, and the association
survives the standard gravity controls. Bilateral exports rise by approximately six to ten per cent
for each additional consulate abroad, and the effect varies by exporter and is non-linear:
consulates have smaller effects than the creation of an embassy [@rose2007]. The size should be read
off his tables, by exporter and type of post, rather than remembered from a summary.

## Three objections

Rose's result faces three objections, and a fourth problem that lies in the data. Each is a general
problem in inferring the effect of a policy from a cross-section, and each will recur in the
evaluation of sanctions in Chapter 10.

### Reverse causality

Countries open posts where they already trade. A foreign ministry deciding where to open a new
consulate looks, among other things, at where its exporters are active and where its nationals
travel and invest. If posts follow trade, then a cross-section in which posts and trade go together
tells us that ministries are sensible, not that embassies generate exports. The two explanations
predict the same snapshot.

They predict different *dynamics*. If posts promote exports, then pairs of countries in which a post
is opened should see faster subsequent growth in exports than comparable pairs in which it is not.
If posts follow exports, then export growth should come first and the post should follow. Telling
the two apart requires data over time, and even then requires that the timing of openings not be
driven by anticipated growth — a ministry that opens a consulate because it expects a market to boom
will produce a post that "precedes" growth without causing it. Rose took the objection seriously and
argued that his evidence was consistent with posts having an effect, but a cross-section cannot
settle the question. The networks themselves do change, and the changes offer a first, rough test:
between 2017 and 2023 the major diplomatic services opened some posts and closed others, and a
relationship that holds in the cross-section should leave a trace in the trade of the pairs whose
posts changed (@fig-ch09-network-change). A trace, however, is not proof, for the reason just
given — a post opened in anticipation of growth will appear to cause it.

### Omitted variables

Large, rich and nearby partners receive more posts *and* more trade, for reasons that have nothing
to do with each other. The gravity controls are designed to absorb exactly this, but a raw scatter
of exports against posts does not include them. The simplest correction — dividing exports by the
partner's GDP — removes the most obvious confounder and shows how much of the raw relationship was
simply size. Other confounders are harder: a shared language, a large diaspora, a history of
colonial ties or a security alliance may raise both the number of posts and the volume of trade.

### Measurement

A post is not a budget. A small consulate with two staff and a large embassy with a commercial
section of fifty count the same in a tally of posts, and an honorary consulate run by a local
businessman may count too. The type of post matters, which is why embassies and consulates must be
counted separately. The direction of the measurement problem is not obvious. Consulates are often
placed in commercial cities precisely to serve trade; yet Rose found the creation of an embassy to
matter more than an additional consulate, which may say as much about what an embassy signals as
about what it does.

### The zeros

There is a fourth problem, which the data make unavoidable. The most comprehensive public record of
diplomatic networks, the Lowy Institute's Global Diplomacy Index, lists posts that exist
[@lowy2024]. A country with no post in a partner does not appear — the zero is missing, not zero —
and must be constructed. Once constructed, the zeros create a second difficulty: the logarithm of
zero is undefined, so on a chart with logarithmic axes the partners with no posts fall off the plot.
Dropping them silently changes the sample in a way that is correlated with the outcome, because the
partners with no posts are disproportionately the small and distant ones with little trade. The same
problem in the trade variable — pairs of countries that do not trade at all — led Santos Silva and
Tenreyro to argue that the standard practice of estimating gravity equations in logarithms is
biased, and to propose an estimator that keeps the zeros in [@santossilva2006]. The lesson for a
chart is simpler: say how many observations a log scale has removed, and show what the relationship
looks like with and without them.

::: {.warn}
**A trend line with a high R² and a tiny p-value does not show that embassies cause exports.** It
shows a strong association in a cross-section. The R² measures how closely the points cluster around
the line; the p-value measures how surprising the slope would be if there were no association at
all. Neither says anything about which way the causation runs, or whether a third variable drives
both.
:::

## The rest of the toolbox, and what the evidence says

Embassies are one instrument among several. The literature on the others is useful both for what it
finds and for how it finds it, because each study faces the same objections as Rose.

**State visits and trade missions.** Nitsch examined the travel of the heads of state of France,
Germany and the United States from 1948 to 2003 and found that state and official visits are
associated with exports higher by about 8 to 10 per cent; when he turned to a
difference-in-differences design to address reverse causality, the effect on export growth was
strong but short-lived [@nitsch2007]. Head and Ries studied Canada's trade missions, which during
the 1990s took the prime minister abroad with large delegations of provincial premiers and business
leaders. Their conclusion was sobering. Canada does trade more than normal with the countries to
which it sent missions, but the missions do not seem to have caused the increase: in their preferred
specification, with country-pair fixed effects, the effects are small, negative and mainly
insignificant [@head2010].

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · Team Canada, 1994]{.archive-label}

In November 1994 the Canadian prime minister, Jean Chrétien, led a delegation to China that included
most of the provincial premiers and hundreds of business representatives. The mission was branded
"Team Canada", and it became the model for a series of similar missions over the following years, to
destinations in Asia, Latin America, Europe and elsewhere. Each mission announced a large total of
contracts and agreements signed during the visit; according to the Canadian government, the missions
generated tens of billions of dollars in new business deals [@head2010].

The announced totals were the wrong evidence. Critics pointed out that many of the agreements were
memoranda of understanding, that some were deals that had been negotiated before the trip and timed
to be signed during it, and the missions were sent to markets that were already growing. Head and
Ries's later evaluation used the missions as a natural test of whether high-profile commercial
diplomacy changes trade flows, and compared Canada's trade with mission destinations before and
after the missions against the trade it would otherwise have been expected to have. They found that
the missions had small, negative and mainly insignificant effects.

The episode is a clean illustration of the reverse-causality objection to Rose. Governments direct
their most visible commercial diplomacy at the markets they expect to matter — which are, very
often, the markets that would have grown anyway. A ministry that evaluates its missions by the deals
announced during them, or by the subsequent growth of trade with the destinations, will always
conclude that the missions succeeded.
:::

**Export promotion agencies.** Lederman, Olarreaga and Payton surveyed national export promotion
agencies and found that, on average, their budgets are associated with higher exports, with
diminishing returns as budgets grow and with considerable differences in effectiveness depending on
how the agencies are organised [@lederman2010]. **The body of evidence as a whole.** Moons and van
Bergeijk assembled 32 empirical studies published between 1986 and 2011 into a meta-analysis and
found a positive and significant effect of economic diplomacy on trade and investment — with the
exception of state visits — and reported effects that are sensitive to the design of the individual
studies [@moons2017]. The fair summary is that commercial diplomacy probably helps at the margin,
that the size of its effect is hard to pin down, and that the most visible instruments are the most
vulnerable to the selection problems that inflate naive estimates.

## Triangular diplomacy

The two-level game puts firms inside the domestic politics of states. A second tradition puts them
across the table. John Stopford and Susan Strange argued that the changing structure of the world
economy had turned diplomacy into a triangle [@stopford1991; @strange1992]. Alongside the familiar
**state–state** diplomacy of governments, there is **state–firm** diplomacy, in which governments
bargain with multinationals over the terms of investment — where a plant will be built, what share
of inputs will be sourced locally, what will be exported — and **firm–firm** diplomacy, in which
companies form alliances, joint ventures and consortia that shape markets as surely as treaties do.
Governments, on this account, compete for world market shares through the firms they attract, and
firms compete partly by bargaining with governments.

The bargaining has a dynamic, and Raymond Vernon described it before Stopford and Strange formalised
the triangle [@vernon1971]. Before an investment is made, the firm holds the cards: it can choose
among countries, and each host must offer terms good enough to win. Once the investment is made —
once the mine is dug, the plant built, the pipeline laid — the firm's capital is sunk and cannot be
moved. The host's bargaining power rises and the firm's falls. Terms that were agreed when the firm
was mobile can be renegotiated when it is not.

::: {.definition}
[**Obsolescing bargain**]{.term} — the tendency for a multinational's bargaining power over a host
government to decline after its investment is sunk, so that the terms agreed at entry become
vulnerable to renegotiation, higher taxation or, in the limit, expropriation. The bargain
"obsolesces" because the conditions that made it acceptable to the host no longer hold.
:::

::: {.archive}
[From the archive · The nationalisation of Chilean copper, 1971]{.archive-label}

In the 1960s the large copper mines that dominated Chile's exports were owned by two American
companies, Anaconda and Kennecott. The terms on which they operated had been renegotiated more than
once during the decade, in each case with the Chilean state taking a larger share — first through
taxation, then through partial state ownership under the "Chileanisation" policy of President
Eduardo Frei. In July 1971, under President Salvador Allende, the Chilean Congress unanimously
approved a constitutional amendment nationalising the large copper mines. The compensation formula
allowed the government to deduct "excess profits" earned in previous years, and the deductions
largely eliminated what the companies would otherwise have been owed.

The episode is the textbook case of the obsolescing bargain, and it was a geopolitical event as much
as a commercial one. The companies' home government treated the nationalisation as a matter of
foreign policy, and the dispute became entangled with the wider hostility of the United States to
Allende's government in the years before the coup of 1973. Kobrin's study of expropriations across
developing countries from 1960 to 1979 placed Chile's in a wider wave, in which natural resources —
where capital is most sunk — were prominent [@kobrin1984]. For a firm, the lesson has two halves:
sunk capital weakens bargaining power over time, and a dispute over that capital can quickly stop
being a dispute between a firm and a host and become one between two states.
:::

## Screening: diplomacy aimed at firms

The newest element of economic diplomacy reverses the direction of the oldest. Commercial diplomacy
helps a state's firms abroad; **investment screening** examines foreign firms at home, and blocks or
conditions their acquisitions on grounds of national security.

The United States has had a screening body, the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States
(CFIUS), since the 1970s, but its reach and visibility have grown sharply. In 2006 the acquisition
by Dubai Ports World of a British company that operated terminals at several American ports,
approved under the existing procedures, provoked a political storm in Congress; the company agreed
to divest the American operations, and the following year Congress tightened the law. A further
reform in 2018 widened the committee's jurisdiction to non-controlling investments in businesses
involving critical technologies, critical infrastructure and sensitive personal data. The European
Union adopted a framework regulation for the screening of foreign direct investment in 2019
(Regulation 2019/452), which created a mechanism for member states and the Commission to share
information and comment on each other's cases. Canada added a national-security review to the
*Investment Canada Act* in 2009, and has since used it in a widening range of sectors.

The widening is the important part. Screening began as a defence matter — who may own a supplier of
military equipment. It now reaches data, critical minerals, infrastructure, semiconductors and
artificial intelligence, which is to say much of the modern economy. For a multinational this is
geopolitical risk through the **market access** and **assets** channels at once: an acquisition can
be blocked, a completed one unwound, and a firm's ownership structure — who its shareholders are,
where its parent is incorporated — becomes a determinant of what it may buy. It is also, in Putnam's
terms, a Level II phenomenon: screening decisions are made by bodies that answer to domestic
politics, and they are subject to the same reverberation as any other part of a negotiation between
states.

## When to lean on the home state

Commercial diplomacy is not only promotion. When a firm's investment is threatened by a host
government — an expropriation, a cancelled licence, a discriminatory regulation — its home state's
embassy is one of the places it turns. Gertz, studying the commercial work of American embassies,
showed that diplomats routinely intervene on behalf of their firms in disputes with host
governments, and argued that commercial diplomacy is therefore in part an instrument of
political-risk management: a way of raising the cost to a host of mistreating a foreign investor
[@gertz2018].

That protection has a price, and the price has risen with the redefinition this chapter describes.
In a dispute that remains commercial, home-state backing helps: the host weighs the diplomatic cost
of mistreatment against the gain. In a confrontation between states, being seen as an instrument of
one of them can make the firm a target of the other. A firm that accepts its ambassador's offer to
raise its mining concession with a host president has gained an advocate and has also, in the host's
eyes, become part of its home state's foreign policy. If relations between the two states
deteriorate, that association will not be forgotten. Blackwill and Harris describe a world in which
states use economic instruments systematically for geopolitical ends [@blackwill2016]; in such a
world, the line between being protected by one's home state and being used by it is thin.

Canada's experience after December 2018 shows how quickly a firm can move from beneficiary to
hostage. When Canadian authorities arrested a senior executive of Huawei in Vancouver at the request
of the United States, China detained two Canadian citizens and, in March 2019, suspended the export
permits of major Canadian canola exporters, citing quality concerns. The exporters had done nothing
in the dispute; they were simply the most visible Canadian commercial interest in China that could
be squeezed without large cost to China itself. Their home state could protest, and did, but it
could not restore their market access without conceding on an issue that was not theirs. In Putnam's
terms, they had become instruments for acting on Canada's Level II — a constituency whose losses
were meant to raise the domestic cost of the government's position.

The decision can be framed with the five channels used throughout this book. Leaning on the home
state may protect **market access** and **assets** in a dispute, but it may endanger **market
access** in a rival's market, **inputs** sourced from a rival, **finance** from banks that fear
sanctions, and the safety of **people** posted in a country where the firm has become a symbol. And
it may draw the firm into the **data** demands that come with being seen as part of a state's
infrastructure. The question is not whether a firm should ever accept help. It is whether the firm
has thought about what the help will mean if relations between the two states change — and whether
it is prepared to be, in the eyes of its host, its government's instrument.

## What the diplomatic networks show

The Lowy Institute's index records the networks of 66 countries and territories — G20, OECD and
Asian states, with data collected up to November 2023 — at the level of the individual post. Each of
its roughly 7,700 entries for 2023 is one post, with the sending country, the host country, the city
and the type of post, which distinguishes embassies and high commissions from consulates, permanent
missions and other offices. Earlier releases allow the 2023 networks to be compared with those of
2017, which cover 60 senders.

The headline the index made famous is a change at the top. In 2017 the United States kept the
largest diplomatic network in the record, with 274 posts against China's 271. By 2023 the order had
reversed: China kept 274 and the United States 271, with Türkiye, Japan and France following at
around 250 each [@lowy2024]. The difference at the top is small, and a tally of posts says nothing
about the size of the staff in them or the quality of their work. But the composition is revealing.
China's lead rests on its consulates — 91 against the United States' 83 in 2023 — which are the
posts most directly concerned with trade, investment and nationals abroad, while the United States
keeps more permanent missions to international organisations. A state's diplomatic network is a
revealed preference: it shows where a foreign ministry has chosen to spend scarce money, and the
shift at the top is a shift in where two great powers expect their commercial and political
interests to lie.

Across all the senders the index covers in both years, the networks grew rather than shrank: the 60
foreign services present in 2017 kept 85 more posts in 2023, and 33 of them grew while 21 shrank
(@fig-ch09-network-change). That pattern sits awkwardly with any simple story of diplomatic
retrenchment, and it is the raw material for the dynamic test of Rose's result: if posts promote
exports, the pairs in which Türkiye, Ireland or the United Kingdom opened posts after 2017 should
show faster export growth than comparable pairs in which they did not, and those in which Spain or
France closed posts slower growth. Six years and a pandemic are a short and noisy window, and the
selection problem applies to closures as much as to openings: a ministry closes a consulate where it
expects little.

Three features of the data carry the arguments of this chapter into any test of Rose's result. The
first is that the record is at the wrong level: the gravity question is about pairs of countries,
and the record is about posts. Turning posts into a count per pair is a reduction in Krippendorff's
sense, and like every reduction it discards information — the city, the type of post — that the
analysis may later need. The second is the missing zeros: the pairs in which a country keeps no post
must be built from the list of partners in the trade data, and without them the analysis can only
compare partners with one post against partners with several, which is not Rose's question. The
third is the exports themselves. The IMF's bilateral trade statistics record each pair's trade
twice, as one country's exports and the other's imports, and the two often disagree; using the
exporter's own report is a choice, and scaling it by the partner's GDP is the simplest available
correction for the most obvious omitted variable.

The type of post deserves a moment of its own. Embassies and high commissions are the standard
bilateral posts; consulates and consulates-general are usually in commercial or populous cities
other than the capital, and are the posts most directly concerned with trade and with citizens
abroad. Permanent missions are a different thing altogether: a mission to the United Nations in New
York or Geneva, or to an international organisation elsewhere, is located in the host country but is
accredited to the organisation, not to the host. Counting it as a bilateral post would credit
Switzerland or the United States with diplomatic attention that is really directed at multilateral
bodies. Deciding which types to count is a measurement decision of exactly the kind Chapter 7 asked
to be made explicitly.

A humbler feature matters as much: the index identifies countries by name only, with inconsistent
spellings, while trade statistics use codes, so every link between the two depends on a concordance
built by hand — and an error there changes the slope more than any substantive objection.

### Six diplomatic services, one test

The choices can be made concrete for one exporter and then repeated for others. Take Canada's
embassies and consulates in 2023, leaving aside permanent missions and other offices; count them for
every partner; and set them against Canada's exports of goods to that partner in 2023, as reported
by Canada to the IMF, and the partner's GDP. Of the 188 partners with both trade and GDP data, 93
have no Canadian embassy or consulate at all — half the sample that a logarithmic axis would drop
without comment. Together they take 1.2 per cent of Canada's exports to the 188.

The raw relationship is as steep as anyone could want (@fig-ch09-canada-posts-exports). The median
partner with no Canadian post buys about \$11 million of Canadian goods a year; with one post, \$187
million; with two, \$906 million; with three, \$5.2 billion; and the three partners with four or
more, \$22.6 billion — the United States alone hosts sixteen Canadian posts. Scale exports by the
partner's GDP and the staircase almost disappears. The median share of the partner's GDP that it
spends on Canadian goods is 0.085 per cent where Canada has no post, and between 0.12 and 0.14 per
cent wherever it has one, two, three, or four and more. Most of the slope in the left panel is the
size of the partner.

partner's GDP cuts the coefficient to between a fifth and a third of its naive value for four of the
six senders; for Germany it disappears, and only Australia's keeps two-thirds of its size. Scaling
exports by GDP instead leaves an R² of 0.04 or less everywhere. The last two columns keep every

None of these regressions includes distance, a common language, a shared border or the
multilateral-resistance terms that @anderson2003 showed to be necessary, and none addresses reverse
causality. They are not estimates of the effect of a post, and they are not meant to be. What they
show is that the sign and the size of the association depend on choices about the sample, the
scaling and the estimator that a chart on logarithmic axes does not display — and that across six
of the world's largest diplomatic services, the only robust feature of the numbers is how much they
move.

## A short review of the literature

::: {.lit}
The theory of how domestic politics shapes international bargaining is @putnam1988, whose two-level
game and win-set remain the standard vocabulary; @bjola2018 extend it to digital diplomacy through
the Iran nuclear negotiations. On the relationship between states and firms, @vernon1971 introduced
the dynamic later called the obsolescing bargain, @kobrin1984 documented the wave of expropriations
that gave it empirical content, and @stopford1991 and @strange1992 recast diplomacy as a triangle of
state–state, state–firm and firm–firm relations. @blackwill2016 describe the return of geoeconomics
— the systematic use of economic instruments for geopolitical ends — that has redefined the field.

The empirical evaluation of commercial diplomacy rests on the gravity model, introduced by
@tinbergen1962 and given its modern theoretical foundation by @anderson2003; @head2014 is the
practical guide and @santossilva2006 the warning about logarithms and zeros. @rose2007 is the
central study of diplomatic posts and exports. @nitsch2007 examines state visits, @head2010 Canada's
trade missions, @lederman2010 export promotion agencies, and @moons2017 synthesises the econometric
evidence in a meta-analysis, and @lowy2024 is the most comprehensive public record of diplomatic
networks. @gertz2018 shows commercial diplomacy at work in investment disputes, connecting the
promotion of firms abroad to the management of political risk.
:::

## What the evidence does not license

Set a country's exports to each partner against the number of posts it keeps there, on logarithmic
axes, and the cloud of points slopes upwards with a respectable fit — for each of the six services
in @tbl-ch09-elasticities. The temptation is to read it as Rose's finding confirmed: posts promote
exports.

The association does not license that conclusion, for a multinational or for a foreign ministry. It
does not show that opening a post in a market will raise exports there, because posts are opened
where exports are expected, and the snapshot cannot tell the two directions apart. It does not show
that the relationship is about diplomacy at all until the size of the partner has been removed, and
it may show something quite different once embassies and consulates are separated. And if the zeros
have been dropped — silently, by a logarithmic axis — it does not show the relationship for the
partners where the question is most interesting: the markets in which a country has no presence yet.
The studies reviewed above point the same way. The more carefully they deal with selection, the
smaller and shorter-lived the effect of commercial diplomacy tends to become, and the most visible
instruments — the state visit, the trade mission — fare worst.

For a firm, the more useful reading is the reverse of the naive one. The density of a home state's
diplomatic network in a market is a measure of how much that state already cares about the market —
a measure of the political attention a firm can expect when it needs help, and of the political
attention it will attract when relations sour. The canola exporters of 2019 operated in a market
where their government kept an embassy and several consulates; the network did not protect them, and
their visibility as Canada's commercial presence in China is what made them useful to squeeze.

The redefinition of economic diplomacy with which this chapter began changes what a firm should ask
of its home state. The old question was how much help was available. The new one is what the help
will cost if the two states fall out — and the answer depends on the two-level game, the screening
regime and the rivalry in which the firm's markets sit. In each market that matters, a firm is some
combination of its home state's client, instrument and hostage. Knowing the proportions, and how
fast they could change, is the part of geopolitical risk that no gravity equation will estimate for
it.
