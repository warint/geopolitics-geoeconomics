## 1. Why would states build institutions — and why should a firm care?

Session 03 left us with a system without a government above states. Session 04 showed trade
reorganising itself along geopolitical lines. Yet the same system is dense with institutions — and
the ones that matter most to a multinational's day-to-day business are **economic**: they lend,
guarantee, insure, procure and arbitrate.

| | **Liberal institutionalism** | **Realism** |
|---|---|---|
| **Key text** | Keohane (1984), *After Hegemony* | Mearsheimer (1994/95), *The False Promise of International Institutions* |
| **Why institutions exist** | They lower the **transaction costs** of cooperation, provide **information**, and lengthen the **shadow of the future** so that states can make credible commitments | They reflect the **distribution of power**; the strong build them to serve their interests |
| **Do they constrain states?** | Yes, at the margin — reneging becomes costly and cooperation valuable | Rarely — when interests and rules collide, power wins |
| **What it predicts for a firm** | Rules are a reasonably stable floor: a loan covenant, an insurance contract, an arbitral award behave predictably | Rules are only as stable as the coalition behind them; read the shareholders, not the charter |

Neither is a strawman. For a firm the useful question is **which institution, on which issue,
behaves like which** — an empirical question. Two facts make it pressing today:

- **The old institutions are business infrastructure.** The World Bank Group, the regional
  development banks and their private-sector arms are among the largest sources of long-term
  finance, guarantees, political risk insurance and public contracts in emerging markets.
- **New institutions have been built beside them** — the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, the
  BRICS New Development Bank, regional reserve pools in Asia and among the BRICS, China's policy
  banks. Whether they **complement** the Bretton Woods order or **contest** it is the geopolitical
  question of the session.

Morse and Keohane (2014) call this **contested multilateralism**: states unhappy with an
institution either shift the issue to another existing institution (*regime shifting*) or build a
new one (*competitive regime creation*). The AIIB is their textbook case of the second.

## 2. The Bretton Woods core: money, trade and the rules around them

**The IMF** (Articles adopted at Bretton Woods, 22 July 1944; in force December 1945) is the
lender to **governments** in balance-of-payments trouble. It never lends to firms — but its
programmes set the conditions firms work in: exchange-rate regimes, capital controls, subsidy
cuts, fiscal targets.

| IMF | |
|---|---|
| **Members** | 191 |
| **Total quotas** | SDR 476 billion; SDR 715.7 billion once the 16th General Review takes effect (as of 30 April 2026) |
| **Voting power** (as of 30 April 2026) | United States 16.49% · Japan 6.14% · China 6.08% · India ≈2.63% · Russia ≈2.59% · Brazil ≈2.22% · South Africa ≈0.63% (shares computed from the vote counts in Appendix IV of the 2026 Annual Report) |
| **The key rule** | An **85% majority** of total voting power is needed for any change in quotas (Article III, Section 2(c)) — so a member with more than 15% can block it alone: today, only the United States |
| **Reform** | The 16th Review (a 50% quota increase, approved 15 December 2023) is **not yet effective**; the consent deadline was extended to 15 November 2026. It increases quotas **equiproportionally** — no realignment of shares. The realignment is left to the 17th Review, guided by the "Diriyah Guiding Principles" endorsed in April 2026; no new formula is agreed |

**Why it matters for the session's question.** China's economy is far larger relative to the
United States' than a 6.08% vs 16.49% vote suggests. **Votes lag economic weight** — and the
failure to realign them is the standard explanation for why emerging economies built their own
institutions (section 6).

**The WTO** (1 January 1995; 166 members) sets the tariff bindings and non-discrimination rules a
firm's market access rests on. Its Appellate Body has had **no members since 30 November 2020** and
cannot hear appeals; a subset of members (62 counting the EU and its 27 member states, per the
European Commission, September 2026) use the **Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement
(MPIA)** instead. The rule exists; the enforcement depends on who has opted in.

**The OECD** (38 members) matters to firms through three instruments: the **Arrangement on
Officially Supported Export Credits** (1978 — it caps the terms that participants' export-credit
agencies may offer, so that they compete on quality, not subsidy; China is not a participant); the
**Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises** (1976, updated 2023 — complaints against firms go to
National Contact Points for Responsible Business Conduct); and the **Pillar Two** global minimum tax
(15% on groups with consolidated revenue of at least €750 million, agreed in the OECD/G20 Inclusive
Framework).

## 3. The World Bank Group as a business toolkit

Five institutions, five things a firm can use.

| Institution | Founded | Members | What it does | What it offers a firm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IBRD** | 1944 (Bretton Woods) | 189 | Lends to middle-income and creditworthy governments | **Contracts**: the projects it finances buy goods, works and services through international competitive procurement |
| **IDA** | 1960 | 175 | Grants and concessional credits to the poorest countries | Same: **procurement** opportunities |
| **IFC** | 1956 | 186 | Invests in the **private sector**: loans (in 88 local currencies), equity (typically stakes up to 20%), guarantees, mobilisation | **Co-investment and finance** for a firm's own project; IFC's presence is also a signal to other lenders and to the host state |
| **MIGA** | 1988 | 182 | **Political risk insurance** and credit enhancement | Cover against **currency inconvertibility and transfer restriction, expropriation, war and civil disturbance, breach of contract, non-honouring of sovereign financial obligations** |
| **ICSID** | Convention in force 14 October 1966 | 159 contracting states (as of 16 August 2026) | Administers **investor–state arbitration** and conciliation | A neutral forum if the host state expropriates or breaches a treaty |

Sources: World Bank Group members page; MIGA and ICSID official pages (accessed 16 October 2026).

**The numbers that show the scale** (latest fiscal years, which end on 30 June):

- **IFC**, FY2026: US$32.0 billion committed on its own account, and US$73.6 billion of private
  capital mobilised. The **Managed Co-Lending Portfolio Program (MCPP)** lets institutional
  investors — insurers, pension funds — lend alongside IFC in a portfolio that mirrors its own:
  US$25.2 billion pledged by 24 investors at 30 June 2026.
- **MIGA**, FY2026: a record US$10.6 billion of guarantees issued for 60 projects; gross exposure
  US$41.4 billion. Since 1 July 2024 the **World Bank Group Guarantee Platform**, housed at MIGA,
  brings the guarantee products of IBRD/IDA, IFC and MIGA under one roof (target: US$20 billion a
  year by 2030).
- **ICSID**: 1,118 cases registered in total, a record 363 administered in FY2026. New cases in
  FY2026: oil and gas 25%, mining 18%; 62% brought under bilateral investment treaties.

**Procurement — the overlooked channel.** Most of the money a development bank lends to a
government is spent buying from firms. The World Bank's **Procurement Framework** (in force since
1 July 2016) sets the rules; notices are published on **UN Development Business** and the World
Bank's site. The other side of the coin is the **sanctions system**: firms found to have engaged in
fraud or corruption are **debarred** — and under the **Agreement for Mutual Enforcement of
Debarment Decisions** (9 April 2010), a debarment of more than a year by the World Bank Group, the
ADB, the AfDB, the EBRD or the IDB Group is enforced by the other four. More than 1,500 firms and
individuals have been cross-debarred.

**Who holds the votes.** IBRD voting power (as of 30 June 2026): United States 15.98%, Japan 6.88%,
China 5.75%. MIGA: United States 14.81%. The insurer that protects a firm against a state is
itself governed by states.

**When the forum itself is contested.** Bolivia (2007), Ecuador (2009 — it rejoined in 2021),
Venezuela (2012) and Honduras (notice in 2024; it rejoined in 2026) denounced the ICSID Convention.
Investment protection is a rule that a host state can leave.

## 4. Does influence inside an institution have a price? Kuziemko and Werker (2006)

Before turning to the new institutions, one question disciplines everything else: if institutions
are arenas where states trade influence, **can we see the trade in the data?** The cleanest test is
at the UN Security Council — which is also the institution whose Chapter VII resolutions bind every
firm in every UN member state (Session 10).

**The Council in five facts.**

1. **Fifteen members**: five permanent (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, the United
   States) and ten elected. Before 1966 there were six elected members.
2. **Elected members** serve **two-year terms**, five elected each year by the General Assembly, by
   regional group, with a two-thirds majority; they cannot be immediately re-elected.
3. **Decisions** on substantive matters need nine votes and **no veto** from a permanent member.
4. Under **Chapter VII** the Council can impose measures — sanctions, arms embargoes, the use of
   force — that **bind every UN member state**.
5. An elected member's vote **can be pivotal**: the P5 need nine votes, so they must court elected
   members.

**The question.** Do powerful states pay for the votes of elected Security Council members?

**The identification idea.** Countries do not choose *when* they rotate onto the Council in any way
that tracks their need for aid — rotation follows regional queues and norms. So the authors compare
a country **with itself** in the years before, during and after its term. If aid rises during the
term and falls back afterwards, the seat — not the country's poverty, alliances or politics — is the
likeliest explanation.

**The findings**, in the paper's own summary:

- US aid to a developing country rises substantially — by about 59% in the abstract's headline
  estimate — when it rotates onto the Council, and aid through the UN rises too, more modestly.
- The increase is concentrated in the **term years** and disappears after the term.
- The effect is **larger in years when the Council matters more** — measured by how much the press
  covered it.

**Why that last result matters.** It is the difference between a statistical curiosity and a
mechanism. That aid tracks the **salience** of Council business is what makes "vote buying" the most
plausible reading.

**From the Council to the banks.** The follow-up literature finds the same pattern inside the
economic institutions of section 3: temporary Council members receive more **World Bank** projects
(Dreher, Sturm & Vreeland, 2009a) and **IMF** programmes with fewer conditions (Dreher, Sturm &
Vreeland, 2009b); Vreeland and Dreher (2014) bring the evidence together. Shareholders' preferences
travel into lending decisions — the World Bank's disbursements respond to the borrower's alignment
with the United States (Kilby, 2009). This is why the voting tables in sections 2, 3 and 7 are not
trivia: **who holds the votes shapes who gets the money — and so where the contracts are.**

## 5. The regional development banks

The regional banks replicate the World Bank model for a region, with a different shareholder
balance — and each has a **private-sector window**, a **trade-finance programme** and a
**procurement** market a firm can use.

| Bank | Founded · HQ | Members | Largest shareholders (voting or capital) | Private-sector and trade tools | Procurement eligibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ADB** | 1966 · Manila | 69 | Japan 15.6%, United States 15.6%, China 6.4%, India 6.3% (shares, 31 Dec 2025) | Non-sovereign loans, equity, guarantees; **Trade and Supply Chain Finance Program** | Firms and goods from **member countries** |
| **AfDB Group** | 1964 · Abidjan | 81 (54 regional) | Nigeria 8.8%, United States 6.6%, Egypt 6.4%, Japan 5.5% (votes, 31 Oct 2025) | Private-sector loans and equity; **Trade Finance Program** (risk participation, lines of credit) | Member countries for AfDB resources; **open to all** for the African Development Fund |
| **IDB Group** (IDB, IDB Invest) | 1959 · Washington, DC | 48 | United States ≈30% of IDB voting power | **IDB Invest** (private-sector arm, operating since 1986): loans, equity, guarantees, **Trade Finance Facilitation Program**; capital increase of US$3.5 billion (2024) with an **"originate-to-share"** model that sells on loans to private investors | **Member countries** only |
| **EBRD** | 1991 · London | 77 countries + EU + EIB | United States 10.1%; France, Germany, Italy, Japan, UK 8.6% each (capital, 31 Dec 2025) | 75% of 2025 investment was private sector; **Trade Facilitation Programme** | **Open to all countries** |
| **EIB** | 1958 · Luxembourg | The 27 EU member states | Germany, France, Italy 18.8% each (capital) | Loans, equity, venture debt, guarantees; EIB Group signed €100 billion in 2025 | Open to all countries for own-resources projects |
| **IsDB Group** | 1975 (operations) · Jeddah | 57 (OIC members) | Saudi Arabia 22.5% (end 2024) | **ICD** (private sector, Shariah-compliant finance), **ITFC** (trade finance), **ICIEC** (export-credit and political risk insurance) | Mainly **member countries** |

Sources: each bank's information statement, annual report or governance page (accessed 16 October
2026). Two sub-regional banks complete the map: **CAF** (Development Bank of Latin America and the
Caribbean, 1968, Caracas; owned mainly by Latin American states — Peru and Colombia 16.7% each of
subscribed capital, August 2025) and the **Eurasian Development Bank** (2006, Almaty; Russia,
Kazakhstan and five other post-Soviet states — Uzbekistan joined in 2025; Russia's share was cut
below half in December 2022).

**Three geopolitical signals in this table.**

- **Procurement eligibility is foreign policy.** Whether a Canadian, Chinese or Turkish firm may bid
  depends on whether its home state is a shareholder.
- **The EBRD suspended Russia's and Belarus's access** to its resources on 4 April 2022 — both
  remain shareholders, with no access. A development bank can be an instrument of sanctions.
- **Shareholding is moving.** The EBRD has expanded to sub-Saharan Africa (Benin, Côte d'Ivoire and
  Nigeria became recipient countries in 2025); the ADB amended its Charter for the first time
  (in force 1 March 2026) to lend more on the same capital.

## 6. The new institutions — from Asia and the BRICS

| Institution | Founded | Members / parties | Capital or size | Governance | Business relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB)** | Articles signed 29 June 2015; in force 25 December 2015; opened 16 January 2016 · Beijing | 111 approved members (106 full) — **not** the United States or Japan; the UK announced it would join in March 2015 | Authorised US$100 billion | **China 26.37% of votes** (30.5% of subscriptions); key decisions need ¾ of votes → **China can block them**. India 7.53%, Russia 5.92%, Germany 4.11%. Non-resident board | Infrastructure loans, including **non-sovereign** loans in local currencies; co-financing with the World Bank and ADB from 2016; its own procurement and Environmental and Social Framework |
| **New Development Bank (NDB)** | Agreement signed Fortaleza, 15 July 2014; in force July 2015 · Shanghai | 5 founders + Bangladesh, UAE, Egypt, Algeria, Uzbekistan (2026); Uruguay, Colombia, Ethiopia, Angola, Zimbabwe, Belarus admitted, not yet full members | Authorised US$100 billion; initial US$50 billion subscribed **equally** by the five founders | **Equal votes** among founders; founders keep at least 55% of votes; non-borrowing members at most 20% | Target of **30% of financing in local currencies** (2022–2026 strategy); 30% non-sovereign |
| **BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA)** | Treaty signed 15 July 2014 | The five founding BRICS | US$100 billion of commitments: China 41, Brazil, India, Russia 18 each, South Africa 5 | Only **30%** of a member's access is available without an IMF programme | A balance-of-payments backstop — it shapes the host-country risk a firm faces, not its own finance |
| **Chiang Mai Initiative Multilateralisation (CMIM)** + **AMRO** | CMI 2000; CMIM in force 24 March 2010; AMRO 2011, an international organisation since February 2016 · Singapore | ASEAN+3 (ASEAN, China, Japan, Korea) | US$240 billion of currency-swap commitments | **40%** IMF-delinked since 2021; a Rapid Financing Facility agreed in 2025, not yet in force (May 2026); paid-in capital still under discussion | Regional crisis insurance; AMRO surveillance reports are a free country-risk source |
| **China's policy banks and funds** | China Development Bank and Export-Import Bank of China (both 1994); **Silk Road Fund** (December 2014, US$40 billion + RMB 100 billion added in 2017) | Bilateral — the **Belt and Road Initiative** (announced 2013; cooperation documents with more than 150 countries, October 2023) | AidData: US$1.34 trillion of Chinese development finance, 2000–2021, in 165 countries (dataset v3.0) | State-owned; terms set bilaterally; much lending collateralised or confidential (Horn, Reinhart & Trebesch, 2021) | Contracts often go to Chinese firms; co-investment opportunities for others are case by case |

**BRICS itself is not an institution with a secretariat** — it is a summit club with two treaty
institutions (the NDB and the CRA). Its expansion still matters for the alignment map:

- **Members**: Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa; **Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, the United Arab
  Emirates** from 1 January 2024; **Indonesia** from 6 January 2025. **Saudi Arabia** is listed by
  the BRICS chairs but has not formally confirmed its membership — treat its status as ambiguous.
- **Partner countries** (a category created at the Kazan summit, October 2024): Belarus, Bolivia,
  Kazakhstan, Cuba, Malaysia, Thailand, Uganda, Uzbekistan (from 1 January 2025), Nigeria (January
  2025), Vietnam (June 2025).
- **2026**: India chaired; the New Delhi summit (12–13 September 2026) admitted no new members or
  partners and asked the BRICS payment task force to keep working on interoperable cross-border
  payments and local-currency settlement — work, not a launched system.

## 7. Complement or counter-hegemony?

Read the evidence on four dimensions.

| Dimension | The case for **complement** | The case for **counter-hegemony** |
|---|---|---|
| **Votes vs capital** | The AIIB and the NDB give emerging economies the voice the IMF's 16th Review did not | China's AIIB veto mirrors the US IMF veto; the NDB's equal-shares rule locks in the founders |
| **Standards** | The AIIB signed co-financing frameworks with the World Bank (April 2016) and the ADB (May 2016) and co-financed many of its first projects with them | Bilateral Chinese lending sits outside the OECD export-credit Arrangement and the cross-debarment agreement; terms are often confidential |
| **Currency** | Local-currency lending reduces currency mismatch for borrowers | Local-currency settlement and BRICS payment work aim to reduce dependence on the dollar — and on the sanctions it carries |
| **Sanctions** | **The test of 2022**: the AIIB put all Russia- and Belarus-related activities on hold (3 March 2022); the NDB put new transactions in Russia on hold the same week — both depend on dollar funding markets | The IMF-delinked shares of the CRA and the CMIM give members crisis money **outside** the IMF; BRICS payment work aims at settlement **outside** Western financial chokepoints (Session 10) |

The literature's verdict so far is **both**: the new banks have borrowed much of the old ones'
operating model (Wang, 2017; Humphrey, 2022), while giving their founders an **outside option** that
strengthens their hand inside the old ones (Morse & Keohane, 2014; Ikenberry & Lim, 2017). For a
firm, the outside option is the point: the more institutions there are, the more **forum shopping**
a host state can do — and the less any one institution's conditions bind it.

## 8. What it means for the firm

**Four business channels**, old and new institutions side by side:

| Channel | Old institutions | New institutions | What the firm does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Procurement** | World Bank (UNDB, STEP), ADB, AfDB, IDB, EBRD, EIB | AIIB, NDB project procurement; Chinese-financed projects | Track pipelines and procurement notices; check **eligibility by nationality** |
| **Finance and co-investment** | IFC, IDB Invest, EBRD, ADB and AfDB private-sector windows, MCPP | AIIB and NDB non-sovereign loans, often in local currency | Bring a development bank into the capital structure: it lowers cost and signals to the host state |
| **Insurance and guarantees** | MIGA and the WBG Guarantee Platform; ICIEC; trade-finance programmes | Chinese export-credit insurance (for Chinese firms) | Insure transfer, expropriation, war, breach-of-contract risk |
| **Dispute settlement** | ICSID; bilateral investment treaties | None comparable yet | Structure the investment through a treaty-protected jurisdiction |

**Five geopolitical risks that come with them:**

1. **Political conditionality** — IMF and bank programmes change the host's policy mix; the
   "price of a seat" literature shows lending also follows politics.
2. **Sanctions exposure** — an institution can cut off a member (EBRD 2022), freeze a country
   (AIIB, NDB 2022), or debar a firm everywhere at once (cross-debarment).
3. **Alignment** — BRICS membership, AIIB or EBRD shareholding, and Belt and Road memoranda signal
   which way a host state leans — useful for the course's alignment indicators (Session 04).
4. **Competing standards** — environmental and social safeguards, procurement rules and
   transparency differ across lenders; a project financed by one may not be bankable by another.
5. **Exit from the forum** — host states can denounce ICSID; members can leave or be suspended.

**The lesson.** An institution is a risk **mitigator** when the coalition that sustains it is
stable, and a risk **source** when it is not. A risk dashboard should therefore track **the
coalition**, not only the rule: who holds the votes, who sits on the Council, what they are being
offered — which is exactly what today's practice round measures for the Security Council.

## 9. The Tableau skill of the day — LOD expressions

The practice needs two numbers for every country-year that do not exist in either file:

**Years relative to a term.** Join the term table (one row per term) to the aid table (one row per
country-year) on the country code — a **join** in the physical layer, not a relationship, because a
row-level calculation cannot mix fields from two related tables. Each country-year now appears once
per term of that country, which is exactly what an event study needs:

```text
[Event time]   =  [Year] - [Term start]
```

**The country's own pre-term baseline**, a *Level of Detail* expression that fixes the computation at
the country × term level whatever is in the view:

```text
[Baseline aid] = { FIXED [Iso3], [Term start] :
                   AVG( IF [Event time] >= -3 AND [Event time] <= -1 THEN [Aid] END ) }

[Aid relative to baseline] = [Aid] / [Baseline aid]      // row level: one value per country-year-term
```

`FIXED` computes before the view's dimension filters (context filters apply first) and ignores what
is on Rows and Columns: every row of a term carries that term's baseline. The ratio is then computed
row by row, and averaging it at each event year gives the **average across terms** of a ratio
computed **within** each term.
