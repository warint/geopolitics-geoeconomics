## 1. Infrastructure in orbit

Few multinationals own a satellite. Almost all of them depend on one. Three services carry most of
that dependence:

| Service | What the firm uses it for | Who provides it |
|---|---|---|
| **Positioning, navigation and timing (PNT)** | fleet and container tracking, aviation and shipping, precision agriculture — and above all **time**: telecom networks, power grids and trading venues synchronise their clocks to GNSS signals | four global systems, each run by a state: **GPS** (United States), **Galileo** (European Union), **BeiDou** (China), **GLONASS** (Russia) |
| **Connectivity** | ships, aircraft, mines, rigs and remote sites; back-up links for networks on the ground | geostationary operators, and since 2019 **constellations** of thousands of satellites in low Earth orbit |
| **Earth observation** | crop and commodity forecasts, insurance claims, supply-chain and site monitoring, due diligence | state agencies and a growing number of commercial imaging firms |

Sessions 08 and 10 showed how networks become instruments of power. Today's network is physically
above the firm, and its hubs are a handful of states and, increasingly, a handful of companies. The
scale of the change is recent: ESA's 2026 report notes that the exponential growth in the number of
new payloads continued in 2025, driven by a higher launch frequency, rideshare launches and the
deployment of large constellations by commercial operators (ESA Space Debris Office, 2026).

## 2. The orbital commons: law and physics

**The law.** The **Outer Space Treaty** (1967) is the constitution of the domain, and it was written
for two superpowers, not for a market:

| Article | Rule | What it means for a firm |
|---|---|---|
| **II** | outer space "is not subject to national appropriation" by claim of sovereignty, use or occupation | no one can own an orbit — but whoever occupies it first, in practice, keeps it |
| **IV** | no nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit; the Moon and celestial bodies for peaceful purposes only | **conventional** weapons in orbit and anti-satellite tests are **not** prohibited by the Treaty |
| **VI** | states bear international responsibility for national activities, "whether … carried on by governmental agencies or by non-governmental entities", which require "authorization and continuing supervision" | every commercial satellite has a **state behind it** — its licensing state |
| **VII** | the launching state is liable for damage caused by its objects | debris has an owner, in law, long after it has stopped working |
| **IX** | "due regard" to the interests of others; consultations before potentially harmful interference | the closest the Treaty comes to traffic rules — without an enforcer |

Two further layers matter to business. The **International Telecommunication Union** coordinates
radio frequencies and orbital positions through its Radio Regulations: an operator's state files a
satellite network with the ITU and coordinates it with earlier filings, so priority goes, broadly,
to the first to file — an incentive to file early and large, sometimes for "paper satellites" that
may never fly. And **national licensing** — of launches, spectrum, remote sensing, exports — is where
the Treaty's "authorization and continuing supervision" becomes a permit a firm must hold.

**The physics.** **Kessler and Cour-Palais (1978)** showed that once the density of objects in an
orbital shell passes a threshold, collisions produce fragments that cause further collisions: a
debris belt that grows on its own, even if no one launches again. A commons in the strict sense — no
one can be excluded, and every use depletes it for others — with physics in place of an economy.
Debris in low orbit re-enters as the atmosphere drags it down; the higher the orbit, the longer it
stays — years at a few hundred kilometres, decades to centuries above that.

> **For the firm:** an orbit is a **shared infrastructure** whose quality is set by the least careful
> actor in it. A destructive test by one state raises the collision risk — and the insurance price —
> of every operator in that shell.

## 3. Astropolitics: three readings of the new space race

| | Reading | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Dolman (2002)**, *Astropolitik* | classical geopolitics carried into space: in the spirit of Mackinder, whoever controls low Earth orbit controls access to near-Earth space, and through it the Earth; a realist case for the United States to take and hold that control | space is a **territory to dominate** — Session 03's anarchy, with orbits as chokepoints |
| **Pekkanen (2019)**, "Governing the new space race" | the new space race is driven by three intertwined trends — **democratisation** (more states and non-state actors), **commercialisation** (firms striking out on their own, not only supplying governments), **militarisation** — while about **95% of space technologies are dual-use**, and treaty-making is in decline | space is a **market** governed by national laws, contracts and a few old principles — and the firms in it are part of the security problem |
| **Bowen (2020)**, *War in Space* | spacepower matters for its effects **on Earth**; Earth orbit is a "cosmic coastline" from which states support, and threaten, what happens on land, at sea and in the air | space is **infrastructure for terrestrial power** — and therefore a target when terrestrial power is contested |

The three readings do not exclude one another. Read together they explain why the same
constellation can be a consumer broadband business, a military communications network and a
foreign-policy instrument, all at once — Pekkanen's dual use, with the strategic stakes of Dolman and
Bowen.

## 4. Counterspace: the threats

The Secure World Foundation's annual open-source assessment (Samson & Brett, 2026) tracks what
states can do to deceive, disrupt, deny, degrade or destroy space systems, across five categories,
in thirteen countries:

| Category | What it is | Reversible? | Who it hits first |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct-ascent** | a missile launched from Earth to destroy a satellite | no — and it creates debris | the target, then every operator in that shell |
| **Co-orbital** | a satellite that approaches, inspects, interferes with or attacks another | depends | the target |
| **Electronic warfare** | jamming or spoofing of signals — uplinks, downlinks, GNSS | yes | **users on the ground**: ships, aircraft, networks |
| **Directed energy** | lasers or microwaves to dazzle or damage sensors | partly | imaging satellites |
| **Cyber** | attacks on satellites, ground stations or user terminals | yes, unless hardware is destroyed | **customers** of the operator |

Its central finding for this course: many states have researched and tested destructive
capabilities, but **only non-destructive capabilities are actively used against satellites in
current military operations**. Four states have destroyed a satellite with a missile from the ground:
the United States (1985, and the low-altitude intercept of a failing satellite in 2008), China
(2007), India (2019) and Russia (2021). In April 2022 the United States declared it would not conduct
destructive direct-ascent tests, and the UN General Assembly called on all states to make the same
commitment in December 2022.

What firms have actually suffered is the reversible kind. On **24 February 2022**, the day of the
invasion of Ukraine, a cyberattack on the ground segment of Viasat's KA-SAT network disabled
thousands of user modems in Ukraine and across Europe — including the remote monitoring of thousands
of wind turbines in Germany; the EU, the UK and the US attributed it to Russia. GNSS jamming and
spoofing around conflict zones has become routine; in 2024 Finnair suspended flights to Tartu, in
Estonia, for several weeks because interference made satellite-based approaches unusable.

> **The lesson for an analyst.** Destructive tests make headlines and debris. Jamming, spoofing and
> cyberattacks on ground segments make **losses** — and they fall on the customers, not on the
> satellite.

## 5. The evidence: the debris record

**ESA's Space Debris Office** has published its *Space Environment Report* every year since 2017.
The 2026 edition (Issue 10.0, data to the end of 2025) is today's big work. Its method is worth
reading before its figures: it counts **catalogued** objects — from the US public catalogue, the
Russian Vimpel catalogue and the UK's historical RAE tables — and **asserted** objects known to
exist by design, cross-correlated in ESA's DISCOS database; objects too small or too erratic to be
tracked are outside its scope.

Its headline numbers, at the end of 2025 (Table 3.1):

- **44,964 objects** in Earth orbit, of which **16,946 payloads**; **25,060** in low Earth orbit;
- "ever since the start of the space age there has been more space debris in orbit than operational
  satellites";
- **fragmentations other than collisions** — explosions, mostly of old rocket stages and batteries —
  are the **dominant source** of debris: on average 9.8 non-deliberate fragmentations a year over the
  last two decades; 2024 added over 3,000 catalogued fragments, mostly from propulsion-related
  break-ups.

**The target figure** — Figure 2.1, the number of objects in geocentric orbit by object class since
1957 — shows the cumulative result. Three events stand out, all recorded in the report:

| Date | Event | Altitude | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| **11 January 2007** | China destroys its Fengyun-1C weather satellite with a ground-launched missile | about 850 km | the largest single source of catalogued debris in the public catalogue; thousands of fragments, most still in orbit |
| **10 February 2009** | the active Iridium 33 and the defunct Cosmos 2251 collide | about 780 km | the first accidental collision between two intact satellites — Kessler, observed |
| **15 November 2021** | Russia destroys Cosmos 1408 with a ground-launched missile | about 470–490 km | some 1,800 catalogued fragments; low enough that almost all re-entered within three years — ESA attributes the 2022 spike in re-entering debris to the test |

**What the figure does not measure.** It counts objects, not risk: a 10-cm fragment and a 5-tonne
rocket stage each count once, and objects too small to catalogue — the majority of the fragments
able to disable a satellite — do not appear at all. And it is drawn from catalogues that themselves
improve: when sensors get better, the number of objects rises without anything new happening in
orbit. In the practice round, you will find a third limit — the one hidden in how the public
catalogue **dates** debris.

## 6. One operator: concentration, and weaponised interdependence in orbit

The new space race has a feature the Cold War one did not: most of the active satellites in orbit
belong to **one company**. SpaceX's Starlink, first launched in May 2019, grew in six years into
the majority of the world's operating satellites; you will measure how large a majority in the
practice round, and you will find that concentration of **who launches payloads** has returned to
Cold War levels — with a firm, not a superpower, at the top.

**Starlink in Ukraine** shows what that means. Within days of the 2022 invasion, Starlink terminals
became a backbone of Ukraine's military and civil communications. The company's own decisions about
where the service would work — including, by its owner's account, a refusal to enable it for an
attack near Crimea in 2022 — became questions of war policy, and governments moved to contract for
the service directly.

Read it with **Farrell and Newman (2019)** from Session 08. A constellation is a **hub**: its
operator sees who connects, where (a panopticon), and can switch a user, a region or a country off
(a chokepoint). The two conditions for weaponisation are met when the network is **concentrated**
and when a **state can direct the firm** that runs the hub — and Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty
guarantees that every operator has a licensing state behind it. A firm that depends on one operator
depends on that operator's state as well.

States are responding as you would expect from Session 03: by building their own. The EU's
**IRIS²** secure-connectivity programme (Regulation (EU) 2023/588), China's **Qianfan** and
**Guowang** constellations, and national launch programmes are all, in part, answers to dependence on
one American firm. **Export controls** draw the same line from the other side: spacecraft and their
components sit on the US Munitions List (Category XV of the ITAR) or, for most commercial satellites
since 2014, under the Export Administration Regulations (the "9x515" entries) — so a non-US
manufacturer with a US component needs US permission to sell to some customers.

## 7. What can the firm do?

**Through which channel does space risk reach the P&L?** The course's five channels apply:

| Channel | How space risk arrives | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Market access** | export controls and licensing on space technology; spectrum and landing rights | a satellite or terminal that cannot be sold or switched on in a market |
| **Inputs** | a service from a single operator or a single state's GNSS | connectivity for ships or mines from one constellation |
| **Finance and payments** | trading and payment systems time-stamped from GNSS; insurance capacity | a timing outage; a premium that doubles after a fragmentation event |
| **Assets and people** | ships and aircraft navigating in jammed or spoofed areas | rerouted flights; vessels misreporting their position |
| **Data and operations** | cyberattacks on ground segments and terminals; loss of imagery feeds | the KA-SAT modems; a crop forecast without images |

**What a dashboard should track** — each with a threshold that triggers a named decision:

1. **Dependence** — the share of a critical service (connectivity, imagery, timing) supplied by one
   operator or one state's system; *e.g. above 50% from one operator → contract a second one*.
2. **Interference** — official warnings of GNSS interference (aviation authorities publish them) in
   the regions where the firm's ships, aircraft or sites operate.
3. **Environment** — destructive tests and fragmentation events in the orbital shells of the firm's
   providers; ESA's yearly environment indicators.
4. **Rules** — changes in export-control lists, licensing conditions and ITU filings that affect the
   firm's suppliers or markets.

**Strategies**, each with a cost and a limit:

| Strategy | Example | Cost | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Multi-orbit, multi-operator** | geostationary + low-orbit links from two providers | price, integration | the second operator may share the first one's state |
| **Multi-constellation receivers** | GPS + Galileo (+ others) in the same receiver | small | one jammer can still blind them all locally |
| **Terrestrial back-up** | holdover clocks and fibre-delivered time; cellular and fibre links | capital | not available at sea or in remote sites |
| **Insurance** | in-orbit and third-party liability cover | premium | a thin market; capacity shrinks after large losses |
| **Compliance by design** | export-control classification of every space component bought or sold | staff, delays | rules change with the balance of power |

## 8. The Tableau skill of the day

- **A stock from flows.** Pivot `LAUNCH_DATE` and `DECAY_DATE` into one `Event date` column; a
  calculated field `Change` = +1 for a launch, −1 for a decay; `SUM([Change])` by year with a
  **Running Total** table calculation is the number of objects in orbit.
- **HHI with LOD expressions.** Share of each owner in a year's payloads:
  `{FIXED [Launch year], [OWNER] : COUNT([NORAD_CAT_ID])} / {FIXED [Launch year] : COUNT([NORAD_CAT_ID])}`;
  the sum of squared shares, built so that it adds up correctly over rows, is the HHI.
- **Dashboard action across data sources**: *Dashboard → Actions → Add Action → Filter*, mapping
  `Year` in one source to `Launch year` in the other.
